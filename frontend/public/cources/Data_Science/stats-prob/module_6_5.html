Continuous-Time Systems

Module 6.4 covered discrete-time stochastic processes. This module enters continuous time. The Poisson process — the "completely random" model of events arriving at random instants — has three equivalent characterizations and underlies all of queueing theory, network traffic modeling, and event-stream analytics. Brownian motion arises as the scaling limit of random walks (Donsker's theorem) and is the foundation of stochastic calculus, financial models, and diffusion processes. We close with geometric Brownian motion — the canonical model of stock prices and the basis of Black-Scholes option pricing.

⚡ Kartify — When Time Becomes Continuous
Week 13. The platform infrastructure team has questions Markov chains alone can't answer.

Q1. "Orders arrive throughout the day at random times. We need to provision server capacity for the 99th percentile peak load. How do we model 'random arrivals'?"
Q2. "Fraud incidents occur on average 3.2 times per hour. What's the probability of 5+ fraud events in the next 30 minutes?"
Q3. "Our stock-tracking module needs to simulate price paths under standard finance assumptions. What model do we use?"

These are continuous-time questions. Discrete Markov chains tick at integer time steps; the real world doesn't. Orders don't arrive at minute :00, :01, :02 — they arrive at 14:23:07.412, then 14:23:11.806, then 14:24:02.193. The relevant mathematics is that of Poisson processes (events at random points in time) and Brownian motion (continuous random paths).

The Poisson process is the unique "memoryless" model of event arrivals — the continuous-time analog of independent Bernoulli trials. It has three equivalent characterizations (counts are Poisson; inter-arrivals are exponential; arrivals in disjoint intervals are independent), and once you internalize them, you can answer any question about event-arrival systems.

Brownian motion is the most important continuous-time process in mathematics. It arises naturally as the scaling limit of random walks (Donsker's theorem — a deep CLT for paths). It underlies the Black-Scholes formula, modern portfolio theory, diffusion-based generative models in ML, and physical models of particle motion. Geometric Brownian motion (Brownian motion with drift, exponentiated) is the workhorse model of stock prices.

Module 6.5 doesn't aim to do stochastic calculus from scratch (that's a year's worth of math). It aims to give you the working knowledge: definitions, key properties, simulations, and the conceptual bridge from discrete to continuous time. After this, when you encounter "Poisson process" or "Brownian motion" in a paper, ML system, or finance text, you'll know exactly what's going on.

Running examples. Kartify's order-arrival stream as a Poisson process, fraud-detection event modeling, server-queue capacity sizing, and stock-price simulation via geometric Brownian motion.
// Section 1 · The Poisson Process

The Poisson Process — Three Equivalent Definitions

The Poisson process is so fundamental that it can be defined three ways — and proving they're equivalent is half the theory.

Definition 1 — by counts
$$\{N(t)\}_{t \ge 0} \text{ is a Poisson process with rate } \lambda > 0 \text{ if:}$$
$$(\text{a}) \; N(0) = 0; \quad (\text{b}) \; N(t) - N(s) \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda(t-s)) \;\;\forall s < t;$$
$$(\text{c}) \; N(t_1) - N(t_0), \ldots, N(t_k) - N(t_{k-1}) \text{ independent for disjoint intervals.}$$
Definition 2 — by inter-arrival times
$$\text{Let } T_1, T_2, \ldots \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Exp}(\lambda). \text{ Define arrival times } S_n = T_1 + \ldots + T_n.$$
$$N(t) = \max\{n : S_n \le t\} \text{ is a Poisson process with rate } \lambda.$$
Definition 3 — by infinitesimal axioms
$$(\text{a}) \; P(N(t+h) - N(t) = 1) = \lambda h + o(h)$$
$$(\text{b}) \; P(N(t+h) - N(t) \ge 2) = o(h) \;\;(\text{no simultaneous events})$$
$$(\text{c}) \; \text{Increments in disjoint intervals are independent.}$$
⚡ Why three equivalent definitions matter

Each definition is useful for different things. Counts (Def 1): natural for "how many arrivals in $[s, t]$?". Inter-arrivals (Def 2): natural for simulation (just generate exponentials). Infinitesimal (Def 3): natural for proving theorems and connecting to continuous-time Markov chains. Switching between them is a key skill — most problems are easiest in one view, and the equivalence lets you transfer the answer.

Kartify · order arrivals as a Poisson process
Setup. Orders arrive at average rate $\lambda = 12$ orders/minute during peak.
Q1. P(15 orders in next minute)? $N(1) \sim \text{Poisson}(12)$. $P(N=15) = e^{-12} \cdot 12^{15}/15! \approx 0.0724$.
Q2. What's the distribution of time until the next order? $T \sim \text{Exp}(12)$. $E[T] = 1/12$ min $= 5$ sec. $P(T < 3 \text{ sec}) = 1 - e^{-12 \cdot 0.05} = 0.451$.
Q3. P(zero orders in next 10 seconds)? $N(1/6) \sim \text{Poisson}(2)$. $P(N=0) = e^{-2} \approx 0.135$. So 13.5% of the time there'll be a 10-second gap with no orders.
Capacity planning. For 99th percentile peak: solve $P(N(1) \ge k) = 0.01 \Rightarrow k \approx 22$. Provision servers to handle 22 orders/minute (vs the average of 12) to absorb random surges with 99% confidence.
// Section 2 · Exponential Inter-arrivals

Inter-arrival Times — The Memoryless Property

Memoryless property of exponential
$$P(T > s + t \mid T > s) = P(T > t)$$

If you've already waited $s$ time units for the next event without seeing one, the additional waiting time is still distributed exactly as $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ — same as if you'd just started waiting. This is unique to the exponential distribution among continuous distributions.

Proof of memorylessness
1
$P(T > s + t \mid T > s) = P(T > s + t \cap T > s) / P(T > s) = P(T > s + t) / P(T > s)$.
2
For exponential: $P(T > x) = e^{-\lambda x}$.
3
Therefore $P(T > s + t \mid T > s) = e^{-\lambda(s+t)} / e^{-\lambda s} = e^{-\lambda t} = P(T > t)$. $\blacksquare$
No "due" effect
Past doesn't predict future
If no order has arrived for 30 seconds, you're NOT "due" for one. Next-arrival time is still $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$. The Poisson process has perfect short-term unpredictability.
Hazard rate is constant
$h(t) = \lambda$ for all $t$
Hazard $h(t) = f(t)/[1 - F(t)]$ — the instantaneous rate of failure given survival. For exponential, this is constant $\lambda$. Implies the Poisson process is "completely random" — no aging effects.
When real systems aren't memoryless
Time-dependent hazard → non-Poisson
Hardware failures often have "bathtub" hazard (high early, low middle, high old) — Weibull, not exponential. Calls to a help desk concentrate around business hours — non-homogeneous Poisson with $\lambda(t)$ varying.
Inspection paradox
"My wait time" is longer than the average gap
If you arrive at a random time and wait for the next event, your expected wait is $1/\lambda$ — but so is the time since the last event. Total inter-arrival containing your moment averages $2/\lambda$, double the typical gap. Counterintuitive consequence of the size-biased sampling of intervals.
// Section 3 · Superposition & Thinning

Superposition & Thinning — Combining and Splitting Poisson Streams

Superposition
$$N_1 \sim \text{PP}(\lambda_1), \; N_2 \sim \text{PP}(\lambda_2) \text{ independent} \;\Rightarrow\; N_1 + N_2 \sim \text{PP}(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)$$
Thinning
$$N \sim \text{PP}(\lambda), \text{ each event independently kept w.p. } p \;\Rightarrow\; N_{\text{kept}} \sim \text{PP}(\lambda p), \; N_{\text{discarded}} \sim \text{PP}(\lambda(1-p))$$
$$N_{\text{kept}} \text{ and } N_{\text{discarded}} \text{ are independent.}$$
Kartify · superposition and thinning in production
Superposition example. Mobile orders arrive at $\lambda_M = 8$/min, desktop at $\lambda_D = 4$/min, independently. Total order stream is Poisson with rate $\lambda = 12$/min. Server capacity planning uses the combined rate.
Thinning example. Suppose 3% of orders are flagged as fraud (independently). Total stream is $\text{PP}(12)$/min. Fraud stream is $\text{PP}(12 \cdot 0.03) = \text{PP}(0.36)$/min — about one fraud every 2.8 minutes. Legitimate stream is $\text{PP}(11.64)$/min, INDEPENDENT of the fraud stream.
Independence is the key insight. Even though both streams come from the same parent process, they're statistically independent — so anything you do with one (e.g., trigger fraud-investigation team) does not affect the analysis of the other. This is a remarkable and often counterintuitive property.
Designing experiments. A/B test that randomly assigns 50% of arriving users to treatment? Treatment stream is $\text{PP}(\lambda/2)$, control is $\text{PP}(\lambda/2)$, both independent of each other. The arrival randomness doesn't bias the treatment effect estimation.

// Section 4 · Compound Poisson

Compound Poisson — Random Jumps at Random Times

Compound Poisson process
$$X(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N(t)} Y_i, \quad N(t) \sim \text{PP}(\lambda), \quad Y_i \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} F$$

At each arrival of the underlying Poisson process, a random amount $Y_i$ is added (or subtracted). Models: insurance claims (Poisson arrivals, random claim sizes), revenue from orders (Poisson arrival, random order value), particle accumulations (Poisson hits, random impulse strength).

Mean and variance of compound Poisson
$$E[X(t)] = \lambda t \cdot E[Y], \quad \mathrm{Var}(X(t)) = \lambda t \cdot E[Y^2]$$
Derivation via conditional expectation
1
$E[X(t)] = E[E[X(t) \mid N(t)]] = E[N(t) \cdot E[Y]] = \lambda t \cdot E[Y]$. (Conditioned on $N(t) = n$, $X = \sum_{i=1}^n Y_i$, mean $nE[Y]$.)
2
For variance, law of total variance: $\mathrm{Var}(X) = E[\mathrm{Var}(X \mid N)] + \mathrm{Var}(E[X \mid N])$.
3
$\mathrm{Var}(X \mid N) = N \cdot \mathrm{Var}(Y)$, so $E[\mathrm{Var}(X \mid N)] = \lambda t \cdot \mathrm{Var}(Y)$.
4
$\mathrm{Var}(E[X \mid N]) = \mathrm{Var}(N \cdot E[Y]) = (E[Y])^2 \cdot \lambda t$. (Poisson variance = mean.)
5
Sum: $\lambda t [\mathrm{Var}(Y) + (E[Y])^2] = \lambda t \cdot E[Y^2]$. $\blacksquare$
Kartify · daily revenue as compound Poisson
Setup. Orders arrive at $\lambda = 600$/day. Order value $Y$ has $E[Y] = ₹1{,}450$, $\mathrm{Var}(Y) = ₹^2 850{,}000^2$.
Daily revenue. $X = \sum_i Y_i$. $E[X] = 600 \cdot 1{,}450 = ₹870{,}000$. $\mathrm{Var}(X) = 600 \cdot (850000^2 + 1450^2) = 4.34 \times 10^{14}$. SD $\approx ₹20.8M$. Wait — that's much larger than the mean!
Reality check. SD of order value is $850k$, not $850M$. Recompute: $\mathrm{Var}(Y) = 850^2 = 722{,}500$. Then $\mathrm{Var}(X) = 600 \cdot (722500 + 1450^2) = 600 \cdot 2{,}825{,}000 = 1.7 \times 10^9$. SD $\approx ₹41{,}200$.
Conclusion. Daily revenue $\approx N(₹870k, ₹41.2k)$ approximately (Normal by CLT for large $N$). 95% CI: $[₹789k, ₹951k]$. Useful for variance budgeting and detection of anomalous days (revenue outside CI prompts investigation).
// Section 5 · Random Walks

Simple Random Walk — The Discrete Foundation of Brownian Motion

Simple symmetric random walk
$$S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i, \quad X_i \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Uniform}\{-1, +1\}$$

$E[S_n] = 0$, $\mathrm{Var}(S_n) = n$. Position at time $n$ has mean 0 and SD $\sqrt n$. The famous "$\sqrt n$ scaling" of random walks.

Recurrence in 1D and 2D
Returns to origin a.s.
In 1D and 2D, simple random walks are recurrent — return to the origin infinitely often with probability 1. ("A drunk man will find his way home, but a drunk bird may get lost forever.")
Transience in 3D+
May escape forever
In 3D, the simple random walk is transient — positive probability of never returning. Pólya's theorem (1921). The bird may indeed get lost.
Gambler's ruin
Hitting probabilities for random walk on $\{0, \ldots, N\}$
Start at $k$; absorbing barriers at $0$ and $N$. P(hit $N$ before $0$) $= k/N$ for symmetric walk. For biased walk with $p \ne 0.5$, probability is $(1 - (q/p)^k)/(1 - (q/p)^N)$.
Reflection principle
Counts paths via mirror trick
Tools for computing $P(\max_{k \le n} S_k \ge a)$ — extreme values, ballot problems, hitting times. Ingenious combinatorial argument extending to Brownian motion's running maximum.
// Section 6 · Brownian Motion

Brownian Motion — The Limit of Random Walks

Standard Brownian motion (Wiener process)
$$\{W(t)\}_{t \ge 0} \text{ is standard Brownian motion if:}$$
$$(\text{a}) \; W(0) = 0; \quad (\text{b}) \; W(t) - W(s) \sim N(0, t-s) \text{ for } s < t;$$
$$(\text{c}) \; \text{Independent increments; } \quad (\text{d}) \; t \mapsto W(t) \text{ is continuous a.s.}$$
Donsker's theorem (functional CLT)
$$\text{Rescaled random walk } \frac{S_{\lfloor nt \rfloor}}{\sqrt n} \Rightarrow W(t) \;\;\text{as } n \to \infty$$

Brownian motion is the scaling limit of random walks — the continuous-time, continuous-state analog. Sample paths are continuous everywhere but differentiable nowhere (a beautiful pathological property).

Quadratic variation
$[W, W]_t = t$
Sum of squared increments converges to $t$ (not 0!). This is the foundation of Itô calculus: $(dW)^2 = dt$. Brownian paths "wiggle so much" that squared moves don't vanish in the limit.
Markov + Gaussian
$W$ is Markov in continuous time
Conditional on $W(s)$, future $W(s+t)$ is $N(W(s), t)$ — depends only on current position. The continuous-time analog of Markov chains; foundation of stochastic differential equations.
Scaling self-similarity
$W(ct) \stackrel{d}{=} \sqrt c \cdot W(t)$
Brownian motion is statistically self-similar — zoom in by factor $c$ in time, by $\sqrt c$ in space. Fractal structure. This is the formal version of "$\sqrt n$ scaling" for random walks.
Maximum process
$\max_{s \le t} W(s) \stackrel{d}{=} |W(t)|$
Reflection principle (continuous version): the running max is distributed like the absolute value of the current position. Useful for: barrier-option pricing, ruin probabilities, extreme-value analysis.
// Section 7 · Geometric Brownian Motion

Geometric Brownian Motion — The Stock Price Model

Geometric Brownian motion (GBM)
$$dS_t = \mu S_t \, dt + \sigma S_t \, dW_t$$
$$S_t = S_0 \exp\!\left[(\mu - \sigma^2/2)t + \sigma W_t\right]$$

Stock price $S_t$ has constant percentage drift $\mu$ and constant percentage volatility $\sigma$. The closed-form solution comes from Itô's lemma applied to $\log S_t$. Three key properties:

PropertyFormulaWhy it matters
$E[S_t]$$S_0 e^{\mu t}$Expected price grows at rate $\mu$ — the "drift"
$\log S_t$ distribution$N(\log S_0 + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)t,\; \sigma^2 t)$Log-prices are Normal — log-returns are Normal with constant volatility
$S_t > 0$ a.s.By construction (exponential)Prices can't go negative — fits real markets
⚡ Black-Scholes from GBM

Assuming GBM dynamics, no-arbitrage pricing yields the Black-Scholes formula for European call options: $C = S_0 \Phi(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}\Phi(d_2)$ where $d_1 = [\log(S_0/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T]/(\sigma\sqrt T)$, $d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt T$. The $\sigma^2/2$ correction in the GBM closed form is what becomes the famous "vol drag." 50 years of derivative pricing rests on this single SDE.

Python — Simulating Continuous-Time Processes

python · simulating a Poisson process two ways
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

np.random.seed(42)
lambda_rate = 12   # orders per minute
T = 10             # 10-minute window

# ─── Method 1: simulate inter-arrival times ───
inter_arrivals = []
t = 0
while t < T:
    delta = np.random.exponential(1 / lambda_rate)
    t += delta
    if t < T:
        inter_arrivals.append(t)
arrivals_method1 = np.array(inter_arrivals)
print(f"Method 1 (inter-arrivals): {len(arrivals_method1)} arrivals in {T} min")

# ─── Method 2: simulate count, then place uniformly ───
N = np.random.poisson(lambda_rate * T)
arrivals_method2 = np.sort(np.random.uniform(0, T, N))
print(f"Method 2 (count + uniform): {N} arrivals")
# Both methods produce statistically identical samples — Poisson process property.

# Verify empirically: counts in disjoint intervals are independent Poisson
n_sims = 5000
counts_first_half = []
counts_second_half = []
for _ in range(n_sims):
    n_total = np.random.poisson(lambda_rate * T)
    arrivals = np.sort(np.random.uniform(0, T, n_total))
    counts_first_half.append((arrivals < T/2).sum())
    counts_second_half.append((arrivals >= T/2).sum())

print(f"\nEmpirical mean counts: first half = {np.mean(counts_first_half):.2f}, second = {np.mean(counts_second_half):.2f}")
print(f"Theory: each = {lambda_rate * T / 2}")
print(f"Empirical correlation: {np.corrcoef(counts_first_half, counts_second_half)[0,1]:.4f} (should be ~0)")
python · Brownian motion and geometric Brownian motion simulation
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

np.random.seed(42)

# ─── Standard Brownian motion ───
T = 1.0           # 1 year
n_steps = 252      # trading days
n_paths = 10
dt = T / n_steps

# Generate increments: each ~ N(0, dt)
increments = np.random.normal(0, np.sqrt(dt), (n_paths, n_steps))
W = np.cumsum(increments, axis=1)
W = np.column_stack([np.zeros(n_paths), W])  # Prepend W(0) = 0

t_grid = np.linspace(0, T, n_steps + 1)

# Verify: W(t) should be approximately N(0, t)
print(f"W(T) empirical mean: {W[:, -1].mean():.3f} (theory: 0)")
print(f"W(T) empirical SD: {W[:, -1].std(ddof=1):.3f} (theory: {np.sqrt(T):.3f})")

# ─── Geometric Brownian motion ───
S0 = 2500           # initial price
mu = 0.12           # annual drift
sigma = 0.30        # annual volatility

n_paths_gbm = 10000
W_gbm = np.cumsum(np.random.normal(0, np.sqrt(dt), (n_paths_gbm, n_steps)), axis=1)
W_gbm = np.column_stack([np.zeros(n_paths_gbm), W_gbm])
S = S0 * np.exp((mu - 0.5 * sigma**2) * t_grid + sigma * W_gbm)

# Distribution at end: log-normal
S_final = S[:, -1]
print(f"\nS(T=1) statistics:")
print(f"  Mean: ₹{S_final.mean():,.0f} (theory: ₹{S0 * np.exp(mu):,.0f})")
print(f"  Median: ₹{np.median(S_final):,.0f} (theory: ₹{S0 * np.exp(mu - 0.5*sigma**2):,.0f})")
print(f"  5th, 95th %ile: ₹{np.percentile(S_final, 5):,.0f}, ₹{np.percentile(S_final, 95):,.0f}")

# Volatility drag visible: median < mean by exp(-σ²/2 · T) factor
python · M/M/1 queue simulation (Poisson arrivals + exponential service)
import numpy as np
import heapq

np.random.seed(42)

# M/M/1 queue: Poisson arrivals at rate lambda, exp service at rate mu
lam = 8             # arrivals per minute
mu = 10            # service rate (must be > lam for stability)
T_max = 100        # simulate for 100 minutes

# Theoretical results for M/M/1 with rho = lam / mu < 1:
rho = lam / mu
L_theory = rho / (1 - rho)             # avg number in system
W_theory = 1 / (mu - lam)              # avg time in system
print(f"Utilization rho = {rho}")
print(f"Theory: avg # in system = {L_theory:.2f}, avg time in system = {W_theory*60:.1f} sec")

# Discrete-event simulation
events = []  # priority queue: (time, type) where type = "arrival" or "departure"
heapq.heappush(events, (np.random.exponential(1/lam), "arrival"))

queue_size = 0
queue_history = []  # (time, queue_size) for averaging
wait_times = []
arrival_times_in_queue = []
last_event_time = 0

while events:
    t, event_type = heapq.heappop(events)
    if t > T_max: break

    queue_history.append((t, queue_size))

    if event_type == "arrival":
        queue_size += 1
        arrival_times_in_queue.append(t)
        # If first in queue, schedule its departure
        if queue_size == 1:
            heapq.heappush(events, (t + np.random.exponential(1/mu), "departure"))
        # Schedule next arrival
        heapq.heappush(events, (t + np.random.exponential(1/lam), "arrival"))
    else:  # departure
        arrival_t = arrival_times_in_queue.pop(0)
        wait_times.append(t - arrival_t)
        queue_size -= 1
        if queue_size > 0:
            heapq.heappush(events, (t + np.random.exponential(1/mu), "departure"))

# Time-averaged queue length
times = np.array([h[0] for h in queue_history])
sizes = np.array([h[1] for h in queue_history])
durations = np.diff(np.append(times, T_max))
L_empirical = (sizes * durations).sum() / T_max
W_empirical = np.mean(wait_times)

print(f"\nEmpirical: avg # in system = {L_empirical:.2f}, avg time in system = {W_empirical*60:.1f} sec")
# Should match theory closely. Little's Law: L = lambda * W

BContinuous everywhere but differentiable nowhere a.s. Beautiful pathological property — the path "wiggles" infinitely much in every interval. This is why $(dW)^2 = dt$ in Itô calculus (quadratic variation $= t$, not 0). Foundation of stochastic differential equations and stochastic finance.
CDiscrete jumps
DLinear in time
Q 07 / 10
Donsker's theorem says rescaled random walks converge to Brownian motion. What's the analogy?
AIt's a special case of bootstrap
BFunctional CLT — the path-space generalization of the central limit theorem. CLT says $\sqrt n(\bar X - \mu) \to N(0, \sigma^2)$ — a single rescaled value. Donsker says the entire rescaled trajectory $S_{\lfloor nt \rfloor}/\sqrt n$ converges to a Brownian path. This is why Brownian motion shows up everywhere: it's the universal scaling limit.
CDonsker is unrelated to CLT
DOnly applies to symmetric walks
Q 08 / 10
GBM closed form: $S_t = S_0 \exp[(\mu - \sigma^2/2)t + \sigma W_t]$. Why does $\sigma^2/2$ appear?
AConvention
BVolatility drag from Itô's lemma. When you exponentiate Brownian motion, the convexity of $e^x$ adds positive variance. To keep $E[S_t] = S_0 e^{\mu t}$, the drift in the exponent must be reduced by $\sigma^2/2$. This is THE famous "volatility drag" of finance — why high-vol assets underperform their average return on a compounded basis.
CMistake in derivation
DTo make formulas symmetric
Q 09 / 10
Inspection paradox in Poisson processes. Why is the inter-arrival containing your moment of arrival LONGER than typical?
AIt isn't — they're the same
BSize-biased sampling. Long intervals are more likely to "contain" your random arrival moment than short ones — bigger fish caught more often in fish-by-net analogy. For Poisson with rate $\lambda$, expected interval containing your moment is $2/\lambda$ (sum of past-elapsed and remaining-wait, each $\sim$ Exp($\lambda$) by memorylessness).
CNumerical bias in simulations
DOnly happens in non-Poisson
Q 10 / 10
M/M/1 queue with arrival rate $\lambda = 8$ and service rate $\mu = 10$. Stability condition?
A$\lambda \ge \mu$
B$\lambda < \mu$ (utilization $\rho = \lambda/\mu < 1$). Otherwise queue grows without bound. With $\rho < 1$: avg # in system $= \rho/(1 - \rho)$, avg time in system $= 1/(\mu - \lambda)$. As $\rho \to 1^-$, both blow up — the famous "queue explosion" near full utilization that drives capacity-planning decisions.
C$\lambda = \mu$
D$\lambda + \mu < 1$
