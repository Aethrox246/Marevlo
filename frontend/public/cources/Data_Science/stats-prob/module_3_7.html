Sampling Distributions
& Standard Error

The cornerstone module of inference. Every statistic $\bar{X}$, $s^2$, $\hat\theta$ is itself a random variable; its distribution across repeated sampling is what makes confidence intervals and hypothesis tests possible. Standard error, the $\chi^2$ distribution of sample variance, and Student's $t$ for the mean when $\sigma$ is unknown — all live here.

⚡ Kartify — The Gap Between "Sample Mean" and "Truth"
Friday week 3. Priya's revenue slide has one figure: ₹47.3 lakh ± 1.28. The ± 1.28 part is everything. Without it: "here's a number." With it: "here's a range in which the truth likely lies."

Where does 1.28 come from? It's $1.96 \times s / \sqrt{n}$. The $s / \sqrt{n}$ part is the standard error — the standard deviation of the sample mean across hypothetical repeated samples. The $1.96$ is the Normal 97.5% quantile. But wait — we used $s$ (sample std), not $\sigma$ (population std, unknown). That substitution costs us: the sampling distribution is no longer exactly Normal; it's Student's $t$, and for $n = 90$ the correction is small but for $n = 12$ it's enormous.

Module 3.7 is where statistics goes from describing data to making probabilistic claims about reality from data. The key mental shift: every statistic is a random variable, with its own distribution. Understanding that distribution (the "sampling distribution") is what unlocks confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, and the entire machinery of Tracks 4 and 5.
// Section 1

The Sampling Distribution — A Statistic is a Random Variable

Sampling distribution
$$\text{For a statistic } T = T(X_1, \ldots, X_n), \text{ the distribution of } T \text{ across all possible samples of size } n \text{ is the } \textbf{sampling distribution of } T.$$
The most important idea in statistics, stated simply
You collected one sample and computed $\bar{X} = 47.3$. Imagine collecting 1,000 independent samples of size $n = 90$ from the same population — each yielding a different $\bar{X}$. Those 1,000 values form a distribution. That distribution is the sampling distribution of $\bar{X}$. Understanding it tells you how much your one observed value might have differed from the truth.

Sampling Distribution of $\bar X$ — The Exact Result for Normal Data

Sampling distribution of the sample mean · Normal parent
$$X_i \stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} N(\mu, \sigma^2) \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \bar X \sim N\!\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)$$
Why the result is exact for Normal parent
1
$\bar X = \frac{1}{n} \sum X_i$ is a linear combination of Normals.
2
By Normal closure (Module 3.4): linear combinations of independent Normals are Normal.
3
Mean: $E[\bar X] = \mu$ (always, from linearity). Variance: $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X) = \sigma^2/n$ (from independence).
4
Therefore $\bar X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$. Exact, not approximate. $\blacksquare$
Standard Error — the standard deviation of the sampling distribution
$$\mathrm{SE}(\bar X) = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$
◆ For non-Normal data, the result is approximate — CLT provides the asymptotic bridge

If data is not Normal: $\bar X$ is not exactly Normal, but by the Central Limit Theorem (Module 3.8), $\bar X$ is approximately Normal for large $n$. How large? Rule of thumb: $n \ge 30$ for moderately skewed data. Heavy-tailed data needs more ($n \ge 100$). The machinery of sampling distributions relies on exactly this asymptotic normality.

Known vs Unknown $\sigma$ — The Bedrock Distinction

SituationStatisticSampling distributionImplication
$\sigma$ known$Z = \dfrac{\bar X - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}$$N(0, 1)$ exactly (for Normal parent)Use Normal quantiles (1.96 for 95% CI)
$\sigma$ unknown, estimated by $s$$T = \dfrac{\bar X - \mu}{s/\sqrt{n}}$Student's $t_{n-1}$Use $t$-quantiles; approach Normal as $n \to \infty$
⚠ The subtle but crucial difference

If $\sigma$ were known, we could use it directly: $Z$ is exactly standard Normal. But we don't know $\sigma$ — we estimate it with $s$ from the same sample. That estimator $s$ has its own variability. Substituting $s$ for $\sigma$ means the denominator is also random, which inflates the tails. The $t_{n-1}$ distribution has fatter tails than Normal, exactly right to account for this extra uncertainty.

// Section 4

Student's $t$-Distribution — The Workhorse of Small-Sample Inference

Student's $t_\nu$ — definition by construction
$$T = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{V / \nu}}, \quad Z \sim N(0, 1), \quad V \sim \chi^2_\nu, \quad Z \perp V$$
Shape
Symmetric bell, heavier tails than Normal
For $\nu = 1$: Cauchy (very heavy tails, no mean). $\nu = 2$: heavy-tailed. $\nu \to \infty$: exactly Normal.
Degrees of freedom
$\nu = n - 1$ for sample mean
One degree of freedom is consumed estimating $\bar X$. Larger $n$ gives more df, tighter tails, closer to Normal.
Why heavier tails
$s$ has its own variability
The denominator $s/\sqrt{n}$ is random — not fixed $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. Small $s$ gives extreme $T$ values, fattening the tails. Heavy-tailed distribution of the ratio.
Practical rule
$t \approx$ Normal for $n > 30$
For Kartify's $n = 90$, $t_{89}$ is essentially Normal (97.5% quantile: 1.987 vs Normal's 1.960). For $n = 12$, $t_{11}$ 97.5% quantile is 2.201 — 12% wider than Normal.

Sampling Distribution of $s^2$ — $\chi^2_{n-1}$ for Normal Data

Sampling distribution of sample variance · Normal parent
$$X_i \stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} N(\mu, \sigma^2) \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \frac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$$
⚡ Why $\chi^2$? The geometry

$\chi^2_\nu$ is the distribution of the sum of $\nu$ independent squared standard Normals. $(n-1)s^2 / \sigma^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X)^2 / \sigma^2$ is nearly this — summing $n$ squared standardized residuals. The "$-1$" comes from the single constraint $\sum (X_i - \bar X) = 0$. Every squared-Normal-sum statistic carries $\chi^2$ behavior in its bones.

Kartify · confidence interval for population variance
Setup. $n = 90$ days of revenue, $s^2 = 38.4$ (i.e., $s = 6.2$ lakh).
Critical values. $\chi^2_{89, 0.025} = 65.6$, $\chi^2_{89, 0.975} = 116.3$.
95% CI for $\sigma^2$. $\left[\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{89,0.975}}, \frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{89,0.025}}\right] = \left[\frac{89 \cdot 38.4}{116.3}, \frac{89 \cdot 38.4}{65.6}\right] = [29.4, 52.1]$.
95% CI for $\sigma$. $\sqrt{[29.4, 52.1]} = [5.42, 7.22]$ lakh.
Interpretation. True daily revenue std is somewhere between ₹5.4 lakh and ₹7.2 lakh with 95% confidence. Asymmetric CI — the $\chi^2$ distribution is right-skewed, so upper bound is further from point estimate.
// Section 6

Confidence Interval Construction — Putting It All Together

$100(1-\alpha)\%$ CI for $\mu$ with unknown $\sigma$
$$\bar X \pm t_{n-1, \alpha/2} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$
Kartify · the CFO's revenue CI, done correctly
Given. $n = 90$, $\bar x = 47.3$ lakh, $s = 6.2$ lakh.
Standard error. $s/\sqrt{n} = 6.2/\sqrt{90} \approx 0.653$ lakh.
Critical value. $t_{89, 0.025} \approx 1.987$ (vs Normal's 1.960). Difference: 1.4%.
Margin of error. $1.987 \times 0.653 \approx 1.298$ lakh (vs 1.281 with Normal).
95% CI. $[47.3 - 1.30, 47.3 + 1.30] = [46.00, 48.60]$ lakh.
CFO answer. "We are 95% confident the true daily mean lies between ₹46.0 and ₹48.6 lakh." Both ₹45 and ₹50 sit outside this range — neither is consistent with the observed data at the 95% level.
What does "95% confident" actually mean?
It does NOT mean "there's a 95% probability $\mu \in [46, 48.6]$." $\mu$ is not random — it's a fixed unknown. The CI is random; it's different every sample. The 95% is a statement about the procedure: if we repeated the experiment many times, 95% of constructed intervals would contain the true $\mu$. This procedural interpretation is the frequentist foundation. Bayesian credible intervals do allow "probability $\mu \in [a, b] = 0.95$" statements — but only under a prior (Track 4).

Python — Sampling Distributions from Scratch

python · simulating the sampling distribution of $\bar X$
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(42)
mu_true, sigma_true = 47.3, 6.2    # pretend the true Kartify params
n_sample = 90
n_repetitions = 10_000

xbars = []
for _ in range(n_repetitions):
    sample = np.random.normal(mu_true, sigma_true, n_sample)
    xbars.append(sample.mean())
xbars = np.array(xbars)

print(f"Empirical mean of X̄:     {xbars.mean():.4f}  (expected {mu_true})")
print(f"Empirical std of X̄ (SE): {xbars.std(ddof=1):.4f}  (expected σ/√n = {sigma_true / np.sqrt(n_sample):.4f})")

# Histogram should match N(μ, σ²/n)
from scipy.stats import shapiro
stat, p = shapiro(xbars[:5000])    # test normality of the sampling distribution
print(f"Shapiro–Wilk p-value for X̄: {p:.3f}  (exact Normal for Normal parent data)")
python · $t$ vs Normal — the small-sample correction
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import t, norm

# Small-sample CI for Kartify weekly team metric (n = 12)
np.random.seed(42)
sample = np.random.normal(47.3, 6.2, 12)
xbar = sample.mean()
s = sample.std(ddof=1)
se = s / np.sqrt(12)

# t-based CI (correct)
t_crit = t.ppf(0.975, df=11)
ci_t = [xbar - t_crit*se, xbar + t_crit*se]

# Normal-based CI (wrong for small n)
z_crit = norm.ppf(0.975)
ci_z = [xbar - z_crit*se, xbar + z_crit*se]

print(f"t_{{11, 0.025}} = {t_crit:.3f}  vs  Normal 1.960 (+{(t_crit-z_crit)/z_crit*100:.1f}% wider)")
print(f"Correct t-CI: [{ci_t[0]:.2f}, {ci_t[1]:.2f}]")
print(f"Wrong Z-CI:   [{ci_z[0]:.2f}, {ci_z[1]:.2f}]")
# The Z-CI is optimistically narrow — pretends we knew σ when we didn't.
python · confidence interval for variance via $\chi^2$
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import chi2

n = 90
s_squared = 38.44                           # (s = 6.2)²
alpha = 0.05

# χ² critical values
chi2_lower = chi2.ppf(alpha/2, df=n - 1)         # 2.5% tail
chi2_upper = chi2.ppf(1 - alpha/2, df=n - 1)     # 97.5%

# CI for σ² (inversion)
sigma2_low  = (n - 1) * s_squared / chi2_upper
sigma2_high = (n - 1) * s_squared / chi2_lower
print(f"95% CI for σ²: [{sigma2_low:.2f}, {sigma2_high:.2f}]")
print(f"95% CI for σ:  [{np.sqrt(sigma2_low):.2f}, {np.sqrt(sigma2_high):.2f}]")

# Notice: asymmetric. χ² is right-skewed, so upper bound is further from s.

Q 06 / 08
What does "95% confidence interval" formally mean?
AThere is 95% probability that $\mu$ lies in this specific CI
BThe procedure that generated this CI covers the true $\mu$ 95% of the time across repeated experiments. The parameter $\mu$ is fixed; the CI is random. Never interpret a specific CI as having "probability 0.95 of containing $\mu$" — that's Bayesian credible interval territory.
C95% of future data will fall in this range
D95% of samples have $\bar X$ in this range
Q 07 / 08
Quadrupling Kartify's sample size $n$ from 90 to 360 changes SE by what factor?
AStays the same
BSE scales as $1/\sqrt n$, so 4× more data halves SE. To halve a CI width, need 4× data. To reduce SE by factor 10, need 100× data. Quadratic cost in precision — often the binding constraint on experimental design.
CReduced by factor 4
DReduced by factor 16

BThe $\chi^2$ distribution (sampling distribution of $(n-1)s^2/\sigma^2$) is right-skewed. Inversion gives an asymmetric CI. Point estimate 38.4 is closer to the lower bound (29.4) than to the upper (52.1).
