Track 6 · Advanced Topics

MCMC —
Sampling from Complex Posteriors

Conjugate priors give closed-form posteriors. But real models — hierarchical regressions, mixture models, neural nets with priors — almost never have conjugate structure. The posterior is known only up to a normalization constant: $p(\theta \mid \mathcal D) \propto p(\mathcal D \mid \theta) p(\theta)$. MCMC's trick: build a Markov chain whose stationary distribution IS the posterior. Sample from the chain; throw away the burn-in; treat the remaining draws as posterior samples. Metropolis-Hastings, Gibbs, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, and NUTS — the engines of Stan, PyMC, NumPyro.

⚡ Kartify — When Conjugacy Fails
Week 11. Module 6.1's elegant Beta-Binomial / Normal-Normal / Gamma-Poisson updates handled simple cases beautifully. Now Priya's model has gotten complicated.

The new question. "Estimate conversion rates for each of our 47 product categories, but borrow strength across them — categories with little data should shrink toward the global mean." This is a hierarchical model: each category's $\theta_j$ is drawn from a higher-level distribution $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ with hyperparameters $\alpha, \beta$ themselves estimated from data. The joint posterior $p(\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_{47}, \alpha, \beta \mid \mathcal D)$ has 49 parameters and is not conjugate. No closed-form solution exists.

The bad option. Numerical integration. For 49 dimensions, the curse of dimensionality bites hard: even with 10 grid points per dimension, you'd need $10^{49}$ evaluations — more than the number of atoms in a thousand observable universes. Quadrature is dead in moderate dimensions; rejection sampling is dead by 5–10 dimensions; importance sampling fails when the proposal doesn't match the target.

The MCMC insight. You don't need to evaluate the posterior. You need to sample from it. And there's a beautiful trick: construct a Markov chain on parameter space whose stationary distribution is the posterior. Run the chain. After it has "forgotten" its starting point (burn-in), the remaining samples are draws from the posterior — usable for any expectation, quantile, or interval you want.

Even better: you only need the posterior up to a constant. The normalization $p(\mathcal D)$ — the impossible integral — never appears in the algorithm. MCMC works on $p(\mathcal D \mid \theta) p(\theta)$, the unnormalized product.

The progression. Metropolis-Hastings (1953/1970) — simplest and most general. Gibbs sampling (1984) — exploits conditional structure when you can sample each parameter conditional on the others. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (Duane et al. 1987) — uses gradient information to take much larger steps. NUTS (Hoffman & Gelman 2014) — auto-tuning HMC; the modern default in Stan and PyMC.

Module 6.2 derives each algorithm from first principles, proves convergence via detailed balance, and shows the diagnostics ($\hat R$, ESS, trace plots) that tell you when the chain has converged. After this, you can read any modern Bayesian paper and implement any custom sampler.

Running examples. A hand-coded Metropolis sampler on the Beta-Binomial (where we know the truth, so we can verify); Gibbs sampling for Bayesian linear regression; HMC's geometric intuition for high-dimensional posteriors; PyMC for the hierarchical pricing model.
// Section 1 · Why MCMC

The Curse of Dimensionality — Why Direct Methods Fail

Direct integration / quadrature
Dies at $d \approx 5$
$d$-dimensional grid with $k$ points per axis: $k^d$ evaluations. $k = 10$, $d = 10$: 10 billion calls. $d = 20$: $10^{20}$. Quadrature is competitive only for $d \le 4$.
Rejection sampling
Acceptance $\to 0$ exponentially in $d$
Need an envelope $Mq(\theta) \ge p(\theta)$. Acceptance rate $\propto 1/M$. In high dim, even good envelopes have huge $M$ — acceptance vanishes.
Importance sampling
Variance explodes in $d$
Importance weights $w_i = p(\theta_i)/q(\theta_i)$ have huge variance when $q$ poorly matches $p$ — and matching is hard in high dimensions.
MCMC
The only general option
Builds a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is $p(\theta)$. Doesn't need normalization, doesn't need an envelope, scales to thousands of dimensions. The workhorse of modern Bayesian computation.
The MCMC promise — and its caveat
Promise. Given the unnormalized target $\tilde p(\theta) = p(\mathcal D \mid \theta) p(\theta)$, MCMC produces samples $\theta^{(1)}, \theta^{(2)}, \ldots$ whose empirical distribution converges to the true posterior $p(\theta \mid \mathcal D)$ as the chain runs. Sample averages converge to posterior expectations.

Caveat. Samples are not iid. They are autocorrelated — successive draws are similar. This means $S$ MCMC samples carry less information than $S$ iid samples; the effective sample size (ESS) measures how many "effective iid samples" your chain has produced. Diagnostics ($\hat R$, ESS, trace plots) tell you whether the chain has converged and how many useful samples you have.
// Section 2 · Markov Chain Fundamentals

Markov Chain Fundamentals — Detailed Balance and Stationarity

Markov chain on $\Theta$
$$P(\theta^{(t+1)} \mid \theta^{(t)}, \theta^{(t-1)}, \ldots) = P(\theta^{(t+1)} \mid \theta^{(t)}) = K(\theta^{(t)}, \theta^{(t+1)})$$

The transition kernel $K(\theta, \theta')$ is the conditional density of moving to $\theta'$ given current state $\theta$. The Markov property says: future depends only on present, not on past.

Stationary distribution
$$\pi \text{ is stationary if } \pi(\theta') = \int \pi(\theta) K(\theta, \theta') d\theta$$

If we sample $\theta^{(t)} \sim \pi$ and apply the kernel, the resulting $\theta^{(t+1)}$ is also distributed as $\pi$. The chain is "in equilibrium."

Detailed balance — the easy way to verify stationarity
$$\pi(\theta) K(\theta, \theta') = \pi(\theta') K(\theta', \theta) \;\;\;\forall\, \theta, \theta'$$
Why detailed balance implies stationarity
1
Suppose $\pi(\theta) K(\theta, \theta') = \pi(\theta') K(\theta', \theta)$ holds for all $\theta, \theta'$.
2
Integrate both sides over $\theta$: $\int \pi(\theta) K(\theta, \theta') d\theta = \int \pi(\theta') K(\theta', \theta) d\theta = \pi(\theta') \int K(\theta', \theta) d\theta = \pi(\theta')$ (since the kernel integrates to 1).
3
So $\int \pi(\theta) K(\theta, \theta') d\theta = \pi(\theta')$ — exactly the stationarity condition. $\blacksquare$
4
Detailed balance is a sufficient (not necessary) condition for stationarity. It's the "physical" intuition: in equilibrium, the rate of mass flowing from $\theta$ to $\theta'$ equals the rate flowing back.
Irreducibility
Chain can reach any state
For any two states $\theta, \theta'$ with positive $\pi$-mass, there's positive probability of going $\theta \to \theta'$ in some finite number of steps.
Aperiodicity
No deterministic cycles
Chain doesn't return to starting state only in multiples of some period $> 1$. Continuous-state MCMC is essentially always aperiodic.
Ergodic theorem
Sample averages → expectations
Irreducible + aperiodic + stationary $\pi$ exists $\Rightarrow$ $\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T f(\theta^{(t)}) \to E_\pi[f(\theta)]$ as $T \to \infty$, regardless of starting state.
Convergence rate
Geometric ergodicity (the good case)
$\|\pi^{(t)} - \pi\|_{\text{TV}} \le C \rho^t$ for some $\rho < 1$. Most well-designed MCMC algorithms on regular targets achieve this. Without geometric ergodicity, convergence can be arbitrarily slow.
// Section 3 · Metropolis-Hastings

Metropolis-Hastings — The Universal MCMC Algorithm

Metropolis-Hastings algorithm
$$\text{Given } \theta^{(t)}: \;\; \theta^* \sim q(\theta^* \mid \theta^{(t)}), \;\; \alpha = \min\!\left(1, \frac{\tilde p(\theta^*) q(\theta^{(t)} \mid \theta^*)}{\tilde p(\theta^{(t)}) q(\theta^* \mid \theta^{(t)})}\right)$$
$$\theta^{(t+1)} = \begin{cases} \theta^* & \text{w.p. } \alpha \\ \theta^{(t)} & \text{w.p. } 1 - \alpha \end{cases}$$
Proof that MH satisfies detailed balance with $\pi(\theta) \propto \tilde p(\theta)$
1
The MH transition kernel: $K(\theta, \theta') = q(\theta' \mid \theta) \alpha(\theta, \theta')$ for $\theta' \neq \theta$, plus a point mass at $\theta$ with weight $1 - \int q(\theta' \mid \theta) \alpha(\theta, \theta') d\theta'$ (the rejection probability).
2
For $\theta \neq \theta'$, want to show $\pi(\theta) q(\theta' \mid \theta) \alpha(\theta, \theta') = \pi(\theta') q(\theta \mid \theta') \alpha(\theta', \theta)$.
3
WLOG suppose $\pi(\theta') q(\theta \mid \theta') \le \pi(\theta) q(\theta' \mid \theta)$ (so the MH ratio when going $\theta \to \theta'$ is $\le 1$, meaning $\alpha(\theta, \theta') = \pi(\theta') q(\theta \mid \theta') / [\pi(\theta) q(\theta' \mid \theta)]$). Then $\alpha(\theta', \theta) = 1$ (the reverse direction is "uphill" so always accepted).
4
LHS: $\pi(\theta) q(\theta' \mid \theta) \cdot [\pi(\theta') q(\theta \mid \theta') / (\pi(\theta) q(\theta' \mid \theta))] = \pi(\theta') q(\theta \mid \theta')$.
5
RHS: $\pi(\theta') q(\theta \mid \theta') \cdot 1 = \pi(\theta') q(\theta \mid \theta')$. LHS = RHS. $\blacksquare$
6
Critical observation: only $\tilde p$ (unnormalized) appears in the ratio. The normalization $p(\mathcal D)$ cancels — this is what makes MCMC tractable for posteriors.

Random-walk Metropolis — the simplest practical version

Choose $q(\theta^* \mid \theta) = N(\theta^*; \theta, \Sigma)$ — a symmetric Gaussian proposal. Then $q(\theta^* \mid \theta) = q(\theta \mid \theta^*)$ (symmetry), and the MH ratio simplifies:

Random-walk Metropolis acceptance ratio
$$\alpha = \min\!\left(1, \frac{\tilde p(\theta^*)}{\tilde p(\theta^{(t)})}\right)$$
Step size $\Sigma$
The single most important tuning parameter
Too small: high acceptance but tiny moves — chain crawls, high autocorrelation. Too large: most proposals rejected — chain stuck. Optimal acceptance rate (Roberts, Gelman, Gilks 1997): ~0.234 for high-dim, ~0.44 for 1-D.
Burn-in
Discard initial samples
Chain starts at an arbitrary $\theta^{(0)}$, far from typical posterior. Discard the first $T_{\text{burn}}$ samples (typically 1000–10000) to let the chain reach stationarity.
Thinning
Keep every $k$-th sample
Reduces autocorrelation and storage. Mostly cosmetic now (modern advice: don't thin unless storage is a hard constraint — every sample carries some info).
Multiple chains
Always run 4 in parallel
Start from over-dispersed initial points. If all chains converge to the same distribution → trustworthy. If they disagree → not converged. Modern $\hat R$ diagnostic compares within-chain and between-chain variance.

// Section 4 · Gibbs Sampling

Gibbs Sampling — Conditional Conjugacy Exploitation

When $\theta = (\theta_1, \theta_2, \ldots, \theta_p)$ is multidimensional and we can sample from each full conditional $p(\theta_j \mid \theta_{-j}, \mathcal D)$, Gibbs sampling becomes available — a special case of MH with acceptance probability always 1.

Gibbs sampler (one sweep)
$$\theta_1^{(t+1)} \sim p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2^{(t)}, \theta_3^{(t)}, \ldots, \theta_p^{(t)}, \mathcal D)$$
$$\theta_2^{(t+1)} \sim p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1^{(t+1)}, \theta_3^{(t)}, \ldots, \theta_p^{(t)}, \mathcal D)$$
$$\vdots \;\;\;\; \theta_p^{(t+1)} \sim p(\theta_p \mid \theta_1^{(t+1)}, \theta_2^{(t+1)}, \ldots, \theta_{p-1}^{(t+1)}, \mathcal D)$$
When applicable
Conditional conjugacy
Each $p(\theta_j \mid \theta_{-j}, \mathcal D)$ has a known closed-form distribution we can sample from. Common in hierarchical models with conjugate priors at each level (e.g., Bayesian linear regression with Normal-Inverse-Gamma).
Always accepts
Special case of MH with $\alpha = 1$
Proposal IS the conditional posterior, so MH ratio simplifies to 1. No tuning of step size — but mixing depends on parameter correlation.
Good for low correlation
Per-coordinate updates
Updates one coordinate at a time. Mixes well when posterior coordinates are roughly independent. Mixes poorly when strongly correlated — chain has to "zigzag" along the correlation direction.
Block Gibbs
Group correlated parameters
Sample blocks of correlated parameters jointly from their joint conditional. Reduces zigzagging at the cost of harder per-step calculations. The standard fix when vanilla Gibbs mixes badly.
Kartify · Gibbs for Bayesian linear regression
Model. $\mathbf y \sim N(\mathbf X \boldsymbol\beta, \sigma^2 \mathbf I)$, conjugate priors $\boldsymbol\beta \sim N(\mathbf 0, \tau^2 \mathbf I)$, $\sigma^2 \sim \text{Inv-Gamma}(a, b)$.
Joint posterior is non-conjugate — but the conditionals are.
Conditional 1. $\boldsymbol\beta \mid \sigma^2, \mathbf y \sim N\!\left((\mathbf X^T\mathbf X / \sigma^2 + \mathbf I/\tau^2)^{-1} \mathbf X^T \mathbf y / \sigma^2,\; (\mathbf X^T\mathbf X/\sigma^2 + \mathbf I/\tau^2)^{-1}\right)$ — multivariate Normal.
Conditional 2. $\sigma^2 \mid \boldsymbol\beta, \mathbf y \sim \text{Inv-Gamma}\!\left(a + n/2,\; b + \tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf y - \mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta\|^2\right)$ — inverse-Gamma.
Gibbs sweep. Alternate sampling $\boldsymbol\beta$ given $\sigma^2$, then $\sigma^2$ given $\boldsymbol\beta$. Iterate; collect samples after burn-in.
Outcome. Posterior samples for both regression coefficients and noise variance, all jointly. Modern packages (PyMC, brms) automate this.
// Section 5 · Diagnostics

Convergence Diagnostics — Has the Chain Converged?

An MCMC chain that hasn't converged gives wrong answers. Three core diagnostics. Always run all three. Always report all three.

$\hat R$ (Gelman-Rubin) — between-chain vs within-chain variance
$$\hat R = \sqrt{\frac{\hat V}{W}}, \quad \hat V = \frac{N-1}{N} W + \frac{1}{N} B, \quad W = \frac{1}{M(N-1)} \sum_{m,n} (\theta_n^{(m)} - \bar\theta^{(m)})^2$$

$M$ chains of length $N$. $W$ = within-chain variance (mean of within-chain variances). $B$ = between-chain variance (variance of chain means × $N$). $\hat R$ approaches 1 as chains converge to the same stationary distribution. Modern threshold: $\hat R \le 1.01$. Older "$\hat R \le 1.1$" rule is too lenient.

Effective Sample Size (ESS)
$$\text{ESS} = \frac{N}{1 + 2\sum_{k=1}^\infty \rho_k}$$

$\rho_k$ = lag-$k$ autocorrelation of the chain. A chain with $\text{ESS} = 500$ from $N = 5000$ draws carries the same Monte Carlo information as 500 iid samples. Target: ESS $\ge 400$ for each parameter, $\ge 1000$ for tail quantiles (95% CI).

Trace plots
Visual mixing check
Plot $\theta^{(t)}$ vs $t$ for each chain. Good: looks like "fuzzy caterpillar" — chains overlap, no trends. Bad: chains visibly different, trending, or sticky in regions.
Rank plots
Modern alternative to trace
For each iteration $t$, rank $\theta^{(t)}$ across chains. Plot histograms per chain. If converged: histograms are uniform. Powerful at detecting subtle non-mixing.
Divergences (HMC-specific)
Geometric warning
HMC reports "divergent transitions" when the leapfrog integrator becomes unstable — typically in regions of high posterior curvature. Even one divergence after warmup signals biased posterior estimates.
If diagnostics fail
Fix the model, not the chain
Reparameterize (non-centered for hierarchical models). Add weak priors to constrain unidentified parameters. Increase warmup. As a last resort: use a different sampler (NUTS handles many bad geometries).
// Section 6 · HMC

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo — The Gradient-Powered Sampler

Random-walk Metropolis takes small, locally-random steps — exploration is slow in high dimensions. HMC (Duane et al. 1987) uses gradient information $\nabla \log \tilde p(\theta)$ to take much larger, more directed steps. Conceptually: imagine a frictionless puck sliding on the negative log-posterior surface.

Hamiltonian dynamics — the physics analogy
$$H(\theta, \mathbf r) = -\log \tilde p(\theta) + \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf r^T \mathbf M^{-1} \mathbf r$$
$$\frac{d\theta}{dt} = \mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf r, \quad \frac{d\mathbf r}{dt} = \nabla \log \tilde p(\theta)$$

Augment parameter space with momentum $\mathbf r$. Define Hamiltonian $H$ = potential energy ($-\log \tilde p$) + kinetic energy ($\tfrac{1}{2} \mathbf r^T \mathbf M^{-1} \mathbf r$). Hamilton's equations preserve $H$ exactly — and the joint distribution $e^{-H(\theta, \mathbf r)} \propto \tilde p(\theta) e^{-\mathbf r^T \mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf r/2}$. Marginalizing out $\mathbf r$ gives back $\tilde p(\theta)$.

HMC algorithm
1
At step $t$ with state $\theta^{(t)}$: sample momentum $\mathbf r \sim N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf M)$.
2
Run Hamilton's equations forward for $L$ steps using a leapfrog integrator with step size $\epsilon$.
3
Negate momentum at end of trajectory: $\mathbf r^* \to -\mathbf r^*$ (makes proposal reversible).
4
Accept/reject with MH rule using the augmented joint $\tilde p(\theta) e^{-\mathbf r^T \mathbf M^{-1} \mathbf r/2}$. Acceptance ratio = $\exp(H(\theta^{(t)}, \mathbf r) - H(\theta^*, \mathbf r^*))$.
5
Hamilton dynamics conserve $H$ exactly — so in principle acceptance is always 1. Numerical (leapfrog) integration introduces small errors — typical acceptance is 70–95%.
Why HMC outperforms RW
Steps along contours, not random
Random-walk steps rarely "see" the geometry of the target. HMC's trajectory follows energy contours — moves coherently through correlated dimensions. ESS per second often 100×+ better than RW.
Tuning
$\epsilon$ (step size) and $L$ (path length)
$\epsilon$ too large: leapfrog diverges, acceptance crashes. $L$ too small: limited move per iteration. $L$ too large: trajectory loops back ("U-turn"). Manual tuning is painful — NUTS (next section) auto-tunes both.
Requires gradients
Auto-diff systems make this easy
HMC needs $\nabla \log \tilde p(\theta)$. Modern probabilistic programming languages (Stan, PyMC, NumPyro) auto-differentiate the log-posterior — you write the model, gradients are free.
Geometric problems remain
High-curvature regions
Funnel-shaped posteriors (typical in hierarchical models) are hard for any sampler. Reparameterization (centered vs non-centered) is often required. HMC + reparameterization handles most modern problems; classical RW does not.
// Section 7 · NUTS

NUTS — No-U-Turn Sampler — The Modern Default

NUTS (Hoffman & Gelman 2014) auto-tunes HMC's path length $L$ — eliminating the most painful tuning parameter. The "U-turn" criterion: stop the trajectory when it starts to fold back on itself.

U-turn detection criterion
$$\text{stop when } (\theta^+ - \theta^-)^T \mathbf r^- < 0 \;\;\text{or}\;\; (\theta^+ - \theta^-)^T \mathbf r^+ < 0$$

Build the trajectory by doubling forward/backward (binary tree structure). At each doubling, check if any sub-trajectory satisfies the U-turn condition. Choose final state via slice sampling on the tree to maintain detailed balance. Step size $\epsilon$ is auto-tuned during warmup via dual averaging (Nesterov 2009) targeting an acceptance rate of 0.8.

⚡ Why NUTS is the modern default

NUTS (with auto-tuned $\epsilon$) is the default in Stan, PyMC, NumPyro, Turing.jl, and most modern Bayesian software. Reasons: (1) zero hyperparameters to tune by hand; (2) state-of-the-art ESS per gradient evaluation for most problems; (3) automatic divergence detection signals model pathologies; (4) handles tens to thousands of dimensions reliably. For 95% of practical Bayesian problems, you don't need to think about MCMC mechanics — just write your model in a probabilistic programming language and let NUTS run.

// Section 8 · Reparameterization

Reparameterization — When Even NUTS Struggles

Some posteriors are pathological for ANY sampler. The classic example: hierarchical funnel.

Centered parameterization (often pathological for hierarchical models)
$$\tau \sim \text{Half-Normal}(1), \;\; \mu_j \mid \tau \sim N(0, \tau^2), \;\; y_j \sim N(\mu_j, \sigma^2)$$
$$\text{Non-centered: } \mu_j = \tau \cdot z_j, \;\; z_j \sim N(0, 1)$$

In the centered version, when $\tau$ is small, $\mu_j$ is forced into a narrow region — the posterior has an extreme funnel shape that even HMC cannot navigate. The non-centered version separates the global scale ($\tau$) from the standardized values ($z_j$), giving a posterior that's much closer to a Standard Normal and trivial to sample.

⚠ Reparameterization is a model choice, not a sampler choice

The two parameterizations give the SAME posterior distribution mathematically — but very different geometries. NUTS may completely fail on the centered version (divergences, $\hat R \gg 1$) and succeed perfectly on the non-centered version. Modern Bayesian workflow: try centered first; if diagnostics fail, switch to non-centered. For some structures (e.g., dense correlated parameters), there's no easy reparameterization — these are open research problems.

Python — From Hand-Coded MH to PyMC

python · hand-coded random-walk Metropolis on Beta-Binomial (verify against analytic)
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import beta
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

np.random.seed(42)
# Kartify: known posterior is Beta(17, 95) — we'll sample it via MH and verify
alpha_post, beta_post = 17, 95

def log_target(theta):
    if theta <= 0 or theta >= 1:
        return -np.inf
    return (alpha_post - 1) * np.log(theta) + (beta_post - 1) * np.log(1 - theta)

def rw_metropolis(log_target, theta_0, n_iter, step_size):
    samples = np.empty(n_iter)
    samples[0] = theta_0
    n_accept = 0
    log_p_curr = log_target(theta_0)
    for t in range(1, n_iter):
        proposal = samples[t-1] + np.random.normal(0, step_size)
        log_p_prop = log_target(proposal)
        log_alpha = log_p_prop - log_p_curr  # symmetric proposal cancels
        if np.log(np.random.uniform()) < log_alpha:
            samples[t] = proposal
            log_p_curr = log_p_prop
            n_accept += 1
        else:
            samples[t] = samples[t-1]
    return samples, n_accept / n_iter

# Sample
samples, accept_rate = rw_metropolis(log_target, theta_0=0.5, n_iter=10000, step_size=0.05)
print(f"Acceptance rate: {accept_rate:.3f}")

# Discard burn-in
samples = samples[1000:]
print(f"Posterior mean (MCMC): {samples.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Posterior mean (analytic): {alpha_post / (alpha_post + beta_post):.4f}")

ci_mcmc = np.percentile(samples, [2.5, 97.5])
ci_analytic = beta(alpha_post, beta_post).ppf([0.025, 0.975])
print(f"95% CI (MCMC):     [{ci_mcmc[0]:.4f}, {ci_mcmc[1]:.4f}]")
print(f"95% CI (analytic): [{ci_analytic[0]:.4f}, {ci_analytic[1]:.4f}]")

# Try different step sizes — see acceptance / mixing trade-off
print("\nTuning step size:")
for step in [0.005, 0.05, 0.5, 2.0]:
    _, ar = rw_metropolis(log_target, theta_0=0.15, n_iter=5000, step_size=step)
    print(f"  step={step}: acceptance={ar:.3f}")
# Optimal acceptance ~0.44 for 1-D (Roberts, Gelman, Gilks 1997)
python · convergence diagnostics — R-hat and ESS
import numpy as np
import arviz as az

np.random.seed(42)

# Run 4 chains from over-dispersed starts
n_chains, n_iter, burn = 4, 5000, 1000
chains = np.empty((n_chains, n_iter - burn))
for c in range(n_chains):
    init = np.random.uniform(0.05, 0.95)
    samples, _ = rw_metropolis(log_target, init, n_iter, 0.05)
    chains[c] = samples[burn:]

# R-hat: closer to 1.0 = better convergence
# Modern threshold: R-hat ≤ 1.01
idata = az.from_dict(posterior={"theta": chains})
rhat = az.rhat(idata)
ess = az.ess(idata)
print(f"R-hat: {rhat['theta'].item():.4f} (target: ≤ 1.01)")
print(f"ESS:  {ess['theta'].item():.0f} (target: ≥ 400)")

# Trace plot

python · PyMC for the hierarchical Kartify pricing model
import pymc as pm
import numpy as np
import arviz as az

np.random.seed(42)

# Kartify: 47 product categories, conversion data per category
J = 47
n_per_cat = np.random.randint(5, 200, J)        # wildly varying sample sizes
true_thetas = np.random.beta(8, 92, J)         # true rates ~ 8% on avg
y_per_cat = np.random.binomial(n_per_cat, true_thetas)

# Hierarchical model — non-centered parameterization (recommended for hierarchical)
with pm.Model() as model:
    # Hyperpriors on the population distribution of θ_j
    mu = pm.Beta("mu", alpha=1, beta=1)              # global mean
    kappa = pm.HalfNormal("kappa", sigma=50)        # concentration (effective sample size of population)
    
    # Per-category conversion rate, drawn from Beta(mu*kappa, (1-mu)*kappa)
    theta = pm.Beta("theta", alpha=mu*kappa, beta=(1-mu)*kappa, shape=J)
    
    # Likelihood
    y_obs = pm.Binomial("y_obs", n=n_per_cat, p=theta, observed=y_per_cat)
    
    # Sample with NUTS (default)
    trace = pm.sample(2000, tune=1000, chains=4, cores=4, random_seed=42, target_accept=0.95)

# Diagnostics
summary = az.summary(trace, var_names=["mu", "kappa"])
print(summary)
print(f"\nAll R-hats < 1.01: {(az.rhat(trace).max() < 1.01).to_array().all().item()}")
print(f"Min ESS: {az.ess(trace).min().to_array().min().item():.0f}")
print(f"Divergences: {trace.sample_stats.diverging.sum().item()}")

# Per-category posterior means — see shrinkage in action
theta_post_mean = trace.posterior["theta"].mean(dim=["chain", "draw"]).values
mle_per_cat = y_per_cat / n_per_cat
print(f"\nShrinkage (small categories pulled toward global mean):")
print(f"  Smallest category (n={n_per_cat.min()}): MLE={mle_per_cat[n_per_cat.argmin()]:.3f}, posterior mean={theta_post_mean[n_per_cat.argmin()]:.3f}")
print(f"  Largest category (n={n_per_cat.max()}):  MLE={mle_per_cat[n_per_cat.argmax()]:.3f}, posterior mean={theta_post_mean[n_per_cat.argmax()]:.3f}")

Why does direct numerical integration fail for posteriors with $> 5$ parameters?
AIt's algorithmically hard to code
BCurse of dimensionality. Grid quadrature with $k$ points per axis needs $k^d$ evaluations. $k=10, d=10$: 10 billion calls. $d=20$: $10^{20}$. Quadrature is competitive only for $d \le 4$. MCMC scales because it doesn't need a grid — just a chain.

Q 02 / 10
MCMC's killer feature: which calculation does it avoid that direct posterior evaluation cannot?
AComputing the likelihood
BThe marginal likelihood (normalization constant) $p(\mathcal D) = \int p(\mathcal D \mid \theta)p(\theta)d\theta$. MCMC works on the unnormalized posterior $\tilde p(\theta) = p(\mathcal D \mid \theta)p(\theta)$ — the constant cancels in the MH acceptance ratio. This is why MCMC scales to high dimensions.

DComputing the gradient
Q 03 / 10
Detailed balance $\pi(\theta)K(\theta,\theta') = \pi(\theta')K(\theta',\theta)$ implies what?
AThe chain converges in one step
B$\pi$ is a stationary distribution of the chain. Integrate both sides over $\theta$: LHS becomes $\int \pi(\theta)K(\theta,\theta')d\theta$, RHS becomes $\pi(\theta')$ (kernel integrates to 1). So $\int \pi(\theta)K(\theta,\theta')d\theta = \pi(\theta')$ — the stationarity condition. Sufficient (not necessary) for stationarity.
CThe chain is iid
DThe chain is independent of starting state
Q 04 / 10
Random-walk Metropolis: optimal acceptance rate in high dimensions?
AAlways 1.0
B~0.234 (Roberts, Gelman, Gilks 1997). For 1-D it's ~0.44. Too high acceptance → tiny steps, slow exploration. Too low → frequent rejections, chain stuck. The 0.234 rule comes from optimizing diffusion limit of the chain.
C0.5
DLower the better
Q 05 / 10
Gibbs sampling — what's the acceptance probability?
A~0.234
BAlways 1. The proposal IS the conditional posterior — MH ratio simplifies to 1, no rejections. But mixing depends on parameter correlation: strongly correlated parameters cause "zigzag" exploration. Block Gibbs (joint updates) helps.
C0.5
DVariable

BNot converged by modern standards. Modern threshold is $\hat R \le 1.01$ (Vehtari et al. 2021). The older $\hat R \le 1.1$ rule is too lenient — has been shown to allow biased posterior estimates. Run longer or fix model.

DUse Wald test instead

Q 08 / 10
HMC's main advantage over random-walk Metropolis?
AAlways faster per iteration
BUses gradient information $\nabla \log \tilde p(\theta)$ to take coherent, much larger steps along energy contours. Result: ESS per gradient evaluation often 100×+ better than RW in moderate-to-high dimensions. Cost: each step is more expensive (multiple gradient evals); requires differentiable target.

DWorks without a likelihood

NUTS auto-tunes which HMC parameter that previously required manual tuning?

BThe trajectory length $L$ (number of leapfrog steps). NUTS detects "U-turns" and stops the trajectory when it folds back on itself. Step size $\epsilon$ is also auto-tuned during warmup via dual averaging. NUTS effectively eliminates manual MCMC tuning — that's why it's the modern default in Stan/PyMC/NumPyro.
