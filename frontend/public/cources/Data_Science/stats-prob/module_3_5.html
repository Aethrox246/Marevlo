Module 3.5 Population vs sample moments · Bias · Consistency · Skewness · Kurtosis · MoM Kartify: revenue moments, shape diagnostics Wasserman Ch. 3 · Evans & Rosenthal Ch. 3
⚡ Kartify — Two Numbers That Decide Everything
Friday. Priya's CFO: "What's our true average daily revenue?" Priya has 90 days of observations. She types df["R"].mean() — ₹47.3 lakh. The CFO replies: "How confident are you it's not ₹45 or ₹50?"

This is the first question of statistical inference, and it's about distinguishing a sample statistic from a population parameter. The population mean $\mu$ is the true number Priya wants. The sample mean $\bar{X}$ is her noisy estimate. They're related but not equal. Module 3.7 will formalize the sampling distribution of $\bar{X}$; this module builds the foundation — what moments are, how sample versions estimate them, and whether the estimators are any good.

We also add two new moments — skewness (asymmetry) and kurtosis (tail heaviness) — the shape diagnostics that tell you whether a Normal model will work for your data. If sample skewness is 2.8 and sample kurtosis is 15, forget Normal — you have a heavy-tailed distribution and need Log-normal or a mixture model. This is the quiet discipline that separates credible statistical work from dashboard gymnastics.
// Section 1

Population vs Sample Moments — Target vs Estimator

QuantityPopulation (theoretical)Sample (estimator from data)
Mean$\mu = E[X]$$\bar{X} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$
Variance$\sigma^2 = E[(X - \mu)^2]$$s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2$
$k$-th central moment$\mu_k = E[(X - \mu)^k]$$m_k = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^k$
Skewness$\gamma_1 = \mu_3 / \sigma^3$$\hat\gamma_1 = m_3 / s^3$
Kurtosis (excess)$\gamma_2 = \mu_4/\sigma^4 - 3$$\hat\gamma_2 = m_4 / s^4 - 3$
Two key facts — the sample mean is unbiased; sample variance uses $n - 1$
$$E[\bar{X}] = \mu, \qquad E[s^2] = \sigma^2 \text{ (if we divide by } n - 1)$$
Why $n - 1$ and not $n$ — the bias correction
1
If we defined $\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n} \sum (X_i - \bar{X})^2$ — looks natural, but $\bar{X}$ is itself an estimator. Deviations from $\bar{X}$ are always smaller than deviations from the true $\mu$.
2
Claim: $E\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum (X_i - \bar{X})^2\right] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$. Biased low — off by a factor of $(n-1)/n$.
3
Proof sketch: $\sum(X_i - \bar X)^2 = \sum(X_i - \mu)^2 - n(\bar X - \mu)^2$. Take expectations: $n\sigma^2 - n \cdot \sigma^2/n = (n - 1)\sigma^2$.
4
To remove bias, divide by $n - 1$ instead of $n$. Then $E[s^2] = \sigma^2$ exactly. The "lost" degree of freedom is what was used to estimate $\bar{X}$. $\blacksquare$
The big idea: a good estimator's properties are a random variable's properties
$\bar{X}$ is a random variable — it depends on the random sample. To evaluate it, we ask: what's its mean (bias)? What's its variance (precision)? Does it converge to the target (consistency)? These are exactly the properties we learned in Module 2.7 applied to the estimator itself. Statistical inference is the study of estimators as random variables.
// Section 2

Properties of $\bar{X}$ — The Estimator We Trust

Sampling properties of $\bar{X}$ (i.i.d. $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ with mean $\mu$, variance $\sigma^2$)
$$E[\bar X] = \mu \quad (\text{unbiased}), \qquad \mathrm{Var}(\bar X) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \quad (\text{precision grows as } 1/n)$$
Proof — both lines, instantly
1
$E[\bar X] = E\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum X_i\right] = \frac{1}{n} \sum E[X_i] = \frac{1}{n} \cdot n\mu = \mu$ by linearity (no independence needed).
2
$\mathrm{Var}(\bar X) = \mathrm{Var}\left(\frac{1}{n} \sum X_i\right) = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum \mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$ by independence (covariance terms drop).
3
Standard error: $\mathrm{SE}(\bar X) = \sigma/\sqrt{n}$. Doubling sample size reduces SE by factor $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$; need 4× data to halve SE. $\blacksquare$
Kartify · how confident is Priya in ₹47.3 lakh?
Data. $n = 90$ days. Sample mean $\bar{x} = 47.3$ lakh. Sample standard deviation $s = 6.2$ lakh.
Estimated SE. $\hat{\mathrm{SE}}(\bar X) = s / \sqrt{n} = 6.2 / \sqrt{90} \approx 0.653$ lakh.
95% CI for $\mu$. $\bar{x} \pm 1.96 \cdot \hat{\mathrm{SE}} = 47.3 \pm 1.28 = [46.02, 48.58]$ lakh. (Normal approximation; Module 3.7 refines with $t$.)
CFO answer. Priya is 95% confident the true daily mean is between ₹46.0 and ₹48.6 lakh. ₹45 is below the CI (mildly ruled out); ₹50 is above (also ruled out). ₹47.3 ± 1.28 is a much more useful statement than the point estimate alone.
⚡ Consistency — the $n \to \infty$ guarantee

An estimator $\hat\theta_n$ is consistent if $\hat\theta_n \to \theta$ in probability as $n \to \infty$. For $\bar{X}$: since bias is 0 and variance → 0, Chebyshev's inequality gives $P(|\bar X - \mu| > \epsilon) \to 0$ for every $\epsilon > 0$. This is the Weak Law of Large Numbers. "More data gets you closer to the truth" — formalized. Consistency is the minimum bar for any estimator worth using.

// Section 3

Skewness — Is Your Data Symmetric?

Skewness — standardized third central moment
$$\gamma_1 = \frac{E[(X - \mu)^3]}{\sigma^3}$$
$\gamma_1 = 0$
Symmetric
Normal, Uniform, $t$-distribution — symmetric around the mean. Mean = median.
$\gamma_1 > 0$
Right-skewed
Long tail to the right. Mean > median. Exponential, Log-normal, revenue data, income. Most real-world positive data.
$\gamma_1 < 0$
Left-skewed
Long tail to the left. Mean < median. Exam scores with ceiling effects, time-to-event data close to a cap.
Kartify diagnostic
$|\hat\gamma_1| > 1$
Noticeably skewed — Normal fits will miss tails. Consider log transform or Log-normal. $|\hat\gamma_1| > 2$ is strongly non-Normal.
Kartify · skewness of order values
Data. 10,000 Kartify orders. Sample: $\bar x = 1{,}840$, $s = 2{,}400$, median = 720.
Sample skewness. Computed via $\hat\gamma_1 = m_3 / s^3 \approx 3.8$ — strong right skew.
Interpretation. $\hat\gamma_1 \gg 1$ confirms the Log-normal diagnosis from Module 3.3. The distribution has a heavy right tail — a small fraction of very expensive orders dominates the mean. Log-transform brings $\hat\gamma_1$ of $\log X$ close to zero.
Modeling implication. Normal-based inference on order values is inappropriate. Use Log-normal, or work with $\log X$ if Normal is a pragmatic fit for the logged data. Track 4 will formalize this for MLE.

Kurtosis — How Heavy Are the Tails?

Kurtosis and excess kurtosis
$$\mathrm{Kurt}(X) = \frac{E[(X - \mu)^4]}{\sigma^4}, \qquad \gamma_2 = \mathrm{Kurt}(X) - 3 \quad (\text{excess})$$
DistributionKurtosisExcess $\gamma_2$Tail behavior
Normal30Reference; "mesokurtic"
Uniform1.8$-1.2$Light tails, platykurtic
$t$-distribution (df = 4)$\infty$ (for df ≤ 4)$\infty$Heavy tails — kurtosis doesn't exist for df ≤ 4
Exponential96Heavier than Normal right tail
Log-normal ($\sigma = 1$)$\approx 110$$\approx 107$Extremely heavy right tail — Kartify order values territory
⚠ The kurtosis interpretation trap

Common phrasing: "kurtosis measures peakedness." This is wrong. Kurtosis measures tail weight vs a Normal reference. A high-kurtosis distribution has heavier tails, which mathematically forces a sharper peak only as a consequence of variance being fixed. But interpreting high kurtosis as "peaky" misses the actionable business signal — the extremes. For Kartify: "our order value distribution has excess kurtosis $\sim 100$" means "very few extreme-value orders have outsized impact," not "orders cluster tightly around the mean."

Kartify · kurtosis as a risk flag
Daily revenue kurtosis. Observed $\hat\gamma_2 \approx 1.3$. Modestly heavier-tailed than Normal. 3-sigma events occur a bit more often than 1-in-370 days — maybe 1-in-200.
Order value kurtosis. Observed $\hat\gamma_2 \approx 100$. Extreme right tail. Outlier values well beyond 10σ from the mean occur regularly. Planning by "mean and sigma" alone is unsafe.
Decision. For daily revenue, Normal-based inference is okay. For order values, use Log-normal or the empirical distribution (percentile bootstrap, Track 4).

Method of Moments — A Preview of Inference

The simplest parameter estimation strategy: match the sample moments to the population moments, and solve for the parameters.

Method of Moments recipe
$$\text{Set } m_k = \mu_k(\theta) \text{ for } k = 1, 2, \ldots, p, \text{ and solve for } \theta$$
Worked example — MoM for Exponential (one parameter, one equation)
1
Exp$(\lambda)$ has $E[X] = 1/\lambda$.
2
Set sample mean = theoretical mean: $\bar X = 1/\hat\lambda$.
3
Solve: $\hat\lambda_{\text{MoM}} = 1/\bar X$.
4
For Exponential this happens to coincide with the MLE; in general they differ. MoM is simpler; MLE is asymptotically more efficient.
Kartify · Normal — two parameters, two moments
Assume. $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$; unknown parameters $\mu, \sigma^2$.
Match mean. $\bar X = \hat\mu$.
Match second moment. $\frac{1}{n}\sum X_i^2 = \hat\mu^2 + \hat\sigma^2$, so $\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum X_i^2 - \bar X^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum(X_i - \bar X)^2$.
Result. $\hat\mu_{\text{MoM}} = \bar X$, $\hat\sigma^2_{\text{MoM}} = $ sample variance with divisor $n$ (not $n-1$).
Note. MoM's variance estimator is biased. MLE gives the same result. The unbiased estimator $s^2$ (divisor $n-1$) is a small modification.
◆ Why MoM matters even with MLE around

MoM estimators are simple, have closed forms, and serve as starting values for numerical MLE. For complex models where MLE requires iterative optimization (GLMs, mixture models), MoM provides an initial $\hat\theta$ that's almost always good enough to start from. They are also consistent under mild regularity conditions. Track 4 compares MoM and MLE in depth.

Python — Moment Computations & MoM

python · sample moments and shape diagnostics
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import skew, kurtosis

# Simulate Kartify data: daily revenue ≈ Normal, order value ≈ Log-normal
np.random.seed(42)
rev = np.random.normal(loc=47.3, scale=6.2, size=10_000)       # ₹ lakh/day
orders = np.random.lognormal(mean=6.58, sigma=1.37, size=10_000) # ₹ per order

for name, data in [("Daily revenue", rev), ("Order value", orders)]:
    print(f"--- {name} ---")
    print(f"  mean   = {data.mean():,.1f}")
    print(f"  median = {np.median(data):,.1f}")
    print(f"  std    = {data.std(ddof=1):,.1f}  (using n-1)")
    print(f"  skew   = {skew(data):.2f}")
    print(f"  kurt   = {kurtosis(data):.2f}  (excess; Normal is 0)")
# Revenue: skew ≈ 0, kurtosis ≈ 0 → Normal assumption holds
# Orders:  skew ≈ 3-4, kurtosis ≈ 20+ → Normal fails badly; use Log-normal
python · bias of $n$ vs $n - 1$ variance estimator — illustrated
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(0)
true_sigma2 = 9.0       # true σ² = 9
n = 10                 # small sample to make bias visible
n_trials = 50_000

biased, unbiased = [], []
for _ in range(n_trials):
    sample = np.random.normal(0, np.sqrt(true_sigma2), n)
    xbar = sample.mean()
    biased.append(((sample - xbar)**2).sum() / n)        # divide by n
    unbiased.append(((sample - xbar)**2).sum() / (n - 1))  # divide by n-1

print(f"True σ² = {true_sigma2}")
print(f"Biased   (÷ n)  estimator: mean = {np.mean(biased):.3f}  (expected {true_sigma2 * (n-1)/n:.3f})")
print(f"Unbiased (÷ n-1) estimator: mean = {np.mean(unbiased):.3f}  (expected {true_sigma2})")

# The biased version undershoots by factor (n-1)/n = 0.9 for n=10
# For n=100, bias is 1% — often negligible. For n=10, 10% — material.
python · method of moments — Gamma distribution fit
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import gamma

# Suppose Kartify delivery times look Gamma — fit (k, λ) via method of moments
# Gamma(k, λ): E[X] = k/λ, Var(X) = k/λ², so k = E²/Var, λ = E/Var
np.random.seed(42)
k_true, lam_true = 2.5, 0.1
data = np.random.gamma(shape=k_true, scale=1/lam_true, size=5_000)

# MoM estimators
xbar, var = data.mean(), data.var(ddof=1)
k_mom = xbar**2 / var
lam_mom = xbar / var
print(f"MoM:  k̂ = {k_mom:.3f}, λ̂ = {lam_mom:.4f}  (true k = {k_true}, λ = {lam_true})")

# Compare to scipy's MLE
k_mle, loc, scale = gamma.fit(data, floc=0)
lam_mle = 1 / scale
print(f"MLE:  k̂ = {k_mle:.3f}, λ̂ = {lam_mle:.4f}")

# MoM and MLE differ slightly; both are consistent (→ true as n → ∞)
