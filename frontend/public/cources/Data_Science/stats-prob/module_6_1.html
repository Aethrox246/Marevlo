Bayesian Inference —
Prior, Likelihood, Posterior

A different paradigm. Frequentist statistics treats $\theta$ as a fixed unknown constant; Bayesian statistics treats $\theta$ as a random variable with a probability distribution that updates as data arrives. Posterior $\propto$ prior $\times$ likelihood — Bayes's rule applied to inference. Conjugate priors (Beta-Binomial, Normal-Normal, Gamma-Poisson) give closed-form posteriors. Credible intervals are not confidence intervals — the philosophical difference matters. Bayes factors quantify evidence. Posterior predictive distributions answer "what's next?" The foundation for everything in modern probabilistic ML.

⚡ Kartify — Where Frequentist Methods Hit a Wall
Week 10. The data science team is celebrating Track 5 — the regression toolkit is in production. But Priya brings three new questions, and each one breaks the frequentist toolkit.

Question 1. "We launched a new payment flow yesterday. 12 users tried it; 9 converted. Should we roll it out?" Frequentist 95% CI for the proportion: $[0.428, 0.943]$ — useless. The Wald approximation is dreadful for small $n$, and the team has prior beliefs (their old flow converts at 8%) that the analysis ignores entirely.

Question 2. "Is the new model actually better than the old one, or did we just get lucky on test data?" Frequentist gives a $p$-value, but Priya wants a number she can communicate: "the probability that the new model is better is 87%." Frequentist cannot directly answer this — $P(\text{model is better})$ requires treating "model is better" as a random event with a probability, which frequentists don't do for parameters.

Question 3. "Predict tomorrow's order count and give me a range that accounts for both data noise AND parameter uncertainty." Frequentist prediction intervals account for sampling variability of $\hat\theta$ implicitly through plug-in; Bayesian posterior predictive integrates over $\theta$ explicitly, giving honest intervals.

Each of these is a Bayesian-natural question. The Bayesian framework treats parameters as random variables with probability distributions. Prior beliefs $p(\theta)$ combine with data $p(\text{data} \mid \theta)$ via Bayes's rule to give the posterior $p(\theta \mid \text{data})$ — a complete probabilistic description of what we now believe about $\theta$. Everything else (intervals, decisions, predictions) flows from the posterior.

Module 6.1 is the most foundational shift in the entire course. After this, you'll think about $\theta$ as a distribution, not a fixed point — and that single change unlocks Bayesian deep learning, MCMC (Module 6.2), Bayesian optimization, hierarchical models, and the entire probabilistic-programming ecosystem (PyMC, Stan, NumPyro, Pyro).

Running examples. Beta-Binomial conjugacy on Kartify's payment-flow conversions (small-sample inference). Normal-Normal conjugacy on order-time estimation. Gamma-Poisson conjugacy on daily-order rates. Each fully derived; each compared to the frequentist counterpart.
// Section 1 · The Paradigm Shift

The Bayesian Paradigm — Parameters as Random Variables

Frequentist
$\theta$ is fixed, data is random
"$\theta = 0.08$ is the true conversion rate. We observed 9 out of 12 converters; how unusual is that under $H_0$?" Probability is long-run frequency. Inference uses sampling distributions of estimators.
Bayesian
$\theta$ is random, data is fixed
"We observed 9 out of 12. Given our prior beliefs about $\theta$ and this data, what's our updated probability distribution over $\theta$?" Probability is degree of belief. Inference uses the posterior $p(\theta \mid \text{data})$.
Both are valid
Different questions, different answers
Neither paradigm is "correct" — they answer different questions. Frequentist: long-run error rates of procedures. Bayesian: probability statements about parameters. Many problems are answerable in both; some are natural in only one.
When Bayesian shines
Small samples, prior info, decisions
Small samples (where asymptotics fail). Genuine prior information available. Decision-making under uncertainty (loss functions on the posterior). Hierarchical/multi-level data. Combining studies (Bayesian meta-analysis).
Bayes's rule for inference
$$p(\theta \mid \mathcal D) = \frac{p(\mathcal D \mid \theta) \, p(\theta)}{p(\mathcal D)}$$
$$\underbrace{p(\theta \mid \mathcal D)}_{\text{posterior}} \;\propto\; \underbrace{p(\mathcal D \mid \theta)}_{\text{likelihood}} \times \underbrace{p(\theta)}_{\text{prior}}$$

The denominator $p(\mathcal D) = \int p(\mathcal D \mid \theta) p(\theta) d\theta$ — the marginal likelihood or evidence — is just a normalization constant. We work with proportionality $\propto$ whenever possible.

The single equation that unifies all of Bayesian inference
Three terms, three roles:
  • Prior $p(\theta)$. What you believed about $\theta$ before seeing data $\mathcal D$. Encodes background knowledge, previous studies, expert judgment, or vague ignorance.
  • Likelihood $p(\mathcal D \mid \theta)$. Identical to the frequentist likelihood — same formula, same calculation. The bridge connecting both paradigms.
  • Posterior $p(\theta \mid \mathcal D)$. What you believe about $\theta$ after seeing the data. The complete Bayesian inference — every question (point estimate, interval, prediction, decision) is answered by integrating against the posterior.
Note the asymmetry: Bayes's rule lets you compute $p(\theta \mid \mathcal D)$ from $p(\mathcal D \mid \theta)$ — which is critical because the latter is what your model gives you, but the former is what you actually want to know.
// Section 2 · Priors

Prior Distributions — The Most Misunderstood Object in Statistics

Critics often attack Bayesian inference for "introducing subjectivity." This is a misunderstanding. Every analysis makes assumptions; Bayesian analysis just makes them explicit and quantitative.

Prior typeDescriptionWhen to use
InformativeStrong, narrow distribution from prior knowledge or studiesGenuine prior data exists (meta-analysis, replication of past work)
Weakly informativeMild constraints (e.g., "coefficient is probably between -10 and 10")Default in modern practice — regularizes without overwhelming data
Non-informative / vagueFlat or near-flat over plausible range (e.g., $\text{Uniform}(0, 1)$)"Let the data speak" — minimal prior influence
ImproperDoesn't integrate to 1 (e.g., $p(\theta) \propto 1$ on $\mathbb R$)Mathematical convenience; valid if posterior is proper
Jeffreys prior$p(\theta) \propto \sqrt{I(\theta)}$ (Fisher info)Invariant to reparameterization — principled "objective" choice
Reference / empirical BayesData-driven prior (estimated from data)Hierarchical models, large datasets where pure prior elicitation hard
⚡ Informative isn't a dirty word

"Subjective" priors are honest. If Kartify's old payment flow converts at 8% with high confidence (millions of past sessions), and the new flow is structurally similar, a prior centered near 0.08 reflects genuine knowledge — and gives more accurate inference than a flat prior pretending we know nothing. The danger is HIDING the prior assumption (which frequentist analyses do too — choosing a likelihood is itself an assumption). Bayesian analysis just makes assumptions auditable. Best practice: always run a sensitivity analysis with multiple priors. If conclusions are stable, the prior wasn't doing the work.

// Section 3 · Beta-Binomial

Beta-Binomial Conjugacy — The Canonical Bayesian Example

If the likelihood is Binomial and the prior is Beta, the posterior is also Beta. Conjugacy means closed-form posteriors — no integration needed.

Beta-Binomial setup
$$\theta \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta), \quad p(\theta) = \frac{\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha, \beta)}$$
$$Y \mid \theta \sim \text{Binomial}(n, \theta), \quad p(Y=y \mid \theta) = \binom{n}{y}\theta^y (1-\theta)^{n-y}$$
Posterior derivation — full proof of conjugacy
1
By Bayes: $p(\theta \mid y) \propto p(y \mid \theta) \, p(\theta)$.
2
Substitute likelihood and prior (drop constants not depending on $\theta$): $p(\theta \mid y) \propto \theta^y (1-\theta)^{n-y} \cdot \theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}$.
3
Combine exponents: $p(\theta \mid y) \propto \theta^{y + \alpha - 1}(1-\theta)^{n - y + \beta - 1}$.
4
Recognize: this is the kernel of $\text{Beta}(\alpha + y,\; \beta + n - y)$. Therefore $\boxed{\theta \mid y \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + y,\; \beta + n - y)}$. $\blacksquare$
5
Interpretation: prior parameters $(\alpha, \beta)$ are pseudo-counts — $\alpha$ "prior successes" and $\beta$ "prior failures." Posterior just adds observed counts to pseudo-counts.
Beta-Binomial in action — see the prior update to the posterior

Kartify · the new payment flow — small-sample Bayesian analysis
Prior. Old flow converts at ~8%. We assign $\theta \sim \text{Beta}(8, 92)$ — mean 0.08, mode near 0.07, equivalent to having seen 100 sessions with 8 conversions.
Data. 12 trials, 9 successes ($y = 9$, $n = 12$).
Posterior. $\theta \mid y \sim \text{Beta}(8 + 9,\; 92 + 12 - 9) = \text{Beta}(17, 95)$.
Posterior mean. $E[\theta \mid y] = 17/(17 + 95) = 17/112 = 0.152$.
Compare to MLE. $\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = 9/12 = 0.75$. Frequentist 95% Wald CI: $[0.428, 0.943]$.
Posterior 95% credible interval. $[\text{Beta}_{0.025}(17, 95),\; \text{Beta}_{0.975}(17, 95)] \approx [0.092, 0.222]$.
Interpretation. Bayesian posterior says: "Given prior beliefs and data, our updated estimate is 15.2% with 95% probability between 9.2% and 22.2%." MUCH more useful and honest than the frequentist's $[42.8\%, 94.3\%]$ which is technically valid but useless. The prior provides regularization that prevents the MLE from over-fitting to 12 data points.
Posterior as weighted compromise — explicit formula
Posterior mean: $E[\theta \mid y] = \dfrac{\alpha + y}{\alpha + \beta + n} = \dfrac{n}{n + \alpha + \beta} \cdot \dfrac{y}{n} + \dfrac{\alpha + \beta}{n + \alpha + \beta} \cdot \dfrac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}$.

This is a convex combination of the MLE ($y/n$) and the prior mean ($\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$), with weights determined by the relative "sample sizes" ($n$ for data, $\alpha + \beta$ for prior). As $n \to \infty$, posterior mean $\to$ MLE — data dominates. As $n \to 0$, posterior mean $\to$ prior mean — prior dominates. This is shrinkage: posterior pulled toward the prior, controlled by data quantity.
// Section 4 · Normal-Normal

Normal-Normal Conjugacy — The Continuous Analog

For Normal data with known variance and a Normal prior on the mean, the posterior is also Normal — the second canonical conjugacy.

Normal-Normal setup (known $\sigma^2$)
$$\mu \sim N(\mu_0, \tau_0^2), \quad Y_1, \ldots, Y_n \mid \mu \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} N(\mu, \sigma^2)$$
Posterior — derivation via completing the square
1
Likelihood: $p(\mathbf y \mid \mu) \propto \exp\!\left(-\dfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum(y_i - \mu)^2\right) \propto \exp\!\left(-\dfrac{n}{2\sigma^2}(\bar y - \mu)^2\right)$ (after dropping terms not in $\mu$).
2
Prior: $p(\mu) \propto \exp\!\left(-\dfrac{1}{2\tau_0^2}(\mu - \mu_0)^2\right)$.
3
Posterior $\propto$ product of two Gaussian kernels in $\mu$. The product of Gaussians (in $\mu$) is itself Gaussian — quadratic in $\mu$ in the exponent.
4
Combine exponents: $-\dfrac{1}{2}\left[\dfrac{n}{\sigma^2}(\mu - \bar y)^2 + \dfrac{1}{\tau_0^2}(\mu - \mu_0)^2\right]$. Expand and collect $\mu^2$ and $\mu$ coefficients.
5
Complete the square in $\mu$. The result: $\mu \mid \mathbf y \sim N(\mu_n, \tau_n^2)$ where $\dfrac{1}{\tau_n^2} = \dfrac{1}{\tau_0^2} + \dfrac{n}{\sigma^2}$ (precisions add) and $\mu_n = \tau_n^2 \left(\dfrac{\mu_0}{\tau_0^2} + \dfrac{n \bar y}{\sigma^2}\right)$.
6
$\boxed{\text{Posterior precision} = \text{prior precision} + \text{data precision}}$. Posterior mean = precision-weighted average of prior mean and sample mean.
Closed-form posterior — Normal-Normal (key result)
$$\mu \mid \mathbf y \sim N(\mu_n, \tau_n^2), \quad \mu_n = \frac{\frac{\mu_0}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n \bar y}{\sigma^2}}{\frac{1}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}}, \quad \tau_n^2 = \left(\frac{1}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}\right)^{-1}$$
Kartify · estimating typical session duration with prior info
Setup. Estimating mean session duration $\mu$ for a new feature.
Prior. Old features average ~120 seconds with sd ~30 between features: $\mu \sim N(120, 30^2)$.
Data. 25 sessions on the new feature: $\bar y = 145$, known $\sigma = 50$ from history.
Posterior precision. $\dfrac{1}{30^2} + \dfrac{25}{50^2} = \dfrac{1}{900} + \dfrac{1}{100} = \dfrac{1}{100} + \dfrac{1}{900} = \dfrac{10}{900} = 0.0111$. So $\tau_n^2 = 90$, $\tau_n \approx 9.49$.
Posterior mean. $\mu_n = 90 \cdot (120/900 + 25 \cdot 145 / 2500) = 90 \cdot (0.133 + 1.45) = 90 \cdot 1.583 = 142.5$.
Posterior 95% CI. $142.5 \pm 1.96 \cdot 9.49 = [123.9, 161.1]$.
Compare to frequentist. Sample-only 95% CI: $145 \pm 1.96 \cdot 50/\sqrt{25} = [125.4, 164.6]$. Bayesian CI is shifted toward the prior (142.5 vs 145) and slightly tighter (the prior contributes information). With more data ($n \to \infty$), the prior fades and posterior approaches frequentist CI.
// Section 5 · Gamma-Poisson

Gamma-Poisson Conjugacy — Counting Conjugacy

For Poisson data with rate $\lambda$ and Gamma prior on $\lambda$, the posterior is also Gamma. The rate-parameter analog of Beta-Binomial.

Gamma-Poisson setup
$$\lambda \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta), \quad p(\lambda) = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \lambda^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta\lambda}$$
$$Y_1, \ldots, Y_n \mid \lambda \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Poisson}(\lambda), \quad p(y_i \mid \lambda) = \frac{\lambda^{y_i} e^{-\lambda}}{y_i!}$$
Posterior derivation
1
Joint likelihood: $p(\mathbf y \mid \lambda) \propto \prod_i \lambda^{y_i} e^{-\lambda} = \lambda^{\sum y_i} e^{-n\lambda}$ (drop $y_i!$ — not in $\lambda$).
2
Posterior: $p(\lambda \mid \mathbf y) \propto p(\mathbf y \mid \lambda) p(\lambda) \propto \lambda^{\sum y_i} e^{-n\lambda} \cdot \lambda^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta\lambda}$.
3
Combine: $p(\lambda \mid \mathbf y) \propto \lambda^{\alpha + \sum y_i - 1} e^{-(\beta + n)\lambda}$ — kernel of $\text{Gamma}(\alpha + \sum y_i,\; \beta + n)$.
4
$\boxed{\lambda \mid \mathbf y \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha + \sum y_i,\; \beta + n)}$. Prior pseudo-events: $\alpha$ events in $\beta$ time units. Add observed events to $\alpha$, observation time to $\beta$. $\blacksquare$
Kartify · Bayesian estimation of daily fraud-incident rate
Prior. Industry baseline ~3 fraud incidents per day. Use $\lambda \sim \text{Gamma}(6, 2)$ — mean = 6/2 = 3, weakly informative.
Data. 7 days, total 28 incidents.
Posterior. $\lambda \mid \mathbf y \sim \text{Gamma}(6 + 28,\; 2 + 7) = \text{Gamma}(34, 9)$.
Posterior mean. $34/9 = 3.78$ incidents/day.
95% credible interval. $[\text{Gamma}_{0.025}(34, 9),\; \text{Gamma}_{0.975}(34, 9)] \approx [2.61, 5.21]$.
Compare to frequentist. MLE = $28/7 = 4$. Wald CI ≈ $[2.52, 5.48]$. Bayesian shrinks toward prior (3.78 vs 4) and gives slightly tighter interval. As more data arrive, agreement with frequentist increases.

// Section 6 · Credible vs Confidence

Credible Intervals vs Confidence Intervals — The Philosophical Divide

Frequentist 95% CI
Procedure-based
"If we repeated this experiment many times and computed an interval each time, 95% of those intervals would contain the true $\theta$." For any specific interval you computed, $\theta$ is either in it or not — you can't say "95% chance it's in this interval."
Bayesian 95% credible interval
Belief-based
"Given prior and data, there's a 95% probability that $\theta$ lies in this interval." A direct probability statement about $\theta$ — possible because $\theta$ is treated as a random variable.
Two ways to compute
Equal-tail vs HPD
Equal-tail: 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of posterior. Symmetric in tail probabilities. HPD (highest posterior density): shortest interval containing 95% mass. Always single connected interval for unimodal posteriors; may be disjoint for bimodal.
Often nearly identical
When prior is weak and $n$ is large
For weakly-informative priors and large $n$, Bayesian credible intervals and frequentist CIs are numerically very close. The difference matters most for small $n$, strong priors, or non-Normal posteriors — exactly where Bayesian methods are most useful.
⚠ Don't conflate these — even when numerically similar

A frequentist 95% CI for the new payment flow conversion rate ($n = 12$, $y = 9$) is $[0.428, 0.943]$. A Bayesian 95% credible interval (with the Beta(8, 92) prior) is $[0.092, 0.222]$. Reporting "we are 95% confident the conversion rate is in $[0.428, 0.943]$" is technically wrong (frequentist) AND useless (very wide). Reporting "the posterior probability that the conversion rate is in $[0.092, 0.222]$ is 95%" is correct (Bayesian) AND useful. The interpretation is in your hands; don't muddy it.

// Section 7 · Bayes Factors

Bayesian Hypothesis Testing — Bayes Factors

Bayes factor
$$\text{BF}_{10} = \frac{p(\mathcal D \mid H_1)}{p(\mathcal D \mid H_0)} = \frac{\int p(\mathcal D \mid \theta_1) p(\theta_1 \mid H_1) d\theta_1}{\int p(\mathcal D \mid \theta_0) p(\theta_0 \mid H_0) d\theta_0}$$

Ratio of marginal likelihoods. Combined with prior odds, gives posterior odds via $\frac{p(H_1 \mid \mathcal D)}{p(H_0 \mid \mathcal D)} = \text{BF}_{10} \cdot \frac{p(H_1)}{p(H_0)}$.

BF$_{10}$Evidence for $H_1$ (Jeffreys scale)
1–3Weak / barely worth mentioning
3–10Substantial
10–30Strong
30–100Very strong
$> 100$Decisive
⚡ Bayes factors fix the $p$-value's hidden flaw

$p$-values measure how unusual the data is under $H_0$ — but never under $H_1$. A small $p$-value means "data is inconsistent with $H_0$" — but doesn't tell you whether $H_1$ is consistent with the data either. Bayes factors compare BOTH hypotheses on equal footing, giving the relative evidence directly. Also: BFs can support $H_0$ (BF$_{01}$ large) — frequentist tests can only "fail to reject," never affirm.

// Section 8 · Posterior Predictive

Posterior Predictive Distribution — "What Comes Next?"

Posterior predictive distribution
$$p(y^{\text{new}} \mid \mathcal D) = \int p(y^{\text{new}} \mid \theta) \, p(\theta \mid \mathcal D) \, d\theta$$

For predicting a new observation $y^{\text{new}}$, integrate the likelihood over the posterior. This marginalizes over parameter uncertainty — the resulting distribution accounts for both data noise (likelihood) and parameter uncertainty (posterior).

Posterior predictive for Beta-Binomial
1
Posterior: $\theta \mid y \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + y, \beta + n - y) = \text{Beta}(\alpha', \beta')$.
2
Predict $y^{\text{new}}$ over $m$ new trials: $y^{\text{new}} \mid \theta \sim \text{Binomial}(m, \theta)$.
3
Posterior predictive: $p(y^{\text{new}} = k \mid y) = \int_0^1 \binom{m}{k} \theta^k (1-\theta)^{m-k} \cdot \dfrac{\theta^{\alpha'-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta'-1}}{B(\alpha',\beta')} d\theta$.
4
Recognize the Beta integral: $= \binom{m}{k} \dfrac{B(\alpha' + k,\; \beta' + m - k)}{B(\alpha',\beta')}$ — the Beta-Binomial distribution.
5
This is wider than $\text{Binomial}(m, \hat\theta)$ — it accounts for posterior uncertainty about $\theta$, not just sampling variability given a known $\theta$.
Kartify · predicting tomorrow's conversion count for the new payment flow
Setup. Posterior $\theta \sim \text{Beta}(17, 95)$. Plan to launch to 1000 sessions tomorrow.
Naive plug-in. Use $\hat\theta = 17/112 = 0.152$, predict $y^{\text{new}} \sim \text{Binomial}(1000, 0.152)$, mean = 152, sd = $\sqrt{1000 \cdot 0.152 \cdot 0.848} = 11.4$. 95% PI ≈ $[130, 174]$.
Posterior predictive. $y^{\text{new}} \sim \text{Beta-Binomial}(1000, 17, 95)$. Mean = 1000 · 0.152 = 152 (same). Variance = 1000 · 0.152 · 0.848 · (1 + (1000 - 1)/(112 + 1)) = 130.5 · 9.84 = 1284. sd ≈ 35.8.
95% PI. $\approx [82, 222]$ — much wider than naive.
The point. Naive plug-in pretends we know $\theta$ exactly. Posterior predictive correctly accounts for our uncertainty about $\theta$ (which is large after only 12 observations). The Beta-Binomial PI of $[82, 222]$ is the honest range of "what could happen tomorrow." Critical for capacity planning, A/B-test design, and risk management.
// Section 9 · Python

Python — Closed-Form Bayesian Inference

python · Beta-Binomial — full posterior + credible interval + posterior predictive
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import beta, binom, betabinom
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Kartify new payment flow
alpha_prior, beta_prior = 8, 92          # prior: ~8% conversion expected
n, y = 12, 9                            # data: 9/12 converted

# Posterior parameters
alpha_post = alpha_prior + y
beta_post = beta_prior + n - y
posterior = beta(alpha_post, beta_post)
print(f"Posterior: Beta({alpha_post}, {beta_post})")
print(f"Posterior mean: {posterior.mean():.3f} (vs MLE = {y/n:.3f})")

# 95% credible intervals
ci_eq = posterior.ppf([0.025, 0.975])           # equal-tail
print(f"95% equal-tail credible interval: [{ci_eq[0]:.3f}, {ci_eq[1]:.3f}]")

# HPD interval — find shortest interval containing 95% mass
def hpd_interval(dist, mass=0.95, n_grid=10000):
    x = np.linspace(0.001, 0.999, n_grid)
    pdf = dist.pdf(x)
    threshold = np.linspace(pdf.max(), 0, 5000)
    for t in threshold:
        in_region = x[pdf >= t]
        if len(in_region) > 0:
            mass_below = dist.cdf(in_region.max()) - dist.cdf(in_region.min())
            if mass_below >= mass:
                return in_region.min(), in_region.max()
    return dist.ppf(0.025), dist.ppf(0.975)

hpd_lo, hpd_hi = hpd_interval(posterior)
print(f"95% HPD interval: [{hpd_lo:.3f}, {hpd_hi:.3f}]")

# Posterior predictive — Beta-Binomial
m = 1000                                  # planned sessions tomorrow
pp = betabinom(m, alpha_post, beta_post)
print(f"\nPosterior predictive for {m} sessions:")
print(f"  Mean: {pp.mean():.1f} conversions")
print(f"  SD: {pp.std():.1f} (vs naive Binomial SD: {np.sqrt(m * posterior.mean() * (1 - posterior.mean())):.1f})")
print(f"  95% PI: [{pp.ppf(0.025):.0f}, {pp.ppf(0.975):.0f}]")

# Compare to naive plug-in
naive = binom(m, posterior.mean())
print(f"Naive plug-in 95% PI: [{naive.ppf(0.025):.0f}, {naive.ppf(0.975):.0f}]")
# Naive ignores posterior uncertainty about θ — too narrow
python · Normal-Normal — closed-form precision-weighted updating
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

# Kartify session duration estimation
mu_0, tau_0 = 120, 30            # prior: N(120, 30²)
sigma = 50                       # known data sd from history
np.random.seed(42)
data = np.random.normal(145, sigma, 25)
n = len(data); y_bar = data.mean()
print(f"Sample: n={n}, mean={y_bar:.2f}")

# Posterior — precision-weighted
prec_prior = 1 / tau_0**2
prec_data = n / sigma**2
prec_post = prec_prior + prec_data
mu_post = (mu_0 * prec_prior + y_bar * prec_data) / prec_post
tau_post = np.sqrt(1 / prec_post)
print(f"Posterior: N({mu_post:.2f}, {tau_post:.2f}²)")
print(f"95% credible interval: [{mu_post - 1.96*tau_post:.2f}, {mu_post + 1.96*tau_post:.2f}]")

# Compare to frequentist
freq_se = sigma / np.sqrt(n)
print(f"Frequentist 95% CI: [{y_bar - 1.96*freq_se:.2f}, {y_bar + 1.96*freq_se:.2f}]")

# Sequential updating — see how posterior tightens with each batch
print("\nSequential update (5 sessions at a time):")
mu_t, tau_t = mu_0, tau_0
for i in range(0, n, 5):
    batch = data[i:i+5]
    nb = len(batch)
    pp = 1/tau_t**2 + nb/sigma**2
    mu_t = (mu_t/tau_t**2 + batch.mean()*nb/sigma**2) / pp
    tau_t = np.sqrt(1/pp)
    print(f"  After {i+nb} obs: μ_n={mu_t:.2f}, τ_n={tau_t:.2f}")
python · Bayes factor — does new model beat old?
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import beta
from scipy.special import betaln

# Kartify: compare two hypotheses for the new payment flow
# H0: θ = 0.08 (old rate)  vs  H1: θ ~ Beta(2, 18) (mildly informative, prior mean 0.10)

n, y = 12, 9

# Marginal likelihood under H0 (point hypothesis)
log_ml_0 = y * np.log(0.08) + (n - y) * np.log(0.92)
print(f"log p(D | H0) = {log_ml_0:.3f}")

# Marginal likelihood under H1 (composite, with Beta(2,18) prior)
# For Beta-Binomial: p(y | n, α, β) = C(n,y) · B(α+y, β+n-y) / B(α, β)
a1, b1 = 2, 18
from math import lgamma
log_choose = lgamma(n+1) - lgamma(y+1) - lgamma(n-y+1)
log_ml_1 = log_choose + betaln(a1 + y, b1 + n - y) - betaln(a1, b1)
print(f"log p(D | H1) = {log_ml_1:.3f}")

bf_10 = np.exp(log_ml_1 - log_ml_0)
print(f"\nBF_10 = {bf_10:.2f}")

# Jeffreys scale interpretation
if bf_10 > 100: scale = "Decisive"
elif bf_10 > 30: scale = "Very strong"
elif bf_10 > 10: scale = "Strong"
elif bf_10 > 3: scale = "Substantial"
elif bf_10 > 1: scale = "Weak"
else: scale = "Evidence FOR H0"
print(f"Evidence: {scale} for H1 (new rate ≠ 0.08)")

# Posterior odds: BF × prior odds. With equal prior odds = 1:1, posterior P(H1|D) = BF_10/(1+BF_10)
post_p_h1 = bf_10 / (1 + bf_10)
print(f"Assuming equal prior odds, P(H1 | data) = {post_p_h1:.3f}")
