// Section 1 · Sufficient Statistics

Sufficient Statistics — Information Compression Without Loss

A sufficient statistic captures everything in the data relevant to estimating $\theta$. Once you have it, the rest of the data is statistically irrelevant.

Definition — sufficiency
$$T(X) \text{ is sufficient for } \theta \;\;\iff\;\; P(X = x \mid T = t, \theta) \text{ does not depend on } \theta$$
Fisher-Neyman factorization theorem
$$T \text{ is sufficient for } \theta \iff f(x; \theta) = g(T(x); \theta) \cdot h(x)$$

The likelihood factors into a piece depending on $\theta$ only through $T$, times a piece independent of $\theta$. This is the practical tool — you read sufficiency directly off the likelihood.

Kartify · sufficient statistic for the conversion rate
Model. $X_1, \ldots, X_n \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Bernoulli}(\theta)$.
Joint likelihood. $f(x; \theta) = \prod_i \theta^{x_i}(1-\theta)^{1 - x_i} = \theta^{\sum x_i}(1-\theta)^{n - \sum x_i}$.
Factorize. $g(T; \theta) = \theta^T (1-\theta)^{n-T}$ where $T = \sum x_i$. $h(x) = 1$.
Conclusion. $T = \sum X_i$ (the count of conversions) is sufficient for $\theta$. Once you know the total count out of $n$, the specific sequence of which sessions converted carries no additional information about $\theta$. This is the "information compression" — collapsing $n$ bits into one count.
Sufficient ≠ unique
Whole sample is always sufficient
$X = (X_1, \ldots, X_n)$ is trivially sufficient. Useful sufficiency reduces dimension. We seek minimal sufficient statistics — coarsest summary still sufficient.
Exponential family
Always has a low-dimensional sufficient statistic
$f(x; \theta) = h(x)\exp[\eta(\theta)T(x) - A(\theta)]$. The natural statistic $T(x)$ is always sufficient. Bernoulli, Normal, Poisson, Gamma, Beta — all exponential family.
Why we care
Sufficiency enables optimality
Rao-Blackwell theorem (next section) says any estimator can be improved by conditioning on a sufficient statistic. Sufficiency is the key to constructing UMVUEs.
Connecting to Bayesian
Posterior depends on data only via $T$
If $T$ is sufficient, then the posterior $p(\theta \mid X) = p(\theta \mid T)$. Bayesian and frequentist sufficiency coincide. Module 6.1's conjugate updates all happened via sufficient statistics.
// Section 2 · Rao-Blackwell Theorem

Rao-Blackwell Theorem — Conditioning Improves Estimators

Rao-Blackwell theorem
$$\text{Let } W \text{ be unbiased for } \theta, \text{ and } T \text{ sufficient. Define } \tilde W = E[W \mid T].$$
$$\text{Then: } E[\tilde W] = \theta, \quad \mathrm{Var}(\tilde W) \le \mathrm{Var}(W) \text{ for all } \theta.$$
Proof of Rao-Blackwell
1
$\tilde W$ is a valid statistic. Because $T$ is sufficient, the conditional distribution of $X \mid T$ does not depend on $\theta$. So $E[W \mid T]$ depends only on $T$ — it's a statistic, computable without knowing $\theta$. (Without sufficiency, $\tilde W$ would depend on $\theta$ and not be usable as an estimator.)
2
Unbiasedness. By the law of total expectation: $E[\tilde W] = E[E[W \mid T]] = E[W] = \theta$. So $\tilde W$ is unbiased.
3
Variance reduction. By the law of total variance: $\mathrm{Var}(W) = E[\mathrm{Var}(W \mid T)] + \mathrm{Var}(E[W \mid T]) = E[\mathrm{Var}(W \mid T)] + \mathrm{Var}(\tilde W)$.
4
Since $E[\mathrm{Var}(W \mid T)] \ge 0$, we get $\mathrm{Var}(\tilde W) \le \mathrm{Var}(W)$ for all $\theta$. Equality iff $W$ is already a function of $T$. $\blacksquare$
Kartify · Rao-Blackwellizing a clumsy estimator
Setup. $X_1, \ldots, X_n \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Bernoulli}(\theta)$. Want to estimate $\theta$.
Clumsy estimator. $W = X_1$ — just look at the first session. $E[W] = \theta$ (unbiased!), $\mathrm{Var}(W) = \theta(1-\theta)$. Wastes the rest of the data.
Sufficient statistic. $T = \sum X_i$ (Section 1).
Rao-Blackwellize. $\tilde W = E[X_1 \mid T = t] = t/n$. (Each $X_i$ is exchangeable, so by symmetry $E[X_1 \mid T = t] = T/n$.)
Variance. $\mathrm{Var}(\tilde W) = \mathrm{Var}(T/n) = \theta(1-\theta)/n$.
Improvement. Variance dropped from $\theta(1-\theta)$ to $\theta(1-\theta)/n$ — a factor of $n$ smaller. The Rao-Blackwellized estimator is the sample proportion. Conditioning on the sufficient statistic automatically recovered the natural estimator.
// Section 3 · Completeness & Lehmann-Scheffé

Completeness & Lehmann-Scheffé — The UMVUE

Rao-Blackwell improves an unbiased estimator. Are there many UMVUEs, or is there a unique best one? Answer hinges on completeness.

Definition — completeness
$$T \text{ is complete for } \theta \iff [\, E_\theta[g(T)] = 0 \;\forall\theta \;\Longrightarrow\; g(T) = 0 \text{ a.s.} \,]$$

"The only function of $T$ with zero mean for all $\theta$ is the zero function." Equivalently: there's no non-trivial unbiased estimator of zero based on $T$. This rules out multiple distinct UMVUEs.

Lehmann-Scheffé theorem
$$\text{If } T \text{ is complete and sufficient, and } \tilde W = h(T) \text{ is unbiased for } \theta,$$
$$\text{then } \tilde W \text{ is the unique UMVUE.}$$
Proof sketch of Lehmann-Scheffé
1
Let $W$ be any unbiased estimator. By Rao-Blackwell, $\tilde W = E[W \mid T]$ is unbiased and has variance $\le \mathrm{Var}(W)$.
2
Suppose $W_1, W_2$ are both unbiased estimators that are functions of $T$ (so already Rao-Blackwellized). Define $g(T) = W_1 - W_2$.
3
$E[g(T)] = E[W_1] - E[W_2] = \theta - \theta = 0$ for all $\theta$.
4
By completeness, $g(T) = 0$ a.s., so $W_1 = W_2$ a.s. Thus the unbiased estimator that is a function of $T$ is unique. By Rao-Blackwell, it has minimum variance among all unbiased estimators. $\blacksquare$
Kartify · UMVUE for conversion rate
Setup. Bernoulli model. $T = \sum X_i$ is sufficient (Section 1). $T \sim \text{Binomial}(n, \theta)$.
Completeness. Suppose $E[g(T)] = \sum_{t=0}^n g(t) \binom{n}{t}\theta^t(1-\theta)^{n-t} = 0$ for all $\theta \in (0, 1)$. This is a polynomial in $\theta$ of degree $n$, identically zero $\Rightarrow$ all coefficients $g(t)\binom{n}{t} = 0 \Rightarrow g(t) = 0$ for all $t$. So $T$ is complete.
Unbiased function of $T$. $\tilde W = T/n$. $E[T/n] = n\theta/n = \theta$. ✓
Conclusion. By Lehmann-Scheffé, $\hat\theta = T/n = k/n$ is the unique UMVUE for $\theta$ in the Bernoulli model. Sample proportion isn't just a natural choice — it's provably optimal among unbiased estimators.
⚡ Why exponential families are special

For exponential families $f(x; \theta) = h(x)\exp[\eta(\theta) T(x) - A(\theta)]$, the natural statistic $T(x)$ is automatically complete and sufficient (under mild regularity conditions). So Lehmann-Scheffé applies directly: any unbiased function of $T$ is the UMVUE. This is why for Normal data $\bar X$ is UMVUE for $\mu$, for Poisson data $\bar X$ is UMVUE for $\lambda$, and so on. The proof reduces to a uniqueness-of-Laplace-transform argument. Outside exponential families, completeness is much harder to verify — and UMVUEs may not exist.

// Section 4 · Cramér-Rao Lower Bound

Cramér-Rao Lower Bound — The Fundamental Limit on Precision

Module 5.4 stated the CRLB; here we prove it.

Cramér-Rao Lower Bound
$$\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) \ge \frac{1}{n I(\theta)} \quad \text{for any unbiased estimator } \hat\theta$$
$$\text{where } I(\theta) = E\!\left[\left(\frac{\partial \log f(X; \theta)}{\partial \theta}\right)^2\right] \text{ is Fisher information.}$$
Proof of CRLB via Cauchy-Schwarz
1
Score has mean zero. $E[U(\theta)] = E[\partial \log f/\partial\theta] = 0$ (Module 5.4 information identity).
2
Score has variance $nI(\theta)$. For iid data, the joint log-likelihood is $\ell_n = \sum_{i=1}^n \log f(X_i; \theta)$, so $U_n = \sum_i U_i$ and $\mathrm{Var}(U_n) = nI(\theta)$.
3
Differentiate unbiasedness. $E[\hat\theta] = \theta \Rightarrow \int \hat\theta(x) f(x; \theta) dx = \theta$. Differentiate w.r.t. $\theta$ (swap derivative/integral): $\int \hat\theta(x) \cdot \partial f/\partial\theta \, dx = 1$.
4
Rewrite $\partial f/\partial\theta = f \cdot \partial \log f/\partial\theta = f \cdot U$. So $E[\hat\theta \cdot U] = 1$.
5
Compute covariance. $\mathrm{Cov}(\hat\theta, U) = E[\hat\theta U] - E[\hat\theta]E[U] = 1 - \theta \cdot 0 = 1$.
6
Apply Cauchy-Schwarz. $|\mathrm{Cov}(\hat\theta, U)|^2 \le \mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) \cdot \mathrm{Var}(U)$, i.e. $1 \le \mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) \cdot nI(\theta)$. Therefore $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) \ge 1/(nI(\theta))$. $\blacksquare$
Equality condition
$\hat\theta$ achieves CRLB iff exponential family + linear in $T$
Cauchy-Schwarz equality requires $\hat\theta - \theta = c(\theta) U(\theta)$ for some $c$. This forces an exponential family form. UMVUE $\ne$ CRLB-achieving in general.
Multi-parameter version
Matrix CRLB
For $\boldsymbol\theta \in \mathbb R^p$: $\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}) \succeq [n\mathbf I(\boldsymbol\theta)]^{-1}$ in matrix ordering. Each diagonal entry bounds the marginal variance — but joint estimation can be tighter.
MLE achieves CRLB asymptotically
Asymptotic efficiency
$\sqrt n(\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} - \theta) \to N(0, 1/I(\theta))$ as $n \to \infty$. So MLE is asymptotically the best possible unbiased estimator. This is why MLE is the workhorse of statistical inference.
Biased estimators can beat CRLB
CRLB is for UNBIASED estimators
A biased estimator (e.g., shrinkage like Stein) can have smaller MSE than the CRLB — by trading bias for variance. CRLB is the lower bound only within the unbiased class.
Fisher information geometrically — curvature of the log-likelihood
High Fisher info = sharp peak = small CRLB = precise estimation. Low Fisher info = flat peak = large CRLB = noisy estimation.
log-likelihood $\ell(\theta)$true $\theta$CRLB band ($\hat\theta \pm \sqrt{\text{CRLB}}$)
// Section 5 · Fisher Information Geometric

Fisher Information — A Geometric View

Fisher information has a beautiful geometric interpretation: it measures the curvature of the log-likelihood surface. Sharp curvature = the data strongly distinguishes nearby parameter values = small CRLB = precise estimates.

Three equivalent views of Fisher information
$$I(\theta) = E\!\left[\left(\frac{\partial \log f}{\partial \theta}\right)^2\right] \;\;\text{(variance of score)}$$
$$I(\theta) = -E\!\left[\frac{\partial^2 \log f}{\partial \theta^2}\right] \;\;\text{(expected curvature)}$$
$$I(\theta) = \int \frac{(\partial f/\partial\theta)^2}{f}\, dx \;\;\text{(KL divergence localization)}$$
⚡ Fisher info as KL divergence's second derivative

For nearby $\theta_1, \theta_2$, the Kullback-Leibler divergence behaves as $\mathrm{KL}(f_{\theta_1} \| f_{\theta_2}) \approx \tfrac{1}{2} I(\theta_1)(\theta_2 - \theta_1)^2 + O((\theta_2 - \theta_1)^3)$. Fisher information is the local quadratic approximation of KL divergence — the natural Riemannian metric on the parameter space (information geometry). High Fisher info means the model's distributions are "spread far apart" near $\theta$ in KL distance — easy to distinguish. This is the geometric picture behind why high $I(\theta)$ enables precise estimation.

Kartify · Fisher info for Bernoulli, Normal, Poisson
Bernoulli($\theta$). $\log f = x\log\theta + (1-x)\log(1-\theta)$. $\partial^2/\partial\theta^2 = -x/\theta^2 - (1-x)/(1-\theta)^2$. $I(\theta) = -E[\ell''] = 1/[\theta(1-\theta)]$. Maximum at boundaries (near 0 or 1), minimum at $\theta = 0.5$.
Normal($\mu, \sigma^2$) for $\mu$. $\log f = -\tfrac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - (x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)$. $\partial^2/\partial\mu^2 = -1/\sigma^2$. $I(\mu) = 1/\sigma^2$. Higher noise $\sigma^2$ → lower information → wider CRLB.
Poisson($\lambda$). $\log f = x\log\lambda - \lambda - \log(x!)$. $\partial^2/\partial\lambda^2 = -x/\lambda^2$. $I(\lambda) = E[X]/\lambda^2 = 1/\lambda$. Smaller $\lambda$ → higher information per observation.
Pattern. Fisher info is dimensionless — units of $\theta^{-2}$ — and inversely proportional to inherent "noisiness" of the parameter. CRLB $= 1/(nI)$ shrinks with $n$, expressing the universal $1/\sqrt n$ rate of estimation.
// Section 6 · Neyman-Pearson Lemma

Neyman-Pearson Lemma — The Most Powerful Test

The frequentist hypothesis testing analog of the CRLB. For a simple null vs simple alternative, the likelihood ratio test is provably the most powerful test of any size $\alpha$.

Setup — simple vs simple
$$H_0: \theta = \theta_0 \quad \text{vs} \quad H_1: \theta = \theta_1$$
$$\text{Test } \phi: \text{ reject } H_0 \text{ if } \phi(X) = 1, \text{ keep if } \phi(X) = 0$$
$$\text{Size } \alpha = E_{\theta_0}[\phi(X)], \quad \text{Power } \beta(\theta_1) = E_{\theta_1}[\phi(X)]$$
Neyman-Pearson lemma
$$\text{The test } \phi^*(x) = \mathbb{1}\!\left[\frac{f(x; \theta_1)}{f(x; \theta_0)} > k\right]$$
$$\text{(with } k \text{ chosen so that } E_{\theta_0}[\phi^*] = \alpha\text{)}$$
$$\text{is the most powerful test of size } \alpha.$$
Proof of Neyman-Pearson lemma
1
Let $\phi^*$ be the LRT and $\phi$ any other test of size $\le \alpha$. Define the difference $\phi^*(x) - \phi(x)$.
2
Sign-rule observation. When $\phi^*(x) = 1$ (LRT rejects), $f_1(x)/f_0(x) > k$, so $f_1(x) - kf_0(x) > 0$. When $\phi^*(x) = 0$, $f_1(x) - kf_0(x) \le 0$. In both cases: $[\phi^*(x) - \phi(x)] \cdot [f_1(x) - kf_0(x)] \ge 0$.
3
Why? If $\phi^* = 1$, $\phi^* - \phi \ge 0$ (since $\phi \le 1$) and $f_1 - kf_0 > 0$ → product $\ge 0$. If $\phi^* = 0$, $\phi^* - \phi \le 0$ and $f_1 - kf_0 \le 0$ → product $\ge 0$.
4
Integrate. $\int (\phi^* - \phi)(f_1 - kf_0) dx \ge 0$, i.e., $E_{\theta_1}[\phi^* - \phi] - k \cdot E_{\theta_0}[\phi^* - \phi] \ge 0$.
5
Rearrange: $E_{\theta_1}[\phi^*] - E_{\theta_1}[\phi] \ge k(E_{\theta_0}[\phi^*] - E_{\theta_0}[\phi]) = k(\alpha - E_{\theta_0}[\phi]) \ge 0$ (since $\phi$ has size $\le \alpha$).
6
Therefore $E_{\theta_1}[\phi^*] \ge E_{\theta_1}[\phi]$ — LRT power $\ge$ any other test's power. $\blacksquare$
Kartify · Neyman-Pearson optimal fraud detector
Setup. Each session has feature $X$ (e.g., transaction amount). Under $H_0$ (legitimate): $X \sim N(0, 1)$. Under $H_1$ (fraud): $X \sim N(2, 1)$. Detect fraud at false-positive rate $\alpha = 0.05$.
Likelihood ratio. $f_1(x)/f_0(x) = \exp[2x - 2]$. Reject if $> k \iff x > c$ where $c = (\log k)/2 + 1$.
Choose $c$ for size $\alpha = 0.05$. $P_{H_0}(X > c) = 1 - \Phi(c) = 0.05 \Rightarrow c = 1.645$.
Power. $P_{H_1}(X > 1.645) = 1 - \Phi(1.645 - 2) = 1 - \Phi(-0.355) = 0.639$. So Kartify catches 63.9% of fraud at 5% false-positive rate.
Optimality. By Neyman-Pearson, no other test with FPR $\le 5\%$ can detect more fraud. The threshold rule is optimal — not just convenient. Try a clever rule like "reject if $X^2 > c$" → it has lower power. The likelihood ratio is the right statistic.
// Section 7 · UMP Tests

Uniformly Most Powerful Tests — Beyond Simple Hypotheses

Real hypotheses are usually composite (e.g., $H_1: \theta > \theta_0$, not $H_1: \theta = \theta_1$). Neyman-Pearson gives a unique LRT for each $\theta_1$ — but does one test work for all $\theta_1$?

Definition — UMP test
$$\phi \text{ is UMP at level } \alpha \iff E_{\theta_0}[\phi] \le \alpha \text{ and } \forall \theta_1 \in H_1, \forall \phi' \text{ with size} \le \alpha:\; E_{\theta_1}[\phi] \ge E_{\theta_1}[\phi']$$
Karlin-Rubin theorem (sufficient condition for UMP)
$$\text{If the family has } \textbf{monotone likelihood ratio (MLR) in } T(X)\text{, then for } H_1: \theta > \theta_0,$$
$$\text{the test } \phi(x) = \mathbb{1}[T(x) > c] \text{ (with appropriate } c\text{) is UMP at level } \alpha.$$
MLR property
$L(\theta_1)/L(\theta_0)$ monotone in $T(x)$ for $\theta_1 > \theta_0$
All exponential families with natural parameter $\eta(\theta)$ monotone in $\theta$ have MLR. Includes Bernoulli, Poisson, Normal-with-known-σ, exponential, Gamma — most common parametric families.
When UMP exists
One-sided alternatives, MLR family
$H_0: \theta \le \theta_0$ vs $H_1: \theta > \theta_0$ in an MLR family — UMP exists. The "rejection if $T > c$" rule is uniformly best.
When UMP does NOT exist
Two-sided alternatives
For $H_0: \theta = \theta_0$ vs $H_1: \theta \ne \theta_0$, the optimal test for $\theta > \theta_0$ rejects on the right; the optimal for $\theta < \theta_0$ rejects on the left. No single test is UMP for both. Restricted optimality (UMPU — UMP among unbiased) is the salvage.
Why this matters
Justifies all one-sided $z$ and $t$-tests
When you do a one-sided $z$-test for $H_1: \mu > \mu_0$, you're using the UMP test (Normal-with-known-σ has MLR in $\bar X$). For two-sided, the LRT $|\bar X - \mu_0|$-rule is UMPU, the best you can do.
// Section 8 · Generalized LRT

Generalized Likelihood Ratio Tests — Composite vs Composite

For arbitrary composite hypotheses (e.g., regression with multiple parameters), no UMP test usually exists. The generalized LRT is the practical workhorse — asymptotically optimal under mild conditions.

Generalized LRT
$$\Lambda = \frac{\sup_{\theta \in \Theta_0} L(\theta)}{\sup_{\theta \in \Theta} L(\theta)} \in [0, 1]$$
$$-2 \log \Lambda \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \chi^2_k \quad \text{under } H_0 \text{ (Wilks's theorem)}$$

Where $k$ = dimension of $\Theta$ minus dimension of $\Theta_0$. Module 5.4 derived this; here we recognize it as the natural generalization of Neyman-Pearson to composite hypotheses, asymptotically the most powerful test of size $\alpha$ in a wide class.

⚡ The grand unification

The trinity of tests — LRT, Wald, Score (Module 5.4) — are all asymptotically equivalent and asymptotically achieve the optimal test in the Neyman-Pearson sense for "regular" models. So when you do an $F$-test in regression, a $\chi^2$ test for independence, or a likelihood ratio test for nested GLMs, you're implicitly using an asymptotically optimal procedure — Neyman-Pearson lemma extended via Wilks's theorem. This is the deep structural reason why these tests "work": they're not arbitrary conventions, but the asymptotically optimal solution to a well-defined optimization problem.

Python — Optimal Theory in Practice

python · CRLB demonstration — MLE achieves the bound
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

np.random.seed(42)

# ─── Bernoulli: verify MLE achieves CRLB ───
# True parameter
theta_true = 0.3
n_values = [10, 50, 100, 500, 2000]
n_simulations = 5000

print(f"{'n':>6} {'Var(MLE)':>12} {'CRLB':>12} {'Ratio':>8}")
for n in n_values:
    # Simulate many independent samples, compute MLE for each
    samples = np.random.binomial(n, theta_true, size=n_simulations)
    mles = samples / n
    var_mle = mles.var(ddof=1)

    # CRLB: 1 / (n * Fisher info) for Bernoulli
    # I(theta) = 1 / (theta * (1 - theta))
    fisher_info = 1 / (theta_true * (1 - theta_true))
    crlb = 1 / (n * fisher_info)

    ratio = var_mle / crlb
    print(f"{n:>6} {var_mle:>12.6f} {crlb:>12.6f} {ratio:>8.3f}")

# Ratio should be very close to 1.0 for Bernoulli (MLE achieves CRLB exactly)
# In larger samples, ratio is more precise estimate
python · Rao-Blackwellization in action
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(42)
n = 100
theta = 0.3
n_sims = 10000

# Naive estimator: just X_1 (first observation)
# Rao-Blackwellized: T/n where T = sum X_i

naive_var, rb_var = 0, 0

naive_estimates = np.empty(n_sims)
rb_estimates = np.empty(n_sims)

for sim in range(n_sims):
    X = np.random.binomial(1, theta, n)
    naive_estimates[sim] = X[0]   # Just the first observation (unbiased!)
    rb_estimates[sim] = X.mean()  # Sample proportion = E[X_1 | T]

print(f"Naive estimator (X_1):")
print(f"  E[W] = {naive_estimates.mean():.4f} (true: {theta})")
print(f"  Var(W) = {naive_estimates.var(ddof=1):.5f} (theory: {theta*(1-theta):.5f})")

print(f"\nRao-Blackwellized (T/n):")
print(f"  E[W~] = {rb_estimates.mean():.4f} (true: {theta})")
print(f"  Var(W~) = {rb_estimates.var(ddof=1):.5f} (theory: {theta*(1-theta)/n:.5f})")

print(f"\nVariance reduction factor: {naive_estimates.var(ddof=1) / rb_estimates.var(ddof=1):.1f}x")
# Theory predicts factor of n = 100. Result: ~100x. Rao-Blackwell works!
python · Neyman-Pearson optimal fraud detector with ROC analysis
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats import norm

# Kartify fraud detection
# H0: legitimate, X ~ N(0, 1). H1: fraud, X ~ N(2, 1)
mu_0, mu_1, sigma = 0, 2, 1

# NP-optimal test: reject if x > c (since LR is monotone in x)
# Find c for desired size alpha
alpha = 0.05
c_optimal = norm.ppf(1 - alpha, mu_0, sigma)
power_optimal = 1 - norm.cdf(c_optimal, mu_1, sigma)

print(f"NP-optimal threshold: c = {c_optimal:.3f}")
print(f"Power at alpha = {alpha}: {power_optimal:.3f}")
print(f"Catches {power_optimal*100:.1f}% of fraud at {alpha*100}% false positive rate")

# Compare to a SUBOPTIMAL alternative test: reject if x^2 > c'
# (rejects on both tails — catches large negative X too which isn't fraud)

# For X ~ N(0,1), X^2 ~ chi^2_1; for size alpha need P(X^2 > c') = alpha
from scipy.stats import chi2
c_squared_threshold = chi2.ppf(1 - alpha, df=1)

# Power: P_{H1}(X^2 > c') = 1 - P(|X| <= sqrt(c'))
sqrt_c = np.sqrt(c_squared_threshold)
power_suboptimal = (norm.cdf(-sqrt_c, mu_1, sigma) +
                    1 - norm.cdf(sqrt_c, mu_1, sigma))

print(f"\nSuboptimal test (reject if x^2 > {c_squared_threshold:.3f}):")
print(f"Power at alpha = {alpha}: {power_suboptimal:.3f}")
print(f"NP-optimal is {power_optimal/power_suboptimal:.2f}x more powerful")

# Plot: ROC curves for both tests
alphas = np.linspace(0.001, 0.5, 100)
c_opt = norm.ppf(1 - alphas, mu_0, sigma)
powers_opt = 1 - norm.cdf(c_opt, mu_1, sigma)

c_sq = chi2.ppf(1 - alphas, df=1)
sqrt_cs = np.sqrt(c_sq)
powers_sq = (norm.cdf(-sqrt_cs, mu_1, sigma) +
             1 - norm.cdf(sqrt_cs, mu_1, sigma))

Q 01 / 10
$T = \sum X_i$ is sufficient for $\theta$ in Bernoulli($\theta$). What does sufficiency mean here?
A$T$ is the best estimator
BThe conditional distribution of $X = (X_1, \ldots, X_n)$ given $T$ does not depend on $\theta$. Once you know the count $T$, the specific sequence carries no additional information about $\theta$. Equivalently: the likelihood factors as $g(T; \theta) h(X)$ (Fisher-Neyman).
C$T$ is unbiased
D$T$ has minimum variance
Q 02 / 10
Rao-Blackwell takes a clumsy unbiased estimator $W = X_1$ and produces $\tilde W = E[W \mid T]$. What's $\tilde W$ for Bernoulli?
A$X_1$ unchanged
B$T/n$ — the sample proportion. By exchangeability of iid Bernoulli draws, $E[X_1 \mid T = t] = t/n$. Variance drops from $\theta(1-\theta)$ to $\theta(1-\theta)/n$ — a factor of $n$ improvement. Conditioning on the sufficient statistic recovers the natural estimator automatically.
C$X_n$
D$\theta$
Q 03 / 10
Lehmann-Scheffé requires $T$ to be both sufficient AND complete. What does completeness rule out?
ABiased estimators
BMultiple distinct UMVUEs. Completeness: only the zero function has $E_\theta[g(T)] = 0$ for all $\theta$. So if two unbiased estimators $W_1, W_2$ are functions of $T$, then $W_1 - W_2 = g(T)$ has zero mean for all $\theta$, forcing $W_1 = W_2$. Without completeness, multiple unbiased estimators based on $T$ could exist.
CSufficient statistics
DBiased estimators of zero
Q 04 / 10
CRLB: $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) \ge 1/(nI(\theta))$. The proof uses which key inequality?
AMarkov's inequality
BCauchy-Schwarz: $|\mathrm{Cov}(\hat\theta, U)|^2 \le \mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) \mathrm{Var}(U)$. After showing $\mathrm{Cov}(\hat\theta, U) = 1$ (from differentiating unbiasedness) and $\mathrm{Var}(U) = nI(\theta)$, Cauchy-Schwarz gives $1 \le \mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) \cdot nI(\theta)$, i.e., $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) \ge 1/(nI(\theta))$.
CJensen's inequality
DChebyshev's inequality
Q 05 / 10
A biased estimator (e.g., shrinkage) has MSE BELOW the CRLB. Contradiction?
AYes — the CRLB must be wrong
BNo. CRLB bounds the variance of UNBIASED estimators. Biased estimators trade bias for variance (bias² + variance = MSE), so smaller variance is achievable at the cost of bias. James-Stein shrinkage is a famous example: dominates the MLE in $\ge 3$ dimensions for Normal mean estimation, despite being biased.
CYes — Fisher info is wrong
DOnly true asymptotically
Q 06 / 10
Fisher information has three equivalent forms. What's the geometric one?
AVariance of the score
BLocal quadratic approximation of KL divergence: $\mathrm{KL}(f_{\theta_1} \| f_{\theta_2}) \approx \tfrac{1}{2}I(\theta_1)(\theta_2 - \theta_1)^2$. Fisher info IS the natural Riemannian metric on parameter space (information geometry). High info = distributions far apart in KL near $\theta$ = easy to distinguish from data.
CExpected curvature $-E[\ell'']$
DScore squared
Q 07 / 10
Neyman-Pearson lemma. Kartify chooses test threshold $c$ for fraud detection. Which test maximizes power at FPR = 5%?
AReject if $|X| > c$
BReject if $X > c$ (since the likelihood ratio $f_1/f_0 = \exp(2x - 2)$ is monotone increasing in $x$). NP lemma: optimal test rejects when likelihood ratio exceeds a threshold. For monotone LR in $x$, this becomes a one-sided threshold rule on $x$. Two-sided would catch large negatives that aren't fraud — wastes alpha.
CReject if $X^2 > c$
DReject randomly with probability $\alpha$
Q 08 / 10
UMP tests don't exist for two-sided alternatives. Why?
AComputational difficulty
BThe optimal test for $\theta > \theta_0$ rejects on the right; the optimal for $\theta < \theta_0$ rejects on the left. No single test is most powerful against both directions simultaneously. Salvage: UMPU (uniformly most powerful among unbiased tests) — usually the two-sided LRT.
CMLR property fails
DTwo-sided is too lenient
Q 09 / 10
Kartify's MLE for Bernoulli conversion rate is $\hat\theta = k/n$. Which optimality property does it have?

BUMVUE — uniformly minimum variance among unbiased estimators. By Lehmann-Scheffé: $T = \sum X_i$ is complete-sufficient (Bernoulli is exponential family), and $T/n$ is unbiased function of $T$. ALSO achieves the CRLB (variance = $\theta(1-\theta)/n = 1/(nI(\theta))$). NOT minimum MSE — biased shrinkage estimators can have smaller MSE.

Generalized LRT $\Lambda = \sup_{\Theta_0} L / \sup_\Theta L$. What asymptotic property guarantees its optimality?
A$\Lambda \to 0$

Combined with the Neyman-Pearson framework, this means generalized LRTs are asymptotically the most powerful tests of size $\alpha$ in regular parametric models. The trinity of LRT/Wald/Score tests are asymptotically equivalent realizations of this optimal procedure.
