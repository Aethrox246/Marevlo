⚡ Kartify — The Fraud Flag
Tuesday morning. Priya is still thinking about Delhi when a new alert fires. Kartify Pay's fraud model flagged 73 transactions overnight as "high-risk." The model's a good one — it catches 95% of actual fraud, and its false-positive rate is only 2%. Rohan sees the alert and says: "Block all 73. It's a 95% accurate model."

Arjun stops him. "Before we block them, let me ask a different question. Of those 73 flagged orders, how many are actually fraud?" Rohan shrugs: "95% of them — that's what the model accuracy says." Arjun shakes his head: "No. Without the base rate of fraud, you can't say that. You're confusing $P(\text{flagged} \mid \text{fraud})$ with $P(\text{fraud} \mid \text{flagged})$ — and those two are wildly different numbers."

By the end of this module, you'll compute the correct answer yourself — and it will surprise you. The right number is closer to 30%, not 95%. The gap between those two numbers is where half the real-world ML mistakes live. The math that fixes this is called Bayes' theorem.
// Section 1

Conditional Probability

The core shift: probability is context-dependent. $P(\text{return})$ across all Kartify orders is 8%. But $P(\text{return} \mid \text{order was late})$ is different — maybe 23%. Knowing something about the order changes the probability. Conditional probability formalizes this.

Definition — Conditional Probability (Evans & Rosenthal §1.5)
$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \qquad \text{provided } P(B) > 0$$
$P(A \mid B)$
Probability of $A$ given that $B$ happened
$P(A \cap B)$
Probability both $A$ and $B$ happen
$P(B)$
Probability of the "given" event
Why this formula — the restricted-universe argument
When you condition on $B$, you're saying: "forget the rest of the sample space — my universe is now just $B$." Inside that restricted universe, the probability of $A$ is the fraction of $B$-outcomes that are also in $A$. That's literally $P(A \cap B)$ (the overlap) divided by $P(B)$ (the new universe's total weight). Conditioning rescales probability to a new sample space. This single mental model makes every formula below obvious.

Kartify — late delivery and returns

From Module 2.1: $P(L) = 0.15$, $P(R) = 0.08$, $P(L \cap R) = 0.035$. Let's compute both conditional directions.

Worked example · $P(R \mid L)$ — given late, probability of return
Question. An order is late. What's the probability it gets returned?
Formula. $P(R \mid L) = \dfrac{P(R \cap L)}{P(L)} = \dfrac{0.035}{0.15} \approx 0.233$
Answer. Given an order was late, there's a 23.3% chance it gets returned — almost 3× the base rate of 8%. Late delivery is a strong signal.
Worked example · $P(L \mid R)$ — given return, probability of late
Question. An order was returned. What's the probability it was late?
Formula. $P(L \mid R) = \dfrac{P(L \cap R)}{P(R)} = \dfrac{0.035}{0.08} = 0.4375$
Answer. 43.75% of returned orders were late. Very different from $P(R \mid L) = 23.3\%$. The asymmetry matters — which we unpack next.
⚠ Warning — $P(A \mid B) \ne P(B \mid A)$

This is the #1 source of probabilistic confusion in business and ML. $P(R \mid L) = 23\%$ and $P(L \mid R) = 44\%$ describe the same two events but from opposite angles. Confusing them is the prosecutor's fallacy, the base-rate fallacy, and what Arjun just saved Rohan from. Bayes' theorem (Section 5) is literally the equation that lets you safely flip between these two.

Multiplication & Chain Rule

Rearrange the conditional-probability definition and you get the multiplication rule — a way to compute joint probabilities from conditionals.

Multiplication Rule — two forms of the same equation
$$P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\, P(B) = P(B \mid A)\, P(A)$$
Derivation — from the definition
1
Start: $P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)$ Definition of conditional probability.
2
Multiply both sides by $P(B)$: $P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\, P(B)$. Algebra — that's the first form.
3
By symmetry (swap $A \leftrightarrow B$): $P(A \cap B) = P(B \mid A)\, P(A)$. Second form. Both must be equal — this equality is the seed of Bayes.

Chain rule — generalizing to three or more events

Chain Rule (General)
$$P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \ldots \cap A_n) = P(A_1)\, P(A_2 \mid A_1)\, P(A_3 \mid A_1 \cap A_2)\, \cdots\, P(A_n \mid A_1 \cap \ldots \cap A_{n-1})$$

The chain rule lets you decompose any joint probability into a product of conditionals. It's the backbone of sequence models in ML — language models literally compute $P(w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n) = \prod_i P(w_i \mid w_1, \ldots, w_{i-1})$.

Kartify · chain rule — three sequential events
Setup. A customer visits Kartify ($V$), adds to cart ($C$), and completes checkout ($X$). We want $P(V \cap C \cap X)$ — the probability a random visitor actually buys.
Funnel metrics (historical). $P(V) = 1$ (define our sample space as visitors). $P(C \mid V) = 0.18$. $P(X \mid V \cap C) = 0.42$.
Chain rule. $P(V \cap C \cap X) = P(V) \cdot P(C \mid V) \cdot P(X \mid V \cap C) = 1 \times 0.18 \times 0.42$.
Answer. $= 0.0756$ — 7.56% of visitors complete checkout. This is Kartify's funnel conversion rate, derived from individual step probabilities. Every funnel in every business works this way.

Law of Total Probability

Sometimes you don't know $P(A)$ directly, but you do know it conditional on different scenarios. The Law of Total Probability (LTP) stitches those conditionals back into the unconditional probability.

Law of Total Probability — partition form
$$P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_i)\, P(B_i) \quad \text{where } \{B_1, \ldots, B_n\} \text{ partition } S$$
partition
$B_i$ disjoint, $\bigcup B_i = S$
$P(A \mid B_i)$
conditional prob in each branch
$P(B_i)$
weight of each branch
Proof — from axioms + multiplication rule
1
Since $\{B_i\}$ partitions $S$: $A = A \cap S = A \cap (B_1 \cup B_2 \cup \ldots \cup B_n)$. Intersect $A$ with the whole sample space, which equals the union of the partition.
2
Distribute: $A = (A \cap B_1) \cup (A \cap B_2) \cup \ldots \cup (A \cap B_n)$. Standard set-theoretic distributivity.
3
The pieces $(A \cap B_i)$ are disjoint — because the $B_i$ are disjoint. Two disjoint subsets can only intersect $A$ in disjoint regions.
4
By Axiom A3: $P(A) = \sum_i P(A \cap B_i)$. Countable additivity on disjoint pieces.
5
By multiplication rule: $P(A \cap B_i) = P(A \mid B_i) P(B_i)$. Substituting.
6
$\therefore P(A) = \sum_i P(A \mid B_i)\, P(B_i) \quad \blacksquare$

How to think about LTP — the "scenario tree" lens
Whenever you see a question with "depends on which case we're in," you're looking at LTP. Each case is a branch; each branch contributes $P(A \mid \text{branch}) \times P(\text{branch})$. Fraud rates depend on payment method? LTP. Return rates depend on city? LTP. Click rates depend on device type? LTP. The pattern: "I know the conditional probabilities, I need the unconditional — sum them weighted by branch probability."

Bayes' Theorem

This is the most important equation in statistical machine learning. It's four symbols and a divide bar. Nothing you learn in this track will come up more often than Bayes.

Bayes' Theorem — inverting the conditional
$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\, P(A)}{P(B)}$$
Derivation — from the multiplication rule, in two lines
1
Multiplication rule (both forms): $P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) P(B)$ and also $P(A \cap B) = P(B \mid A) P(A)$.
2
Equate: $P(A \mid B) P(B) = P(B \mid A) P(A)$. Same joint, two factorisations.
3
Divide both sides by $P(B)$: $P(A \mid B) = P(B \mid A) P(A) / P(B)$. That's Bayes' theorem. That's all there is to it.
4
When $P(B)$ isn't directly known, use LTP to compute it: $P(B) = \sum_i P(B \mid A_i) P(A_i)$ for a partition $\{A_i\}$. This gives the expanded Bayes' form: $$P(A_k \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A_k) P(A_k)}{\sum_i P(B \mid A_i) P(A_i)}$$
The vocabulary you'll see in every ML paper
$$\underbrace{P(A \mid B)}_{\text{posterior}} \;=\; \frac{\overbrace{P(B \mid A)}^{\text{likelihood}} \cdot \overbrace{P(A)}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{P(B)}_{\text{evidence}}}$$
Prior
What you believed about $A$ before seeing $B$
Likelihood
How well $A$ explains the observation $B$
Posterior
Your updated belief about $A$ after observing $B$
Evidence
Total probability of $B$ — normaliser
How to think about Bayes — the belief-update lens
Bayes is a belief update machine. You walk in with a prior — what you believed before. Evidence arrives ($B$ happened). Bayes tells you exactly how much to shift your belief: multiply prior by likelihood, divide by evidence. That's the entire loop of rational learning. Bayesian ML, Kalman filters, spam classifiers, recommender systems, medical diagnostic AI — all just Bayes, applied over and over.

Bayes Applied — Kartify Fraud Detection

Back to Arjun's argument with Rohan. Kartify Pay processes millions of transactions. The fraud model has:

  • Sensitivity (true-positive rate): $P(\text{flagged} \mid \text{fraud}) = 0.95$ — catches 95% of fraud
  • False-positive rate: $P(\text{flagged} \mid \text{not fraud}) = 0.02$ — 2% of clean transactions are incorrectly flagged
  • Base rate: $P(\text{fraud}) = 0.01$ — 1% of all transactions are actually fraudulent (industry average for Indian e-commerce)

Rohan's question: "What's the probability a flagged transaction is actually fraud?" In symbols: $P(F \mid \text{flag})$. Arjun's claim is that this is not 0.95. Let's prove it.

Kartify · Bayes — the fraud calculation
Step 1. Write Bayes with the partition $\{F, F^c\}$ for the denominator (LTP): $P(F \mid \text{flag}) = \dfrac{P(\text{flag} \mid F)\, P(F)}{P(\text{flag} \mid F)\, P(F) + P(\text{flag} \mid F^c)\, P(F^c)}$
Step 2. Plug in: $P(\text{flag}\mid F) = 0.95$, $P(F) = 0.01$, $P(\text{flag}\mid F^c) = 0.02$, $P(F^c) = 0.99$.
Step 3. Numerator: $0.95 \times 0.01 = 0.0095$.
Step 4. Denominator: $0.0095 + 0.02 \times 0.99 = 0.0095 + 0.0198 = 0.0293$.
Step 5. Divide: $P(F \mid \text{flag}) = 0.0095 / 0.0293 \approx 0.324$.
Answer. Only 32.4% of flagged transactions are actually fraud. Rohan's instinct (95%) was off by a factor of 3×. Arjun was right.
⚠ Base Rate Fallacy — why Rohan was wrong

The intuition that fails: "model is 95% accurate → flagged transactions are 95% likely to be fraud." This confuses $P(\text{flag} \mid F)$ (sensitivity) with $P(F \mid \text{flag})$ (positive predictive value).

Why it fails: when fraud is rare (1%), even a small false-positive rate (2%) overwhelms the true-positive rate because there are so many more clean transactions. Out of 10,000 transactions: ~100 are fraud (95 correctly flagged), and ~9,900 are clean (198 incorrectly flagged). So out of 293 flags, only 95 are real fraud — 32.4%.

Kartify decision: don't auto-block flagged transactions. Route them to manual review or step-up authentication. 2 out of every 3 would be false positives — blocking them directly would kill customer trust.

The same model — same sensitivity, same false-positive rate — can be 84% accurate on flags when fraud is common, and under 1% accurate when fraud is rare. The model didn't change; the base rate did. Independence — When Knowing B Tells You Nothing About A

Two events are independent when conditioning on one doesn't change the probability of the other. Formally: $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$. Equivalently (from the multiplication rule):

Definition — Independence (Evans & Rosenthal §1.5.2)
$$A \perp B \iff P(A \cap B) = P(A)\, P(B)$$
Why this definition is equivalent to "knowing B tells nothing about A"
1
Suppose $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$ (the intuitive form of independence). "Knowing $B$ doesn't update probability of $A$."
2
Multiplication rule: $P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) P(B)$. Substitute: $P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B)$.
3
Conversely, if $P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B)$: dividing by $P(B)$ gives $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$. The two definitions are equivalent.

Kartify — testing for independence

Are "late delivery" and "returned" independent at Kartify?
Data. $P(L) = 0.15$, $P(R) = 0.08$, $P(L \cap R) = 0.035$.
Independence check. If independent, we'd need $P(L \cap R) = P(L) \cdot P(R) = 0.15 \times 0.08 = 0.012$.
Observed. $P(L \cap R) = 0.035$, which is $\approx 2.9\times$ what independence predicts.
Answer. Not independent. Late deliveries and returns are strongly correlated — late orders are ~3× more likely to be returned than independence would predict. Priya should treat logistics as a cause of returns, not an orthogonal issue.
Independent (Kartify)
Payment method & star rating
$P(\text{5-star} \mid \text{UPI}) \approx P(\text{5-star} \mid \text{credit card}) \approx P(\text{5-star})$. Whether you paid by UPI or card doesn't predict your rating. Approximately independent.
Not independent
Late delivery & return
$P(R \mid L) = 0.23 \gg P(R) = 0.08$. Strong positive dependence. Late delivery causes returns.
Not independent
City & return rate
Delhi: 14%. Bengaluru: 5.5%. If independent, they would all be ~8%. The 2.5× gap tells us city matters — features derived from city are predictive in Kartify's ML models.
Nearly independent
Order number parity & return
Whether your order ID is odd or even shouldn't predict anything. $P(R \mid \text{odd ID}) \approx P(R)$. If your model says otherwise, you have a data leak.
⚠ Independence ≠ Disjoint

Common confusion. Disjoint ($A \cap B = \varnothing$): $A$ and $B$ cannot happen together — they block each other entirely. Independent ($P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$): they can happen together, and knowing one doesn't affect probability of the other. Disjoint events with positive probability are NEVER independent, because if $A$ happens, $B$ definitely does not — that's an extremely strong update.

Mutual independence of many events

For three or more events, pairwise independence is not enough — we need mutual independence, which requires every subset to factorize:

Mutual Independence — $n$ events
$$P(A_{i_1} \cap A_{i_2} \cap \ldots \cap A_{i_k}) = \prod_{j=1}^{k} P(A_{i_j}) \quad \text{for every subset } \{i_1, \ldots, i_k\} \subseteq \{1, \ldots, n\}$$

This is exactly what i.i.d. means — independent and identically distributed. All 3,000 Delhi orders modelled as i.i.d. Bernoulli($p$) trials is the assumption we used in Module 2.1 to compute $P(X \ge 420 \mid p=0.08)$. Mutual independence is what lets probabilities factor into clean products — every deep learning loss function implicitly assumes it across training examples.

Conditional Independence & Simpson's Paradox

Two events can be dependent overall, but become independent once you condition on a third variable. This is called conditional independence, written $A \perp B \mid C$.

Conditional Independence
$$A \perp B \mid C \iff P(A \cap B \mid C) = P(A \mid C)\, P(B \mid C)$$

This is the single most important concept in Bayesian networks, causal inference, and modern representation learning. It is also the concept that makes Simpson's paradox possible.

Kartify · Simpson's paradox — the checkout A/B test that lied
Setup. Priya ran an A/B test on the checkout page. Variant A vs B. She measured conversion.
Result overall. Variant A: 7.8% conversion. Variant B: 8.3%. B wins.
Result segmented by device. On mobile: A converts 4.2%, B converts 4.0%. A wins. On desktop: A converts 12.5%, B converts 12.1%. A wins on desktop too.
What happened? The aggregated "B wins" reverses when we condition on device. That's Simpson's paradox.
Cause. Unequal traffic mix. Variant B was shown to 60% desktop traffic (which converts higher regardless of variant). Variant A was 70% mobile. The confound was device, not variant.
The rule — never trust aggregate effects without conditional checks
Simpson's paradox is the formal statement of: correlation without conditioning can reverse when you condition on a confound. In Kartify's case, conversion is conditionally independent of variant given device — meaning variant doesn't matter once you know device. What matters is which variant saw which device mix. This is why A/B tests must either randomize uniformly (so device is balanced) or condition on device (so you compare mobile-A vs mobile-B directly). Either fix works — but you must do one.
◆ Conditional independence shows up everywhere

Naive Bayes classifier: assumes features are conditionally independent given the class — this is the "naive" assumption.

Hidden Markov Models: observations at time $t$ are conditionally independent of all others given the hidden state.

Latent variable models (VAEs, topic models): observations conditionally independent given the latent.

Graphical models: d-separation is literally the graphical rule for reading off conditional independences from a causal DAG.

Python — Conditional Probability & Bayes in Code

Every formula from this module in three compact, Kartify-consistent scripts. Paste any of these into a notebook and run.

python · contingency table → conditional probabilities
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

# Kartify: 1000-order sample — late × returned contingency
data = pd.DataFrame({
    "late":     [1]*150 + [0]*850,
    "returned": ([1]*35  + [0]*115) +   # 150 late orders: 35 returned
                 ([1]*45  + [0]*805),     # 850 on-time: 45 returned
})

# --- Joint, marginal, conditional probabilities ---
N = len(data)
pL    = (data["late"]==1).sum() / N                  # P(L) = 0.150
pR    = (data["returned"]==1).sum() / N              # P(R) = 0.080
pLR   = ((data["late"]==1) & (data["returned"]==1)).sum() / N   # P(L ∩ R) = 0.035

# Conditional probabilities — definition applied
p_R_given_L = pLR / pL         # 0.233 — given late, probability of return
p_L_given_R = pLR / pR         # 0.4375 — given return, probability late

print(f"P(R | L) = {p_R_given_L:.3f},  P(L | R) = {p_L_given_R:.4f}")

# --- Independence test: does P(L ∩ R) = P(L) × P(R)? ---
expected_if_independent = pL * pR
ratio = pLR / expected_if_independent
print(f"P(L ∩ R) observed = {pLR:.3f}")
print(f"P(L) × P(R)       = {expected_if_independent:.3f}  (if they were independent)")
print(f"Ratio             = {ratio:.2f}x    →   late orders ~3x more likely to return")

# One-line pandas version via crosstab
ct = pd.crosstab(data["late"], data["returned"], normalize="index")
# ct gives P(R | L) for each row of L directly — the cleanest way
print(ct)
python · Bayes' theorem — Kartify fraud posterior
def bayes_posterior(p_prior, p_evidence_given_A, p_evidence_given_notA):
    """
    Compute P(A | evidence) using Bayes + Law of Total Probability.
    Works for any binary partition — fraud, disease diagnosis, spam, etc.
    """
    num   = p_evidence_given_A * p_prior
    denom = num + p_evidence_given_notA * (1 - p_prior)
    return num / denom

# --- Kartify fraud detection ---
prior         = 0.01     # P(fraud)             — base rate
sensitivity   = 0.95     # P(flag | fraud)      — true-positive rate
false_pos     = 0.02     # P(flag | not fraud)  — false-positive rate

p_fraud_given_flag = bayes_posterior(prior, sensitivity, false_pos)
print(f"P(fraud | flagged) = {p_fraud_given_flag:.3f}")   # 0.324

# --- Sweep base rate: how does the posterior move? ---
for base in [0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, 0.05, 0.10]:
    p = bayes_posterior(base, sensitivity, false_pos)
    print(f"  base rate {base:.4f} → P(F|flag) = {p:.3f}")
# Shows that the SAME model can be 0.5% accurate on rare fraud
# and 84% accurate on common fraud — it's the base rate, not the model.
python · Law of Total Probability — Kartify city-weighted return rate
import pandas as pd

cities = pd.DataFrame({
    "city":           ["Mumbai","Delhi","Bengaluru","Chennai","Kolkata","Hyderabad","Pune","Ahmedabad"],
    "share":          [0.22, 0.18, 0.16, 0.11, 0.09, 0.10, 0.08, 0.06],   # P(B_i)
    "return_rate":    [0.062,0.140,0.055,0.071,0.089,0.065,0.058,0.080],  # P(R | B_i)
})

# Law of Total Probability — overall P(R)
cities["contrib"] = cities["share"] * cities["return_rate"]
p_R = cities["contrib"].sum()
print(f"Overall P(R) = {p_R:.4f}")   # 0.0794

# Bayes — given a return happened, which city did it likely come from?
cities["p_city_given_R"] = cities["contrib"] / p_R
print(cities[["city","share","return_rate","p_city_given_R"]])
# Delhi is 18% of orders but generates 31.7% of returns — that's the Bayes flip.
The Kartify workflow for every probability question
(1) Write the business question in English. (2) Translate each "given" to a condition after the bar $\mid$. (3) Check: is the probability you want "forward" ($P(\text{evidence} \mid \text{cause})$) or "inverse" ($P(\text{cause} \mid \text{evidence})$)? (4) If inverse, reach for Bayes. If it's decomposable over cases, reach for LTP. (5) Code it — and sanity check: does the posterior shift with the prior the way Bayes predicts? If it doesn't, you have a bug.
