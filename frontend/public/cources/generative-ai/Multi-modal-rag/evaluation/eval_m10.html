01 · Why Human Evaluation Remains the Ground Truth

The Limit That No Automated Metric
Has Crossed

Every automated evaluation metric — BLEU, BERTScore, RAGAS, G-Eval — is a proxy for human judgment. Automated metrics are fast, cheap, and scalable. But they systematically miss things humans catch naturally: subtle tone problems, culturally inappropriate phrasing, responses that are technically accurate but unhelpful, and emergent quality properties that were not anticipated when the metric was designed. Human evaluation is expensive and slow — it is also the only source of truth.

What automated metrics miss that humans catch

Tone and register: A technically correct response in legal language to a consumer question scores high on faithfulness but feels alienating and unhelpful to a real user.
Actionability: "The refund process involves several steps depending on your subscription tier" is coherent and relevant but gives the user nothing actionable. Humans immediately notice this.
Cultural appropriateness: Responses appropriate in one cultural context may be offensive in another. No automated metric covers this.
Overall helpfulness: The gestalt of "did this actually help me?" which requires simulating the user's mental state and goal — something automated metrics cannot do.

When to run human evaluation

Calibration: When setting up a new automated metric — human eval on 200 examples to validate the metric before trusting it at scale.
Pre-launch certification: Final quality gate before any major release — automated metrics clear the bar, human eval certifies it.
Investigating unexplained drops: When metrics degrade but automated investigation finds no root cause — human review reveals subjective quality issues that metrics cannot see.
New domain or use case: Before any automated metric can be trusted in a new domain, validate it against human judgments first.
02 · Rating Scale Design

Designing Annotation Tasks
That Produce Reliable Judgments

The reliability of human evaluation depends critically on how the task is designed. A vague 1-5 scale with no rubric produces inconsistent ratings. A well-designed scale with behavioral anchors at each level produces high Inter-Annotator Agreement (IAA). The goal is to minimize the space for annotator interpretation — each score level should have an unambiguous definition.

Well-designed vs poorly-designed rating scale

❌ Poor scale — produces unreliable ratings
Rate the response quality on a scale of 1-5.

1 = Very bad    2 = Bad    3 = OK    4 = Good    5 = Very good

Problems: "bad" and "very bad" are undefined. Two annotators reading the same response will use the scale differently. No behavioral anchors. No definition of what "quality" means — is it accuracy? helpfulness? tone?
✓ Good scale — produces reliable ratings
Helpfulness: Does this response help the user accomplish their goal?

5: Fully addresses the question, gives actionable next steps, no relevant information missing.
4: Addresses the question with one minor gap or slightly unclear step.
3: Partially addresses the question — answers some but not all aspects.
2: Mentions the topic but does not give enough information to be actionable.
1: Does not address the question at all, or actively misleads the user.
💡

Three design rules for high-IAA rating scales

1. One dimension per scale. Do not combine accuracy AND helpfulness into one score — they can conflict. Rate them separately. 2. Behavioral anchors at every level. Each level must describe specific observable characteristics, not adjectives. "One minor gap" is a behavior; "slightly bad" is an adjective. 3. Include calibration examples. Show annotators 3-5 pre-rated examples before they begin. Annotators who see worked examples produce ratings 40% more consistent with each other.

03 · Inter-Annotator Agreement

Measuring Whether Your Annotators
Are Evaluating the Same Thing

Inter-Annotator Agreement (IAA) measures how consistently multiple human annotators rate the same examples. Low IAA does not mean the annotators are wrong — it means the task definition is ambiguous, the rubric is unclear, or the examples fall in genuine gray areas. You cannot trust evaluation results with low IAA regardless of how many annotators you use.

Cohen's kappa: worked calculation for 2 annotators

Setup: 2 annotators rate 20 examples on binary "Helpful / Not Helpful"

Observed agreement matrix:
A1 says Helpful, A2 says Helpful: 12 examples
A1 says Not Helpful, A2 says Not Helpful: 4 examples
A1 says Helpful, A2 says Not Helpful: 2 examples
A1 says Not Helpful, A2 says Helpful: 2 examples

Observed agreement (P_o) = (12 + 4) / 20 = 0.80

Expected agreement by chance (P_e):
P(both say Helpful) = (14/20) × (14/20) = 0.49
P(both say Not Helpful) = (6/20) × (6/20) = 0.09
P_e = 0.49 + 0.09 = 0.58

Cohen's κ = (P_o - P_e) / (1 - P_e) = (0.80 - 0.58) / (1 - 0.58) = 0.22 / 0.42 = 0.524
Kappa valueInterpretationAction
< 0.20Slight agreement — effectively randomDo not use this data. Rewrite rubric completely.
0.21 – 0.40Fair agreementRubric needs significant revision. Retrain annotators.
0.41 – 0.60Moderate agreementAcceptable for some tasks. Add calibration examples.
0.61 – 0.80Substantial agreementGood. Use this data for metric calibration.
> 0.80Near-perfect agreementExcellent. Use as ground truth with confidence.

κ = 0.524 is moderate agreement — the rubric could be improved. Annotators agreed on 80% of examples but the kappa adjustment reveals the task has genuine ambiguity. Adding calibration examples and clearer anchor descriptions typically raises kappa by 0.10-0.20.

iaa_calculation.py

from sklearn.metrics import cohen_kappa_score
import numpy as np

# Cohen's kappa for 2 annotators (binary or multi-class)
a1 = [5,4,3,5,2,4,1,5,3,4]  # annotator 1 ratings
a2 = [5,3,3,4,2,4,2,5,4,4]  # annotator 2 ratings
kappa = cohen_kappa_score(a1, a2, weights="quadratic")  # quadratic for ordinal scales
print(f"Cohen's κ = {kappa:.3f}")

# Fleiss' kappa for 3+ annotators
from statsmodels.stats.inter_rater import fleiss_kappa, aggregate_raters
ratings_matrix = np.array([  # rows=items, cols=annotators
    [5,5,4], [3,4,3], [1,2,1], [4,4,5], [2,2,3]
])
table, _ = aggregate_raters(ratings_matrix, n_cat=5)
fk = fleiss_kappa(table)
print(f"Fleiss' κ = {fk:.3f}")

# Krippendorff's alpha — most robust for ordinal scales
from krippendorff import alpha
data = np.array([a1, a2])  # rows=annotators, cols=items
ka = alpha(reliability_data=data, level_of_measurement="ordinal")
print(f"Krippendorff's α = {ka:.3f}")
# Target: α > 0.667 for ordinal scales (Krippendorff's recommendation)
04 · Pairwise Preference Studies

A vs B: The Most Reliable Human Evaluation
for Comparative Quality

Pairwise preference studies present annotators with two responses to the same query (Response A and Response B, unlabeled) and ask which is better. Pairwise is more reliable than absolute scoring because humans are better at "which is better?" than "how good is this on a 1-5 scale?" It is the standard method for comparing two model versions or two prompt strategies.

Pairwise preference study: design and analysis

Comparing: Claude Sonnet prompt v1 (System A) vs Claude Sonnet prompt v2 (System B)
200 pairwise comparisons, 3 annotators each, annotations blind to which system generated each response

Results:
A preferred: 112 times (56%)
B preferred: 74 times (37%)
Tied: 14 times (7%)

Win rate A (excluding ties) = 112 / (112 + 74) = 0.603
95% confidence interval: [0.534, 0.669] — entirely above 0.50, so A is reliably preferred

Key design principles for pairwise studies

Blind labeling: Annotators never know which system generated A or B. Position bias (always preferring the first response) is real and large.
Position randomization: Randomly swap which system's response appears first across pairs. This cancels out position bias.
Tie option: Always include a "Both equally good" option. Forcing a choice when there is no meaningful difference inflates apparent differences.
Minimum 150 pairs: Below 100 pairs, the confidence interval is too wide to make reliable conclusions. 200+ pairs gives tight CIs.

Statistical significance testing

from scipy.stats import binomtest A_wins, B_wins = 112, 74 total = A_wins + B_wins # exclude ties # Null hypothesis: A and B are equally good (p=0.5) result = binomtest(A_wins, total, p=0.5, alternative="greater") print(f"p-value: {result.pvalue:.4f}") # → 0.0034 print(f"Statistically significant: {result.pvalue < 0.05}") # → True # Win rate with confidence interval import scipy.stats as stats win_rate = A_wins / total ci = stats.proportion_confint(A_wins, total, alpha=0.05) print(f"Win rate A: {win_rate:.3f} [{ci[0]:.3f}, {ci[1]:.3f}]") # → Win rate A: 0.603 [0.529, 0.673]

06 · Sample Size Calculation

How Many Examples Do You Need
for Statistically Reliable Human Evaluation?

Sample size determines whether your human evaluation conclusions are reliable or noise. Too few examples and you cannot distinguish a real 5% quality improvement from random variation. The sample size required depends on the effect size you want to detect and your desired statistical power.

Sample size table for pairwise preference studies

Minimum detectable win ratePower = 0.80Power = 0.90What this means
60% vs 50% (10pp difference)194 pairs268 pairsDetect moderate improvement with 80% confidence
55% vs 50% (5pp difference)783 pairs1,051 pairsDetect subtle improvement — expensive
65% vs 50% (15pp difference)88 pairs121 pairsDetect large improvement — affordable
70% vs 50% (20pp difference)50 pairs70 pairsDetect major improvement — quick study
⚠️

Most teams run too few annotations

A common mistake: running 50 pairwise comparisons and concluding "System A is better (52% win rate, p=0.42)." With 50 pairs, you cannot detect any difference smaller than 20 percentage points with reliable statistical power. Your 52% win rate could easily be noise. To reliably compare two systems that are close in quality, you need 200+ pairwise comparisons. If your budget allows only 50 pairs, you can only reliably detect differences of 20pp or more — which means your systems must be very different in quality for the study to be conclusive.

07 · Annotation Protocol Template

The Artifacts You Need
Before Annotators Start

Minimum viable annotation protocol: 5 components

Component 1: Task framing

One paragraph explaining: what system generated these responses, who the intended user is, what success looks like from the user's perspective. Without this, annotators judge from their own perspective rather than the target user's.

Component 2: Dimension definitions

One paragraph per dimension being rated, defining exactly what each dimension means in this context. Include: what to ignore (e.g., "do not rate style, only content accuracy") and what to prioritize.

Component 3: Rating rubric with behavioral anchors

For each dimension, a 5-level rubric where each level has a behavioral description (not an adjective). Level 3 should describe "acceptable but improvable" with a concrete example of what a 3/5 response looks like.

Component 4: Calibration examples (5-10 pre-rated examples)

Show annotators 5-10 examples with their correct ratings and explanations. Annotators must rate these first. If their ratings differ significantly from the key (kappa < 0.6), re-training is required before they annotate real data.

Component 5: Edge case handling guide

Address the ambiguous cases you anticipate: what to do when the response is partially correct, when the question is ambiguous, when the response refuses appropriately vs inappropriately. Unaddressed edge cases produce inconsistent annotations.

09 · Assessment

Module 10 Quiz

1. You run a pairwise study with 50 pairs comparing System A vs B and get a win rate for A of 56%. You conclude A is better. Why is this conclusion statistically invalid?

Correct. Statistical power is the probability that you correctly detect a real effect when one exists. With 50 pairs and a true win rate of 56%, your study has approximately 20% power — meaning you would correctly identify A as better only 20% of the time even if it really is better. The other 80% of the time you would fail to detect the difference. This is why sample size calculation must happen BEFORE the study, not after. For 80% power to detect a 56% win rate (6pp above chance), you need ~600 pairs — 12× more than you ran.

2. You run a human evaluation with 3 annotators and compute Cohen's kappa = 0.28 (fair agreement). Should you trust and report these results?

Correct. Raw agreement of 72% with kappa = 0.28 is a classic case where surface agreement looks acceptable but the kappa correction reveals the task is too ambiguous to produce reliable judgments. If 50% of examples are rated "Helpful" by both annotators independently, 25% of the observed agreement (0.50 × 0.50 = 0.25) would occur by chance even if the annotators were randomly clicking. Kappa corrects for this. A raw agreement of 72% with P_e = 0.58 gives kappa = (0.72-0.58)/(1-0.58) = 0.33 — which tells the real story: only 33% of the agreement above chance. Rubric revision is the fix, not averaging.

3. For a medical AI assistant evaluation, you propose using crowd workers on Mechanical Turk at $0.10/annotation to keep costs down. What is wrong with this approach for a medical domain?

Correct. Domain expertise is a prerequisite for reliable annotation in specialized domains. Medical factual accuracy cannot be evaluated by consensus among non-experts — 5 non-experts who are all wrong still agree with each other (high apparent IAA) while producing incorrect ground truth labels. The split approach (crowd for fluency, experts for accuracy) partially addresses this: crowd workers can reliably judge whether a response is grammatically clear and well-organized; only experts can judge whether the clinical content is correct. But in high-stakes domains, you ultimately need experts to validate any dimension that touches domain knowledge.

4. In a pairwise study, you discover that System A's responses are consistently chosen first by annotators when they appear in the first position, but System B wins when in the first position. This gives A an apparent 55% win rate. What evaluation design flaw caused this?

Correct. Position bias is a well-documented cognitive effect in pairwise evaluation. The primacy effect causes evaluators to anchor on the first response they read and judge subsequent responses relative to it. Studies have found position bias effects of 5-15 percentage points — exactly the magnitude of the apparent win rate here. Without randomization, a system that always appears first will systematically appear to win ~5% more often than its actual quality warrants. This is enough to incorrectly conclude a slightly inferior system is superior. Position randomization is a non-negotiable design requirement for any pairwise study.

5. You want to detect a 5% quality improvement (from 75% to 80% helpfulness rate) using a direct rating study. Annotators rate each response 1-5 on helpfulness. How many examples do you need?

Correct. The relationship between effect size and required sample size is nonlinear: detecting small effects requires dramatically more data than detecting large effects. A 5pp absolute rating improvement (75% → 80%) is a small effect that requires 700-1000+ examples to detect reliably. This is frequently impractical. The practical response: (1) decide what the minimum business-meaningful effect size is (maybe you only care if it improves by 10% or more), (2) calculate sample size for that effect size, and (3) if the required sample is too large, either accept lower statistical power or accept that you cannot reliably distinguish systems with similar quality using human evaluation alone — and rely on automated metrics for smaller differences.
