01 · Why Benchmarks Exist and Why They Fail

Measuring Capability at Scale:
What Benchmarks Can and Cannot Tell You

Benchmarks provide standardized, reproducible capability measurements that allow comparison across models, versions, and providers. Without them, "our model is better" is just marketing. With them, you can say "our model scores 85.2 on MMLU vs 82.7 for the previous version." But benchmarks measure capability in controlled conditions — not quality in your specific domain under your specific usage patterns. A model that tops MMLU may still fail at your customer support queries.

The benchmark lifecycle — from signal to noise

Year 1: Benchmark released. Measures a real capability gap. Models cluster around 50-70%.
Year 2: Training teams notice benchmark. Some training data contamination begins. Scores rise faster than capability.
Year 3: Models routinely score 90%+. Benchmark is saturated. Ceases to discriminate between good and great models.
Year 4: New harder benchmark released. Cycle repeats. Old benchmark still cited for marketing by lagging models.

Five ways benchmark scores are inflated

Data contamination: Benchmark test questions appear in training data. Model memorizes answers rather than demonstrating capability.
Prompt engineering: Extensive prompt tuning specifically for benchmark format inflates scores vs zero-shot performance.
Selective reporting: Teams report only the benchmarks where their model performs well, omitting benchmarks where it lags.
Evaluation harness differences: Different implementations of the same benchmark produce scores that differ by 3-8 points.
Distributional shift: Benchmark questions from 2021 may test different things than 2025 production queries due to world knowledge updates.
02 · Knowledge and Reasoning Benchmarks

MMLU, ARC, HellaSwag, GSM8K:
What Each Actually Measures

Knowledge and reasoning benchmarks test whether a model can answer questions correctly across academic domains, commonsense scenarios, and multi-step problems. They are the most commonly cited benchmarks in model release announcements — and among the most frequently misinterpreted.

BenchmarkWhat it measuresFormatSOTA scoreProduction relevance
MMLU

⚠️

MMLU at 90% does not mean the model is reliable for your domain

A model scoring 90% on MMLU answers 10% of academic questions incorrectly — across 57 domains. But MMLU is uniformly sampled: a model may score 98% on easy history questions and 72% on advanced clinical pharmacology. If your application is in clinical pharmacology, the 90% headline number is irrelevant. Always look at per-domain subscores when using MMLU for model selection, and weight them by their relevance to your use case.

03 · Factuality and Truthfulness Benchmarks

TruthfulQA and FactScore:
Does the Model Know What It Does Not Know?

Factuality benchmarks test whether models give accurate answers and whether they appropriately express uncertainty when they do not know. A high-accuracy model that hallucinates confidently is more dangerous than a lower-accuracy model that says "I'm not certain." TruthfulQA specifically targets questions where humans commonly hold misconceptions — testing whether models have learned to propagate false beliefs from training data.

TruthfulQA: what it tests and what it misses

Example TruthfulQA question:
"What happens if you swallow a small amount of bleach?"

Incorrect (but common) answer: "It immediately causes severe internal burns and is almost certainly lethal."
Correct answer: "Small amounts cause mouth/throat irritation and nausea; dangerous but not immediately lethal. Medical attention recommended."

Models trained on internet text learn misconceptions from forums. TruthfulQA tests whether they reproduce these misconceptions.
Truthful score: fraction of answers that are factually accurate
Informative score: fraction of answers that give useful information (not just "I don't know")
Truthful × Informative: the combined metric that matters — being truthful by refusing to answer scores 100% truthful but 0% informative

FEVER and FactScore: structured fact verification

FEVER: Fact Extraction and VERification — 185K claims labeled SUPPORTED, REFUTED, or NOT ENOUGH INFO against Wikipedia. Tests whether models correctly assess claim verifiability. Production relevance: medium (Wikipedia-specific).
FActScore: Atomic claim decomposition + verification against Wikipedia. Measures factual precision of long-form generations. Direct predecessor to RAGAS Faithfulness. High production relevance for RAG applications.
SimpleQA (OpenAI): Short, factual questions with unambiguous answers. Harder than TruthfulQA for frontier models. Designed to resist training contamination via newly-generated questions.
04 · Code Generation Benchmarks

HumanEval, MBPP, SWE-bench:
Testing Whether Code Actually Runs

Code benchmarks are unique: they use execution-based evaluation. A code solution is correct if and only if it passes the unit tests — there is no partial credit for "looks right." This makes code benchmarks the most objective and hardest to game of all benchmark types. Pass@k measures the probability that at least one of k generated solutions is correct.

BenchmarkWhat it measuresMetricSOTAKey limitation
HumanEvalPython function completion from docstring. 164 hand-crafted problems.Pass@1 (single attempt)~90%+ (GPT-4o, Claude Opus)Largely saturated. Simple algorithmic problems only. No multi-file context.
MBPP
Mostly Basic Python Problems		427 crowd-sourced Python problems covering basic programming tasks.Pass@1~90%+ (frontier models)Saturated. Some test cases have errors. Better as a sanity check than a discriminator.
SWE-bench VerifiedReal GitHub issues from major Python repos. Model must generate a patch that passes the repo test suite.% issues resolved~50-55% (frontier agents)Expensive to run. Python-only. But the most production-realistic code benchmark.
LiveCodeBenchNew competitive programming problems scraped continuously. Resists contamination by design.Pass@1~40-60% (varies widely)Harder than HumanEval. Better discriminator for frontier models.
💡

SWE-bench Verified is the most production-relevant code benchmark

SWE-bench Verified uses real GitHub issues from real repos: the model must understand a codebase, identify the bug, and generate a patch that passes existing tests — exactly what a software engineering agent does in production. A 50% SWE-bench score means the model resolves half of real GitHub issues autonomously. This translates directly to engineering productivity. HumanEval Pass@1 90% does not translate to real-world engineering value because it tests toy-sized isolated functions, not multi-file understanding.

05 · Chat Quality and Safety Benchmarks

MT-Bench, LMSYS Arena, HarmBench:
Evaluating Conversational Models

MT-Bench

80 high-quality multi-turn questions across 8 categories: writing, roleplay, extraction, reasoning, math, coding, STEM, humanities. GPT-4 judges each response 1-10. Most cited benchmark for chat model quality.

Score range: 7.5-9.5 for frontier models (2024)
Limitation: Only 80 questions — high variance. GPT-4 judge has verbosity bias.
Relevance: High for general-purpose assistants

LMSYS Chatbot Arena

Real users interact with two anonymous models simultaneously and vote for the better response. Over 1M battles collected. Elo ratings (like chess) derived from win rates. Most human-aligned benchmark.

Elo range: 1100-1400+ for frontier models
Limitation: Favors helpful and verbose responses. Skews toward English speakers. Recency bias.
Relevance: Highest for user-facing chat applications

HarmBench

Standardized red-teaming benchmark with 510 behaviors across 7 harm categories. Tests both direct and transfer attacks. Measures Attack Success Rate across attack methods (GCG, AutoDAN, PAIR, TAP).

ASR range: 2-40% depending on model and attack
Limitation: Known attacks only. Novel jailbreaks not covered.
Relevance: High for any system with safety requirements
06 · HELM — Holistic Evaluation

The Multi-Metric Framework:
Accuracy is Not Enough

HELM (Holistic Evaluation of Language Models) from Stanford evaluates models across 7 dimensions simultaneously: accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity, and efficiency. The insight: a model that scores 90% accuracy but exhibits significant demographic bias or is poorly calibrated (overconfident when wrong) is not a good model — accuracy alone is not enough.

HELM dimensions: what each measures and production relevance

DimensionWhat it measuresWhy it matters for production
AccuracyCorrect answers on tasksThe minimum bar — necessary but not sufficient
CalibrationWhether confidence matches accuracy — overconfident wrong answers vs uncertain correct onesCritical: a miscalibrated model that says "I'm certain" when wrong is more dangerous than a model that hedges
RobustnessScore stability under paraphrasing, punctuation changes, capitalization variationsProduction queries are noisy. Brittle models fail unpredictably on real user inputs.
FairnessConsistency of performance across demographic groupsEssential for any user-facing application. Legal requirement in many jurisdictions.
ToxicityRate of generating harmful, offensive, or abusive contentProduction requirement: zero tolerance for brand-damaging outputs
EfficiencyTokens per dollar, latency per queryBusiness viability: high accuracy at 100× the cost may not be viable

08 · Building Your Own Domain Benchmark

When Public Benchmarks Are Not Enough:
The Domain Evaluation Set

For any production application, your own domain evaluation set is more valuable than any public benchmark. It tests exactly what your users ask, on exactly your knowledge base, with exactly your expected output format. A model that scores 5 points higher on MMLU may score 10 points lower on your domain eval — the public benchmark score was irrelevant to your selection decision.

domain_benchmark_builder.py

from langchain_anthropic import ChatAnthropic
from langchain_core.prompts import ChatPromptTemplate
from langchain_core.output_parsers import JsonOutputParser
import json, random

llm = ChatAnthropic(model="claude-sonnet-4-20250514")

# Step 1: Generate domain questions from your documents
GEN_PROMPT = ChatPromptTemplate.from_messages([("system", """
Generate 5 diverse evaluation questions from this document chunk.
Include: factual recall, reasoning, edge case, comparison, and procedural questions.
For each question include the answer and which part of the text supports it.
Output JSON: [{"question": "...", "answer": "...", "source_quote": "...", "type": "factual|reasoning|edge_case|comparison|procedural", "difficulty": "easy|medium|hard"}]
"""), ("human", "{chunk}")])

gen_chain = GEN_PROMPT | llm | JsonOutputParser()

def generate_domain_benchmark(
    chunks: list[str],
    n_chunks: int = 50
) -> list[dict]:
    """Generate a 250-question domain benchmark from document chunks."""
    sampled = random.sample(chunks, min(n_chunks, len(chunks)))
    all_questions = []
    for chunk in sampled:
        try:
            questions = gen_chain.invoke({"chunk": chunk})
            all_questions.extend(questions)
        except: pass
    return all_questions

# Step 2: Run model against benchmark
def evaluate_on_domain_benchmark(
    model_fn,         # function: query -> response
    benchmark: list[dict],
    judge_fn          # function: (question, reference_answer, model_answer) -> score 0-1
) -> dict:
    results = []
    for item in benchmark:
        response = model_fn(item["question"])
        score = judge_fn(item["question"], item["answer"], response)
        results.append({**item, "model_response": response, "score": score})
    
    by_type = {}
    for t in ["factual", "reasoning", "edge_case", "comparison", "procedural"]:
        subset = [r for r in results if r["type"] == t]
        if subset: by_type[t] = sum(r["score"] for r in subset) / len(subset)
    
    return {
        "overall": sum(r["score"] for r in results) / len(results),
        "by_type": by_type,
        "by_difficulty": {d: [r["score"] for r in results if r.get("difficulty")==d]
                          for d in ["easy","medium","hard"]},
        "n_questions": len(results),
        "failures": [r for r in results if r["score"] < 0.4]
    }
09 · Assessment

Module 12 Quiz

1. Model A scores 91% on MMLU and 88% on TruthfulQA. Model B scores 88% on MMLU and 94% on TruthfulQA. You are building a medical information chatbot. Which model should you choose and why?

Correct. Benchmark selection for model comparison must reflect the application's specific requirements. A medical chatbot is high-stakes: a response that propagates a medical myth (what TruthfulQA measures) can cause real harm. A 6pp advantage on TruthfulQA is substantial and directly relevant. MMLU's breadth is less important because your domain is narrow (medical), and MMLU may have tested irrelevant domains (history, law) in model A's favor. Domain-specific subscores + your own eval set are the only reliable selection criteria for production model choice.

2. A model vendor claims "95% on HumanEval" for their code generation model. You test it on your internal Python codebase and find it correctly completes only 61% of your test cases. What explains the discrepancy?

Correct. The HumanEval-to-production gap is one of the most commonly observed benchmark failures. HumanEval tests an artificial, simplified version of code generation that does not match real software engineering: small functions, clear specs, no dependencies, no multi-file context. Real codebases have all of these. The training contamination issue compounds this: models have seen HumanEval questions in GitHub discussions, blog posts, and tutorials. SWE-bench Verified solves this by using real GitHub issues with tests the model has never seen. The lesson: never use saturated benchmarks (HumanEval 90%+) as the primary selection criterion for code generation models.

3. You observe that your candidate model scores 8.7 on MT-Bench but only 1,150 Elo on LMSYS Arena, while a competitor scores 8.2 on MT-Bench but 1,280 Elo on Arena. How do you explain this divergence?

Correct. MT-Bench and Arena are measuring different human-preference proxies. MT-Bench: 80 curated questions + GPT-4 judge → measures structured reasoning quality as evaluated by a language model. Arena: millions of real user battles + actual human voters → measures practical conversational utility as perceived by real users. The divergence reveals a model that is academically impressive but practically suboptimal: it may over-explain, use formal language where casual is appropriate, or optimize for completeness at the expense of directness. For consumer applications, Arena Elo is the better predictor. For enterprise knowledge work, MT-Bench may be more relevant.

4. A startup claims their model "beats GPT-4 on MMLU" with a score of 88.3% vs GPT-4's 86.4%. Should you update your model selection based on this claim?

Correct. Benchmark claims require scrutiny before influencing production decisions. The 1.9pp gap on MMLU at saturation (86-90%) is in the noise zone where: evaluation harness differences (3-8pp), selective benchmark reporting (cherry-picking the best evaluation run), and distribution-specific advantages (the model trained more on MMLU-style academic text) can fully explain the gap without any real capability difference. The correct response is domain-specific evaluation on your own data, not updating model selection based on a saturated public benchmark.

5. You need to select a model for a legal document analysis application. Rank these benchmarks by relevance to your selection decision and justify the ranking.

Correct. Benchmark relevance is always domain-specific. Legal document analysis requires: deep legal knowledge (MMLU Law subcore), factual precision (TruthfulQA — wrong legal facts are professional liability), structured legal reasoning (MT-Bench reasoning, BIG-Bench), and your domain-specific performance (custom eval). It does not require: general math ability (GSM8K), commonsense physical reasoning (HellaSwag), or general consumer chat preference (Arena Elo). The domain eval set is always rank-1 because it directly tests performance on your task, your documents, and your expected output format — making all public benchmark decisions secondary to it.
