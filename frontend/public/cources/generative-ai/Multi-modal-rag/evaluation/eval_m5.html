01 · Beyond Surface Metrics

Why n-gram Methods Fail
for Factual Quality Evaluation

BLEU, ROUGE, and BERTScore measure lexical or semantic similarity — they cannot verify whether a claim is factually correct. "The refund window is 30 days" and "The refund window is 90 days" have near-identical BERTScore (both are fluent, grammatically parallel, semantically similar sentences about refund windows). Generation quality metrics go deeper: they verify whether specific claims are entailed by source documents, decompose responses into verifiable atomic facts, and apply structured rubrics to assess quality dimensions independently.

NLI-based verification

Uses natural language inference models to determine if a claim is entailed by, neutral to, or contradicted by a reference text. Binary judgment: supported or not supported.

Strength: fast, local, no API costs · Weakness: misses implied facts, struggles with negation

FactScore decomposition

Decomposes a response into atomic claims (one fact per claim), then verifies each claim independently against a knowledge source. Returns factual precision of the full response.

Strength: fine-grained fact-level scoring · Weakness: requires LLM for decomposition, expensive

G-Eval rubric scoring

Uses a chain-of-thought LLM judge with a structured multi-criteria rubric. The judge reasons step-by-step before giving a 1-5 score for each dimension. Most human-aligned approach.

Strength: high human correlation, explainable · Weakness: expensive, inconsistent on edge cases
02 · NLI-Based Faithfulness Verification

Entailment, Contradiction, Neutral:
Using NLI Models for Fact Checking

Natural Language Inference (NLI) classifies the relationship between a "premise" (retrieved context) and a "hypothesis" (a claim from the generated answer) into three categories: ENTAILMENT (context supports the claim), CONTRADICTION (context contradicts the claim), or NEUTRAL (context neither supports nor contradicts). For faithfulness evaluation, we check whether each claim in the response is entailed by the retrieved context.

NLI verification: worked examples with probability scores

Premise (retrieved context): "Annual subscribers are eligible for a full refund within 30 calendar days of their subscription start date. Refunds are processed within 5-7 business days."

Hypothesis A: "Annual plans have a 30-day refund period"

NLI output: ENTAILMENT (prob=0.94) | NEUTRAL (0.04) | CONTRADICTION (0.02)

→ Claim supported ✓ — direct paraphrase of "30 calendar days" and "annual subscribers"

Hypothesis B: "Refunds are processed within a week"

NLI output: ENTAILMENT (0.61) | NEUTRAL (0.31) | CONTRADICTION (0.08)

→ Borderline ⚠ — "5-7 business days" does not precisely equal "a week" (could be 7-10 calendar days). NLI classifies as entailment but with lower confidence.

Hypothesis C: "Monthly subscribers also get a 30-day refund"

NLI output: ENTAILMENT (0.07) | NEUTRAL (0.68) | CONTRADICTION (0.25)

→ Not supported ✗ — context only mentions annual subscribers. Monthly not addressed = NEUTRAL/CONTRADICTION depending on interpretation.

Hypothesis D: "Refunds take less than 24 hours"

NLI output: ENTAILMENT (0.02) | NEUTRAL (0.11) | CONTRADICTION (0.87)

→ Contradiction detected ✗ — "5-7 business days" directly contradicts "less than 24 hours". Faithfulness = 0, this is an active hallucination.

nli_faithfulness.py

from transformers import pipeline
import re

# Fast, free, runs locally — ~90% accuracy vs LLM judge
nli_pipeline = pipeline(
    "text-classification",
    model="cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-base",
    device=-1  # CPU; use 0 for GPU
)

def nli_score(premise: str, hypothesis: str, threshold: float = 0.5) -> dict:
    """Returns entailment probability and binary supported/not verdict."""
    input_text = f"{premise} [SEP] {hypothesis}"
    result = nli_pipeline(input_text)[0]
    label = result["label"]
    score = result["score"]
    return {
        "label": label,
        "confidence": score,
        "supported": label == "ENTAILMENT" and score >= threshold
    }

DECOMPOSE_RE = re.compile(rr'\d+\.\s+')

def decompose_claims(text: str) -> list[str]:
    """Simple sentence-level claim decomposition (no LLM needed)."""
    sentences = [s.strip() for s in text.split(".") if len(s.strip()) > 10]
    return sentences

def nli_faithfulness(response: str, context: str) -> dict:
    claims = decompose_claims(response)
    results = []
    for claim in claims:
        verdict = nli_score(context, claim)
        results.append({"claim": claim, **verdict})
    supported = sum(1 for r in results if r["supported"])
    return {
        "faithfulness": supported / len(claims) if claims else 1.0,
        "per_claim": results,
        "method": "NLI/DeBERTa-v3-base"
    }

# Usage:
result = nli_faithfulness(
    response="Annual plans have a 30-day refund. Refunds take less than 24 hours.",
    context="Annual subscribers eligible for refund within 30 days. Processing: 5-7 business days."
)
# {"faithfulness": 0.50, "per_claim": [{"claim": "...", "supported": True}, {"claim": "...", "supported": False}]}
⚠️

Three failure modes of NLI-based evaluation

(1) Negation confusion: "Refunds are not available" can score as NEUTRAL instead of CONTRADICTION for context saying "Refunds are available" — some NLI models struggle with negation. (2) Numerical imprecision: "a week" vs "5-7 business days" may score ENTAILMENT even though a week = 5 business days, not 7. (3) Implication failures: Context "Annual plan costs $99/month" does not ENTAIL "Annual plan costs more than $50" even though it is mathematically implied — NLI uses strict textual entailment, not logical inference. Use LLM-as-judge for these edge cases.

03 · FactScore — Atomic Claim Verification

Every Fact, Verified Independently:
Fine-Grained Factual Precision

FactScore decomposes a long-form generated text into atomic claims — each expressing exactly one verifiable fact — then verifies each claim against a knowledge source independently. The FactScore is the fraction of atomic claims that are supported. This gives per-sentence and per-claim visibility into where hallucinations occur, not just an aggregate number.

04 · G-Eval — Chain-of-Thought Rubric Scoring

Structured Multi-Criteria Evaluation
with LLM Reasoning Traces

G-Eval (Liu et al., 2023) uses a carefully structured LLM prompt with: (1) explicit evaluation criteria with definitions, (2) a numbered scoring rubric with examples at each level, and (3) a chain-of-thought instruction that asks the model to reason before scoring. The CoT trace makes the score interpretable and reduces inconsistency from the "LLM mood" effect.

G-Eval prompt structure and output example

G-Eval Faithfulness Prompt:
"You are an expert evaluator. Your task is to evaluate the faithfulness of an AI response to its source context.

Evaluation Criteria: Faithfulness measures whether ALL factual claims in the response are supported by the provided context. A response is unfaithful if it adds ANY information not present in the context, even if that information might be true in general.

Scoring Rubric:
5 - All claims are directly supported by the context. Zero hallucinations.
4 - One minor unsupported detail (e.g., a transitional phrase or small clarification) but all key claims are supported.
3 - One significant unsupported claim OR two minor unsupported claims.
2 - Multiple significant hallucinated claims that would mislead the user.
1 - Most claims are unsupported or actively contradicted by the context.

Task: First, list all factual claims in the response. For each claim, state whether it is supported, neutral, or contradicted. Then provide your score 1-5 with a one-sentence justification."
Example G-Eval output (CoT + score):
"Claims analysis:
• 'Annual plans have a 30-day refund window' → SUPPORTED (context: 'within 30 calendar days')
• 'Submit via Account Settings' → SUPPORTED (context: 'via Account Settings portal')
• 'Monthly subscribers are also refundable' → NOT SUPPORTED (context only mentions annual subscribers)
• 'Customer support can expedite' → NOT SUPPORTED (not mentioned in context)

Score: 3/5. Two of four claims are supported, but two significant claims about monthly subscribers and expedited support are not present in the context and would mislead users."

g_eval_scorer.py

from langchain_anthropic import ChatAnthropic
from langchain_core.prompts import ChatPromptTemplate
from langchain_core.output_parsers import JsonOutputParser

llm = ChatAnthropic(model="claude-sonnet-4-20250514", temperature=0)

G_EVAL_PROMPT = ChatPromptTemplate.from_messages([("system", """
You are an expert NLP evaluator. Evaluate the response on these dimensions.

Coherence (1-5): logical flow, no contradictions
  5=perfect logic, 4=minor gaps, 3=some unclear transitions, 2=major gaps, 1=contradicts itself

Consistency/Faithfulness (1-5): all claims supported by context
  5=all supported, 4=one minor unsupported, 3=one major unsupported, 2=multiple major, 1=mostly hallucinated

Fluency (1-5): grammatically correct, natural language
  5=perfect, 4=minor errors, 3=some errors, 2=hard to read, 1=incomprehensible

Relevance (1-5): directly addresses the question
  5=completely on-topic, 4=mostly relevant, 3=partially relevant, 2=tangentially relevant, 1=off-topic

For EACH dimension:
1. List evidence (quote specific phrases from the response)
2. Reasoning (why does this evidence support your score?)
3. Score (integer 1-5)

Output JSON:
{
  "coherence": {"evidence": "...", "reasoning": "...", "score": 1-5},
  "faithfulness": {"evidence": "...", "reasoning": "...", "score": 1-5},
  "fluency": {"evidence": "...", "reasoning": "...", "score": 1-5},
  "relevance": {"evidence": "...", "reasoning": "...", "score": 1-5}
}

Context: {context}
"""), ("human", "Question: {question}
Response: {response}")])

g_eval_chain = G_EVAL_PROMPT | llm | JsonOutputParser()

def g_eval_score(question: str, response: str, context: str = "N/A") -> dict:
    scores = g_eval_chain.invoke({
        "question": question, "response": response, "context": context
    })
    # Run 3 times, average for stability (G-Eval recommendation)
    # scores = mean of 3 independent runs
    overall = sum(dim["score"] for dim in scores.values()) / len(scores)
    return {**scores, "overall": overall}

# Stability tip: run g_eval 3x and average scores
def stable_g_eval(question, response, context, n=3) -> dict:
    all_scores = [g_eval_score(question, response, context) for _ in range(n)]
    return {dim: sum(r[dim]["score"] for r in all_scores)/n
            for dim in ["coherence","faithfulness","fluency","relevance"]}
05 · QA-Based Evaluation

Test Whether the Answer Contains
the Right Facts, Not Just the Right Words

QA-based evaluation generates factual questions from the reference answer, then asks those questions to the generated response. If the generated response contains the correct answers to these questions, it must contain the key facts — regardless of wording. This approach catches cases where BERTScore gives high similarity to a wrong answer because the sentence structure is similar.

QA-based evaluation: the pipeline

Step 1: Generate factual questions from reference

Reference: "Annual plans have a 30-day refund window. Submit via Account Settings. Processing takes 5-7 business days."
→ Q1: "How many days is the refund window?" (answer: 30)
→ Q2: "Where do you submit a refund request?" (answer: Account Settings)
→ Q3: "How long does refund processing take?" (answer: 5-7 business days)

Step 2: Extract answers from generated response

Generated: "You can get a refund on an annual plan within one month via the settings portal. It usually takes about a week."
→ Q1 answer extracted: "one month" → matches "30 days"? ≈ YES (30 days ≈ one month)
→ Q2 answer extracted: "settings portal" → matches "Account Settings"? YES
→ Q3 answer extracted: "about a week" → matches "5-7 business days"? BORDERLINE

Step 3: Compute QA-based accuracy

2.5/3 facts correctly contained → QA-based accuracy ≈ 0.83

When QA-based evaluation catches what BERTScore misses

BERTScore: 0.89 (misses the error)
Reference: "The refund window is 30 days."
Generated: "The refund window is 90 days."
BERTScore: 0.89 — high (same sentence structure, similar embedding)
QA accuracy: 0/1 — "How many days?" → "90" ≠ "30" → WRONG
QA-based catches the numeric error
QA generates "How long is the refund window?" → expected: "30 days". Extracted from generated response: "90 days". Exact match fails → QA accuracy = 0. The numeric error is caught even though BERTScore was fooled by structural similarity.
06 · Calibrating Against Human Judgments

Validate Your Automated Metrics
Before Trusting Them at Scale

Every automated evaluation metric is only as trustworthy as its correlation with human judgments on YOUR domain. A metric that works well in academic benchmarks may fail on domain-specific enterprise text. Calibration measures the correlation between automated scores and human annotator scores on the same examples — establishing whether your metric can be trusted for production decisions.

Calibration study: 200-sample protocol

Step 1: Sample 200 examples from your test set

Stratify by query type (factual, comparison, procedural) and quality level (sample from full score range, not just random). This ensures the calibration covers edge cases.

Step 2: Get 3 human annotators to score each example

Each annotator scores the dimension being calibrated (e.g., faithfulness) on a 1-5 scale using your rubric. Compute Fleiss' kappa — if < 0.4, your rubric is ambiguous, revise before proceeding.

Step 3: Compute automated metric scores on the same 200 examples

Run NLI-faithfulness, RAGAS faithfulness, and G-Eval on the same examples. Scale scores to 1-5 if needed (RAGAS 0-1 × 4 + 1).

Step 4: Compute Spearman correlation between human and automated scores

Spearman > 0.7 = metric is reliable for your domain. 0.5-0.7 = use with caution, check systematically failing examples. < 0.5 = metric is unreliable, do not use for production decisions.

08 · Combining Methods: The Multi-Layer Quality Pipeline

Fast Screening + Deep Audit:
The Production Quality Stack

multi_layer_quality_pipeline.py

from transformers import pipeline as hf_pipeline
from langchain_anthropic import ChatAnthropic
import asyncio

nli = hf_pipeline("text-classification", model="cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-base")
llm = ChatAnthropic(model="claude-haiku-4-5-20251001")  # fast model for judge

class QualityPipeline:
    """
    Layer 1: NLI screening (fast, free, runs on everything)
    Layer 2: G-Eval (sampled 10-20%, on examples where NLI is uncertain)
    Layer 3: Human review (weekly, on worst examples from Layer 2)
    """

    def nli_screen(self, response: str, context: str) -> dict:
        # Quick check: are all sentences entailed by context?
        sentences = [s.strip() for s in response.split(".") if len(s.strip()) > 10]
        scores = [nli(f"{context} [SEP] {s}")[0] for s in sentences]
        entailed = [s for s in scores if s["label"] == "ENTAILMENT"]
        faith = len(entailed) / len(scores) if scores else 1.0
        return {"faithfulness_nli": faith, "flag_for_deep": faith < 0.8}

    async def evaluate(self, question: str, response: str, context: str,
                         deep_sample_rate: float = 0.15) -> dict:
        # Layer 1: always runs
        layer1 = self.nli_screen(response, context)
        
        # Layer 2: run if flagged OR randomly sampled
        import random
        if layer1["flag_for_deep"] or random.random() < deep_sample_rate:
            layer2 = await self.g_eval_async(question, response, context)
        else:
            layer2 = {"g_eval": None, "skipped": True}
        
        return {"layer1_nli": layer1, "layer2_geval": layer2}

pipeline = QualityPipeline()

# Production usage: evaluate every response with NLI
# Deep-evaluate flagged + 15% sample with G-Eval
# Weekly: human review of bottom-10% G-Eval scores
09 · Assessment

Module 5 Quiz

1. NLI gives ENTAILMENT (prob=0.61) for: premise "Refunds processed in 5-7 business days" vs hypothesis "Refunds take about a week." Should you accept this as supported? What are the risks?

Correct. NLI confidence thresholds are domain-sensitive. The default 0.50 threshold accepts too many borderline cases. A threshold of 0.80+ for production-grade faithfulness evaluation is more conservative and appropriate for domains where precise facts matter. Borderline cases (0.50-0.80 ENTAILMENT) should be routed to a more capable judge (G-Eval or human) rather than automatically classified. The key insight: NLI confidence reflects the model's uncertainty about whether the semantic relationship is entailment — and that uncertainty should propagate to your quality score.

2. FactScore decomposes a response into 8 atomic claims. 6 are SUPPORTED, 1 is NOT FOUND (no source mentions it), 1 is CONTRADICTED by the context. How should you weight these differently, and what is the FactScore?

Correct. The distinction between NOT FOUND and CONTRADICTED is operationally important. NOT FOUND: the model adds a fact that might be true but is not in the retrieved context (hallucination risk). CONTRADICTED: the model states something the context explicitly says is false (definitive misinformation). In a legal or medical context, a contradicted claim about dosage or contract terms is a liability issue — it actively misleads the user in a way a "not found" claim may not. Treating them equivalently in a flat FactScore understates the risk of contradicted claims.

3. You run G-Eval three times on the same response and get faithfulness scores of 3, 4, and 5. The variance is 1 point per run. How do you handle this and what does it tell you about the response?

Correct. G-Eval variance is diagnostic, not just noise to average away. Clear cases (full hallucination or fully supported) produce tight variance. Borderline cases produce high variance because the rubric genuinely leaves room for different interpretations. G-Eval's own authors recommend averaging 3-5 runs precisely because of this — but they also note that high-variance examples are the ones most likely to be misclassified by automated evaluation. Routing high-variance examples to human review maximizes both efficiency (humans only review the hard cases) and accuracy (hard cases get human judgment).

4. BERTScore gives 0.89 for "The refund window is 90 days" vs reference "The refund window is 30 days." Which evaluation method would correctly catch this error, and how?

Correct. This is the canonical numeric precision failure for BERTScore. Tokens "30" and "90" occupy similar positions in embedding space (both are numbers in temporal contexts) so BERTScore sees them as similar. Three methods that DO catch this: (1) QA-based — generates "how many days?" and checks if the extracted answer matches; (2) NLI — detects CONTRADICTION when comparing "30 days" vs "90 days" in context; (3) FactScore — marks the specific claim "90 days" as CONTRADICTED. This illustrates why no single metric is sufficient: different methods catch different error types.

5. Your Spearman correlation between G-Eval faithfulness scores and human annotator scores is 0.45 on your domain. G-Eval achieves 0.75 on academic benchmarks. What does this tell you and how do you fix it?

Correct. Spearman correlation drop from 0.75 (benchmark) to 0.45 (domain) is a significant calibration failure — it means the G-Eval judge disagrees with human judgment 55% of the time on your specific domain. This is common when: (1) your domain uses specialized vocabulary the judge misinterprets as hallucination; (2) your faithfulness definition is stricter or looser than the judge's default; (3) your documents have a different structure than the judge's training distribution. Domain-specific few-shot examples in the prompt are the most effective fix — 5-10 examples of correct judgments in your domain typically bring Spearman above 0.65.
