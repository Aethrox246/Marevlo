01 · Why Retrieval Metrics Exist Separately

Evaluating the Retriever
Before the Generator Even Runs

Most RAG debugging focuses on the LLM's output. But if the retriever returns wrong documents, no amount of prompt engineering fixes the answer. Retrieval-only metrics let you isolate the retrieval component, measure it independently, and fix it before touching the generator. These metrics come from Information Retrieval (IR) literature and all require a test set with known relevant documents for each query.

What you need to compute IR metrics

Query set Q: The set of test queries — ideally 200+ sampled from real user queries or domain expert annotations
Relevance judgments R(q): For each query, the set of document IDs that are relevant. Binary (relevant / not) or graded (0=irrelevant, 1=relevant, 2=highly relevant)
Retrieved set Retrieved(q, k): The k documents returned by your retriever for query q, in ranked order

IR metric taxonomy

Set-based: Precision@K, Recall@K, Hit Rate@K — ignore ranking order within top-K
Rank-sensitive: MRR, MAP, NDCG — penalize relevant documents ranked lower
Graded relevance: NDCG only — distinguishes "highly relevant" from "somewhat relevant"
02 · Precision@K

Of the Top-K Retrieved Documents,
What Fraction Are Relevant?

Precision@K answers: "If the LLM reads the top-K documents, how much of what it reads is actually useful?" A Precision@5 of 0.4 means 2 out of 5 retrieved documents are relevant — 3 are noise that fills the context window without helping and may confuse generation.

Precision@K formula and interpretation thresholds

P@K = |{relevant docs} ∩ {top-K retrieved}| / K
Production targets: P@3 > 0.75 (high-precision contexts), P@5 > 0.60 (balanced), P@10 > 0.40 (high-recall contexts). If precision is below target: add a score_threshold to reject low-similarity chunks, use a reranker to push relevant documents to the top, or reduce k to only retrieve the most confident results.

❌ P@5 = 0.20 — dangerously noisy
Query: "How long is the free trial?"
Top 5: [✗ Annual pricing, ✓ Free trial info, ✗ Enterprise features, ✗ Cancellation policy, ✗ Support contact]

Only 1/5 chunks relevant. LLM reads 4 irrelevant documents before reaching the answer. High risk of "lost in the middle" failures. Cost: paying for 4 wasted context window tokens.
✓ P@5 = 1.00 — perfect precision
Query: "How long is the free trial?"
Top 5: [✓ Trial duration, ✓ Trial features, ✓ Trial upgrade, ✓ Trial cancellation, ✓ Trial billing]

All 5 chunks are relevant to the query. LLM has complete, focused context. Answers are complete, accurate, and efficiently generated.
03 · Recall@K

Of All Relevant Documents in the Corpus,
What Fraction Did We Find?

Recall@K answers: "Did we give the LLM all the information it needs?" A Recall@5 of 0.50 means half of the relevant documents in the entire corpus were not retrieved — the LLM will have incomplete information and cannot provide a complete answer. This directly causes the "partial answer" problem where users must ask follow-up questions.

Recall@K: same example, measuring completeness

Query: "What are all the conditions for a refund on annual plans?"
All relevant documents in corpus: Doc-14, Doc-22, Doc-31, Doc-45 (4 total)

Retriever returns k=5: Found Doc-14 ✓, Doc-22 ✓, Doc-31 ✓. Missed Doc-45 ✗ (contains: "Partial refunds not available, Enterprise accounts have different terms").

Recall@5 = 3 found / 4 total relevant = 0.75
Impact: LLM cannot mention "partial refunds not available" or enterprise exceptions because that document was never retrieved. Users asking about these specific conditions get an incomplete answer.

⚠️

The precision-recall tradeoff: how to choose k

Increasing k always improves recall (more documents → more chances to find relevant ones) but typically hurts precision (more noise added). The optimal k depends on your use case: High-stakes completeness (legal, medical): prioritize recall, accept lower precision, use reranker to push noise down. Concise factual QA: prioritize precision, use k=3-5 with high score_threshold. Most production RAG: k=5-8 with a reranker is the sweet spot.

04 · NDCG@K — Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain

Graded Relevance With
Position-Aware Scoring

NDCG (Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain) is the most sophisticated retrieval metric. Unlike Precision@K and Recall@K (which only care about whether a document is relevant), NDCG: (1) handles graded relevance (highly relevant vs somewhat relevant vs not relevant), and (2) penalizes relevant documents that are ranked too low. A highly relevant document at rank 1 contributes more than the same document at rank 5.

NDCG@5: complete step-by-step calculation

Query: "What are the enterprise pricing tiers?"
Graded relevance labels: Doc-A=2 (highly relevant: exact enterprise pricing table), Doc-B=1 (somewhat relevant: mentions enterprise briefly), Doc-C=2 (highly relevant: enterprise tier comparison), Doc-D=0 (not relevant), Doc-E=1 (somewhat relevant: enterprise features list)

Retriever returns (k=5):
Rank 1: Doc-D (relevance=0) — worst case: irrelevant doc at top
Rank 2: Doc-B (relevance=1)
Rank 3: Doc-A (relevance=2)
Rank 4: Doc-E (relevance=1)
Rank 5: Doc-C (relevance=2)
Step 1: DCG@5 = Σ (relevance_i / log₂(rank_i + 1))
= (0/log₂(2)) + (1/log₂(3)) + (2/log₂(4)) + (1/log₂(5)) + (2/log₂(6))
= 0/1.0 + 1/1.585 + 2/2.0 + 1/2.322 + 2/2.585
= 0 + 0.631 + 1.0 + 0.431 + 0.774
DCG@5 = 2.836

Step 2: IDCG@5 = DCG if ranked perfectly (2,2,1,1,0)
= (2/1.0) + (2/1.585) + (1/2.0) + (1/2.322) + (0/2.585)
= 2.0 + 1.262 + 0.5 + 0.431 + 0
IDCG@5 = 4.193

Step 3: NDCG@5 = DCG@5 / IDCG@5 = 2.836 / 4.193 = 0.676
The highly relevant docs were ranked at positions 3 and 5 instead of 1 and 2 — NDCG penalizes this. The irrelevant doc at rank 1 wasted the most valuable position.

When to use NDCG vs Precision@K

Use NDCG when: (1) you have graded relevance labels (some documents are more relevant than others), or (2) the ranking order within the retrieved set matters (because your system presents ranked results to the user, or because the LLM processes earlier chunks more attentively). Use Precision@K when: relevance is binary, all retrieved documents are passed to the LLM in a flat list, or when you want a simpler metric that is easier to explain to stakeholders.

05 · MRR — Mean Reciprocal Rank

Is the Right Answer
in First Position?

Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) focuses on a single question: how early in the ranked list does the first relevant document appear? If the first relevant document is always at rank 1, MRR = 1.0. If it is always at rank 2, MRR = 0.5. MRR is particularly useful for applications where only the top result matters — lookup tasks, command-line completions, and any system where users read only the first result.

MRR: calculation across multiple queries

Query 1: "How do I cancel?" → first relevant at rank 1 → RR = 1/1 = 1.000
Query 2: "What is the enterprise pricing?" → first relevant at rank 3 → RR = 1/3 = 0.333
Query 3: "How long is the free trial?" → first relevant at rank 2 → RR = 1/2 = 0.500
Query 4: "What happens if I miss a payment?" → first relevant at rank 5 → RR = 1/5 = 0.200
Query 5: "Can I add team members?" → first relevant at rank 1 → RR = 1/1 = 1.000

MRR = (1.000 + 0.333 + 0.500 + 0.200 + 1.000) / 5 = 3.033 / 5 = 0.607
⚠️

MRR's blind spot: it ignores everything after the first relevant document

MRR only cares about the rank of the FIRST relevant document. If you retrieve k=5 and documents at ranks 2, 3, 4, 5 are all highly relevant, MRR does not give credit for that. This makes MRR inappropriate for RAG systems where multiple relevant chunks contribute to a complete answer. Use MRR for: search autocomplete, FAQ lookup, any task where one relevant result is sufficient. Use MAP or NDCG for: multi-document RAG where completeness (all relevant docs retrieved) matters.

06 · MAP — Mean Average Precision

Comprehensive Ranking Quality
Across All Relevant Documents

MAP (Mean Average Precision) combines precision and ranking. For each query, it computes Average Precision (AP) — the mean of Precision@K at every rank where a relevant document appears. Averaged across all queries, MAP gives a comprehensive view of retrieval quality that accounts for both how many relevant documents are found and how highly they are ranked.

MAP: step-by-step calculation

Query: "What are the refund conditions?" (4 relevant docs in corpus)
Retrieved order (k=8): [✓, ✗, ✓, ✗, ✗, ✓, ✗, ✓]

Compute P@K at each relevant document position:
Rank 1: relevant → P@1 = 1/1 = 1.000
Rank 3: relevant → P@3 = 2/3 = 0.667
Rank 6: relevant → P@6 = 3/6 = 0.500
Rank 8: relevant → P@8 = 4/8 = 0.500

AP(q) = (1.000 + 0.667 + 0.500 + 0.500) / 4 = 2.667 / 4 = 0.667
(Repeat for all queries, then average → MAP)
High MAP (0.90) — all relevant docs ranked early
Retrieved: [✓, ✓, ✓, ✓, ✗, ✗, ✗, ✗]
AP = (1/1 + 2/2 + 3/3 + 4/4) / 4 = (1.0+1.0+1.0+1.0)/4 = 1.0
All 4 relevant docs in top 4 positions. Perfect retrieval.
Low MAP (0.35) — relevant docs buried at bottom
Retrieved: [✗, ✗, ✗, ✗, ✓, ✓, ✓, ✓]
AP = (1/5 + 2/6 + 3/7 + 4/8) / 4 = (0.2+0.33+0.43+0.5)/4 = 0.365
All relevant docs are at the bottom. MAP penalizes this heavily.
07 · Hit Rate@K and Building a Retrieval Eval Set

The Simplest Production Monitor
and How to Build Your Test Set

Hit Rate@K is the simplest retrieval metric: what fraction of queries have at least one relevant document in the top-K retrieved? Unlike Recall@K (which requires knowing ALL relevant docs), Hit Rate@K only requires knowing that at least one relevant doc exists. This makes it practical for rapid evaluation when full relevance annotation is expensive.

Hit Rate@K: calculation and thresholds

Hit Rate@5 = queries where ≥1 relevant doc in top-5 / total queries

Example across 200 queries: 174 have ≥1 relevant in top-5 → Hit Rate@5 = 174/200 = 0.870

Production targets:
Hit Rate@1 > 0.60 → acceptable for single-result lookup
Hit Rate@3 > 0.75 → acceptable for RAG with k=3
Hit Rate@5 > 0.85 → target for RAG with k=5
Hit Rate@10 > 0.95 → minimum for any RAG system

If Hit Rate@5 is below 0.85: the retriever is completely missing the answer for 15%+ of queries. This is a fundamental document coverage or embedding problem — not fixable by prompt engineering.

Building a retrieval evaluation set: 3 methods

Method 1: Synthetic (fastest)

For each document chunk, generate 3 questions that are answered by that chunk. Each (question, chunk_id) pair is a positive relevance judgment. Cost: ~$0.001 per chunk. 1000 chunks → 3000 QA pairs in 30 minutes.

Method 2: Search log mining (most realistic)

Extract queries from production logs. For each query, annotate which document the user found useful (click data or human review). Requires production system running but produces most realistic query distribution.

Method 3: Expert annotation (highest quality)

Domain experts write queries and identify which document chunks answer them, with graded relevance scores (0/1/2). Most expensive (5 min/query × 300 queries = 25 hours) but produces the highest quality eval set for final certification.

08 · Complete Python Implementation

All IR Metrics
in One Evaluation Script

retrieval_evaluation.py

import math
from typing import list

def precision_at_k(retrieved: list[str], relevant: set[str], k: int) -> float:
    """P@K = |relevant ∩ top_k| / k"""
    top_k = retrieved[:k]
    return sum(1 for doc in top_k if doc in relevant) / k

def recall_at_k(retrieved: list[str], relevant: set[str], k: int) -> float:
    """R@K = |relevant ∩ top_k| / |relevant|"""
    if not relevant: return 1.0
    top_k = retrieved[:k]
    return sum(1 for doc in top_k if doc in relevant) / len(relevant)

def hit_rate_at_k(retrieved: list[str], relevant: set[str], k: int) -> float:
    """1 if any relevant doc in top-k, else 0"""
    return float(any(doc in relevant for doc in retrieved[:k]))

def reciprocal_rank(retrieved: list[str], relevant: set[str]) -> float:
    """RR = 1 / rank_of_first_relevant"""
    for i, doc in enumerate(retrieved, 1):
        if doc in relevant: return 1.0 / i
    return 0.0

def average_precision(retrieved: list[str], relevant: set[str]) -> float:
    """AP = mean of P@k at each rank where a relevant doc appears"""
    if not relevant: return 0.0
    hits, cum_precision = 0, 0.0
    for i, doc in enumerate(retrieved, 1):
        if doc in relevant:
            hits += 1
            cum_precision += hits / i
    return cum_precision / len(relevant)

def ndcg_at_k(retrieved: list[str], relevance_grades: dict[str, int], k: int) -> float:
    """NDCG@K with graded relevance (0, 1, or 2)"""
    def dcg(ranked): return sum(
        relevance_grades.get(doc, 0) / math.log2(i+2)
        for i, doc in enumerate(ranked[:k])
    )
    actual_dcg = dcg(retrieved)
    ideal = sorted(relevance_grades.keys(),
                    key=lambda d: -relevance_grades[d])
    ideal_dcg = dcg(ideal)
    return actual_dcg / ideal_dcg if ideal_dcg > 0 else 0.0

def evaluate_retriever(test_cases: list[dict], k: int = 5) -> dict:
    """
    test_cases: list of {
        "query": str,
        "retrieved": [doc_id, ...],  # ranked order
        "relevant": [doc_id, ...],   # ground truth relevant ids
        "grades": {doc_id: 0|1|2}   # optional graded relevance
    }
    """
    metrics = {"P@k":[], "R@k":[], "HR@k":[], "RR":[], "AP":[], "NDCG@k":[]}
    for tc in test_cases:
        relevant = set(tc["relevant"])
        retrieved = tc["retrieved"]
        metrics["P@k"].append(precision_at_k(retrieved, relevant, k))
        metrics["R@k"].append(recall_at_k(retrieved, relevant, k))
        metrics["HR@k"].append(hit_rate_at_k(retrieved, relevant, k))
        metrics["RR"].append(reciprocal_rank(retrieved, relevant))
        metrics["AP"].append(average_precision(retrieved, relevant))
        if "grades" in tc:
            metrics["NDCG@k"].append(ndcg_at_k(retrieved, tc["grades"], k))
    return {m: sum(v)/len(v) for m, v in metrics.items() if v}

# Example:
test = [{"query": "refund policy",
          "retrieved": ["D14","D07","D22","D55","D31"],
          "relevant": ["D14","D22","D31","D45"],
          "grades": {"D14":2, "D22":1, "D31":2, "D45":2}}]
print(evaluate_retriever(test, k=5))
# {"P@k":0.60, "R@k":0.75, "HR@k":1.00, "RR":1.00, "AP":0.64, "NDCG@k":0.73}
09 · Assessment

Module 4 Quiz

1. Your RAG system retrieves k=5 documents. Precision@5 = 0.80 and Recall@5 = 0.40. Users complain answers are always accurate but incomplete. Which metric explains the user experience and what is the fix?

Correct. High Precision + Low Recall is the "accurate but partial" retrieval pattern. The retriever is very good at finding relevant documents when it does find them (4 out of 5 retrieved are relevant = high quality signal). But it is only finding 40% of the relevant documents that exist — so the LLM has accurate but incomplete context. Increasing k is the immediate fix. The deeper fix is usually improving chunking: if related information about a topic is split across many small chunks, the retriever may find some but not others.

2. Why does NDCG penalize a retrieval where Doc-A (relevance=2, highly relevant) appears at rank 5 instead of rank 1, even though P@5 would be the same?

Correct. The logarithmic discount is the core mechanism of NDCG. The discount factor 1/log₂(rank+1) decreases rapidly with rank: rank 1 discount = 1.0, rank 2 = 0.631, rank 3 = 0.5, rank 4 = 0.431, rank 5 = 0.387. A highly relevant document at rank 5 contributes 0.774 gain; the same document at rank 1 contributes 2.0 — a 2.58x difference. This reflects the real user experience: users read the top result first and extract more value from highly relevant information presented early.

3. You run MRR = 0.95 on your search system, which seems excellent. But user satisfaction surveys show 40% of users cannot find what they need. What is MRR missing?

Correct. This is MRR's most critical limitation for RAG systems. MRR was designed for single-answer retrieval tasks (look up one fact, get one answer). It excels at measuring those systems. But RAG systems typically need multiple relevant chunks to construct a complete answer. A user asking "What are all the refund conditions?" needs chunks about the time window, the submission process, the processing time, and the exceptions. MRR = 0.95 says "the first of those chunks was always at rank 1" — but says nothing about the other 3.

4. You build a synthetic evaluation set by generating questions from chunks. Your retrieval metrics look excellent (P@5 = 0.90, R@5 = 0.95). But when you test with real user queries from production logs, P@5 drops to 0.55. What explains this discrepancy?

Correct. This is the "synthetic eval set overfit" problem. LLM-generated questions are created from the chunk content and naturally echo the vocabulary and framing of that content. When you retrieve against these questions, the embedding similarity is high because the query and the chunk are paraphrases of each other. Real users phrase questions differently — more conversationally, using domain-specific but document-divergent vocabulary. Always validate synthetic eval set performance against at least 50-100 real user queries before relying on it as your primary quality metric.

5. Your team debates whether to use k=5 or k=10 for your customer support RAG system. P@5=0.80, Recall@5=0.60; P@10=0.50, Recall@10=0.95. The domain is insurance claims where missing information has legal consequences. Which k should you choose and why?

Correct. The recall vs precision tradeoff is domain-specific. In legal, medical, and insurance contexts, false negatives (missing critical information) are more costly than false positives (noisy context). A 40% recall gap means that 40% of the time the system gives an incomplete answer about coverage, exclusions, or conditions — potentially causing incorrect claims decisions. The practical solution combines high k for recall with a reranker for precision: retrieve k=10 (ensuring recall=0.95), then rerank to present the most relevant chunks first, reducing the "lost in the middle" risk from the noise.
