Named Entity Recognition for Documents

IOB2 Labelling · LayoutLMv3 Classifier · CRF Layer · Subword Handling · Label Expansion

Named Entity Recognition is how document AI systems find and classify the information they need to extract. On a form, NER identifies which tokens are dates, amounts, addresses, and company names. On a legal contract, it finds parties, obligations, and deadlines. The technical substrate — IOB2 labelling, token classification heads, CRF layers, subword handling — is the same across document types. Understanding these mechanisms lets you diagnose failures, expand label sets, and design robust extraction pipelines.

01 · IOB2 Labelling — The Sequence of Labels Behind NER

B, I, O — How Every Token
Gets Its Role

Named entity recognition converts a sequence of text tokens into a sequence of labels. IOB2 (Inside-Outside-Beginning, version 2) is the standard labelling scheme: every token is either the Beginning of an entity (B-TYPE), Inside a continuation of an entity (I-TYPE), or Outside any entity (O). The scheme must handle adjacent entities of the same type, subword tokenisation, and multi-token spans — all of which have specific solutions.

IOB2 vs IO vs BIOES — why IOB2 is the standard

IO labelling (no B marker) cannot represent adjacent entities of the same type — two consecutive DATE spans merge into one. IOB2 solves this with the B marker. BIOES adds End (E) and Single (S) markers, giving the model explicit span boundary information at the cost of a larger label set. For transformer-based models, IOB2 is almost universally preferred — transformers learn span boundaries implicitly through attention, so the additional BIOES markers provide marginal benefit while increasing label space complexity.

02 · Document NER Pipeline

From Document Image to
Labelled Entity Spans with Bounding Boxes

LayoutLMv3 for NER adds a linear classification head on top of the encoder. Each text token's 768-dim representation is projected to a logit vector of size |IOB2_labels| and a softmax produces per-token label probabilities. Because LayoutLMv3 works on OCR tokens with known bounding boxes, every extracted entity span automatically inherits the bounding box of its constituent tokens — document NER produces spatially localised extractions.

Question 1 of 5

A sequence "B-DATE I-DATE B-DATE I-DATE" appears in NER output for the text "15 March 2024 Invoice". Is this valid IOB2 and what does it mean?

Correct. IOB2's key feature is that B marks a new entity start unconditionally. This handles the case of adjacent entities of the same type: "15 March" (invoice date) immediately followed by "2024" as a separate entity (payment year) can be represented as B-DATE I-DATE B-DATE I-DATE. Without the B marker, a simpler IO scheme would merge all adjacent DATE tokens into one span, losing the entity boundary information.
Question 2 of 5

You are fine-tuning LayoutLMv3 for form NER on FUNSD. The model achieves F1=0.89 on the validation set. However, you notice that the entity type ANSWER (the value filled in by a form respondent) achieves F1=0.71, while QUESTION (the printed field label) achieves F1=0.94. Why is ANSWER harder to detect and what can you do?

Correct. The semantic and visual variability of ANSWER entities is the root cause. QUESTION labels are stereotyped — you can almost predict what form labels look like. ANSWER content is open-ended and visually diverse. The solutions target different aspects of this variability: data augmentation addresses visual diversity, oversampling addresses vocabulary diversity, and auxiliary objectives create additional training signal for the underperforming entity type.
Question 3 of 5

A CRF (Conditional Random Field) layer is sometimes added on top of the transformer token classifier for NER. The CRF defines transition probabilities between label states (e.g. O→B-DATE is valid but O→I-DATE is invalid). For a LayoutLMv3-based document NER system, when is adding a CRF actually worthwhile?

Correct. The CRF benefit diminishes as the transformer model gets larger and training data increases. The transformer's self-attention learns label co-occurrence patterns implicitly — a well-trained model rarely predicts O→I violations. CRF adds a global sequence normalisation step that catches residual violations, and is most impactful when training data is small (the model hasn't seen enough examples to learn all valid transitions) or when there are many entity types with complex adjacency rules.
Question 4 of 5

Your document NER system labels tokens with entity types {KEY, VALUE, OTHER} for form field extraction. A form contains: 'Date: 15 March 2024'. The model correctly labels 'Date' as KEY and '15 March 2024' as three VALUE tokens. But 'March' is subword-tokenised to ['Mar', '##ch']. How should you handle the IOB2 label assignment for subword tokens?

Correct. The first-subword labelling strategy is the universal standard for subword tokenisation with IOB2 NER. Assigning B-VALUE to continuation subwords ('##ch') would be semantically incorrect and contaminate the label sequence. The -100 ignore index in PyTorch/HuggingFace cross-entropy loss excludes these positions from gradient computation. At inference, entity spans are reconstructed by: (1) taking only first-subword predictions; (2) merging consecutive I tokens with their B token to form spans; (3) mapping subword character offsets back to word boundaries.
Question 5 of 5

You need to add a new entity type SIGNATURE to your document NER model, which already recognises {DATE, AMOUNT, ADDRESS, COMPANY, PERSON}. You have 80 annotated examples with SIGNATURE labels. The existing model was trained on 2,000 examples across the 5 original types. What is the best strategy?

Correct. Mixed-batch fine-tuning with stratified original examples is the standard approach for continual NER label expansion. Retraining from scratch wastes the 2,000 original examples and compute. Fine-tuning on 80 new examples alone causes catastrophic forgetting — the classifier forgets the decision boundaries for the original 5 types after gradient updates from only the new type. The mixing ratio (5-10× oversampling new examples, or equivalently undersampling original examples) balances stability (preserving original types) and plasticity (learning the new type).
