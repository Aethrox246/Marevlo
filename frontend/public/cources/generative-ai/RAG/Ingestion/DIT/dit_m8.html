Efficient Transformers for Long Documents

Longformer · BigBird · Sliding Window · Global Tokens · Random Attention · O(n) Complexity

Standard transformers hit a hard wall at 512 tokens — most legal contracts, research papers, and annual reports far exceed this limit. Efficient attention models (Longformer, BigBird) extend the context window to 4,096+ tokens using sparse attention patterns that trade full cross-token attention for structured approximations. Understanding the specific trade-offs — what information each pattern captures, what it misses, and when the approximation is sufficient — is essential for choosing the right architecture for long document tasks.

01 · The Long Document Problem

From 512 Tokens to 4,096+ —
Sparse Attention Patterns

Standard transformers compute attention between all n² token pairs. At n=512, this is manageable. At n=4,096 (a 15-page contract), the attention matrix is 64× larger — exceeding GPU memory for typical batch sizes. Efficient attention models solve this by replacing full attention with structured sparse patterns that cover the same information at O(n) or O(n·w) cost.

▸ Longformer sliding window + global attention — local context + full-document reach
🔑

Global tokens are the key design decision

Local window attention gives each token context from its neighbours. Global tokens give specific tokens full-document reach. The design question is: which tokens need to see the whole document? For DocVQA: question tokens. For classification: [CLS]. For clause extraction: section boundary tokens. Choosing the right global tokens determines whether the sparse attention pattern captures the information needed for the task.

Question 1 of 5

A standard BERT model processes a legal contract that is 8,000 tokens long. BERT's maximum context is 512 tokens. What are the two main strategies for handling this, and what information does each lose?

Correct. Both workaround strategies lose cross-document attention — sliding window loses cross-chunk dependencies, hierarchical encoding loses token-level cross-document attention. These losses are real: in contracts, a liability clause in chapter 8 may reference a definition in chapter 2 that spans the chunk boundary. Efficient attention models (Longformer, BigBird) solve this by computing sparse attention patterns that cover the full sequence, trading exact global attention for approximate but full-document coverage.
Question 2 of 5

Longformer designates certain tokens as 'global' — they attend to all tokens and all tokens attend to them. In a DocVQA application on a 4,000-token legal contract, which tokens should be designated as global and why?

Correct. Question tokens as global is the key design decision for DocVQA with Longformer. The reasoning is sound: the question must 'reach' every document token to find the answer, but document tokens only need local context to encode their own meaning. Making question tokens global achieves this at O(n_question × n_document) cost — linear in document length — rather than the O(n²) cost of full attention. This is the exact design used in LED (Longformer Encoder-Decoder) and Long-DocVQA systems.
Question 3 of 5

BigBird uses random attention in addition to local window and global tokens. Why is random attention beneficial even though randomly sampled pairs seem arbitrary?

Correct. The graph-theoretic argument is the key insight: random edges in a sparse attention graph create short paths between any pair of tokens. Local edges cover nearby tokens; global tokens cover the whole sequence; random edges bridge the remaining long-range pairs. The combination ensures the sparse attention graph has small diameter — information can flow between any two positions in O(1) attention steps. This is why BigBird provably matches full-attention expressivity despite using only O(n) attention entries per layer.
Question 4 of 5

You are processing 50-page annual reports (approximately 25,000 tokens each) for financial field extraction. Neither Longformer (4,096 max) nor BigBird (4,096 max) can handle the full document. What is the most practical production approach?

Correct. Hierarchical retrieval + extraction is the production-standard approach for documents that exceed any single model's context window. The key insight is that field extraction is inherently local — revenue figures appear in the income statement, not distributed throughout the entire 50-page document. Section detection localises the extraction problem. Retrieval selects the right section. Extraction operates on a bounded context. This is functionally equivalent to how a human analyst reads an annual report: they go to the income statement for P&L figures, the balance sheet for assets, not reading every page sequentially.
Question 5 of 5

A Longformer model (4,096 tokens) is fine-tuned for clause NER on legal contracts. You observe that clauses appearing after token position 3,000 have 15% lower F1 than clauses in the first 1,000 tokens. What causes this and how do you fix it?

Correct. Annotation density imbalance is the most common cause of position-dependent performance drops in NER on long documents. Legal annotation datasets reflect the annotator's tendency to focus on commercially important early clauses. The model learns to associate 'early position' with 'important clause' rather than learning position-invariant clause detection. Position-stratified training corrects this by ensuring equal annotation coverage across all document positions during training. This is a diagnostic check that should be run on any NER model showing position-correlated performance drops.
