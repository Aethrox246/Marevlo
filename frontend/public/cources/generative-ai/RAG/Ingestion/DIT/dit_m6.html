Document Visual Question Answering

Extractive vs Abstractive · ANLS · InfographicVQA · Multi-Hop · Per-Category Evaluation

Document Visual Question Answering is the most open-ended document AI task: given a document image and a natural language question, return the answer. Unlike NER (fixed entity types) or extraction (fixed field schema), DocVQA handles arbitrary questions about document content, structure, and meaning. This module covers the extractive span-finding architecture, ANLS as the evaluation metric, why infographic VQA requires chart-specific reasoning, multi-hop question answering over multi-page documents, and how aggregate metrics can mislead production deployment decisions.

01 · Document VQA — Architecture and Formulation

Question + Document → Answer:
Two Approaches, Different Trade-offs

Document VQA systems take two forms. Extractive models find the answer as a text span within the document — the answer must exist verbatim in the text. Abstractive models generate the answer — they can synthesise from multiple document parts or reason beyond what is written. Extractive models are more reliable and verifiable; abstractive models handle a broader class of questions. Most production systems use extractive approaches for structured documents and reserve abstractive generation for open-ended analytical queries.

Why bidirectional question-document attention matters

In a standard LayoutLMv3 VQA setup, question tokens and document tokens are concatenated into one sequence separated by [SEP]. Every question token can attend to every document token, and vice versa. This means "total amount" in the question directly influences the attention weights over document tokens — the model learns to attend to tokens near financial summary rows when it sees "total" in the question context. This bidirectional interaction is what enables zero-shot generalisation to unseen question phrasings.

02 · ANLS — The Right Metric for Document VQA

Why Exact Match Fails
and ANLS Measures What Matters

Document VQA answers are strings extracted from OCR output. OCR introduces character-level noise — commas and periods swapped in numbers, hyphens dropped from dates, trailing spaces included or excluded. Exact match would score these minor OCR variations as zero correct, severely penalising otherwise correct extractions. ANLS (Average Normalised Levenshtein Similarity) rewards near-correct answers proportionally to their character-level accuracy.

Question 1 of 5

DocVQA uses an extractive approach — the answer must be a text span from the document. A question asks 'What is the document's main conclusion?' on a 10-page research report. Why does the extractive formulation struggle here, and what alternative approach handles it?

Correct. The extractive formulation is fundamentally limited to questions where the answer exists verbatim in the document. Analytical, synthesising, or comparative questions ('What is the trend?', 'What changed from Q1 to Q2?', 'What is the main argument?') require generating an answer from multiple document parts — the exact answer string does not exist anywhere in the document. This is why InfographicVQA and multi-hop document reasoning benchmarks require generative approaches beyond the span-extraction paradigm.
Question 2 of 5

A DocVQA model is evaluated with ANLS (Average Normalised Levenshtein Similarity). The ground truth answer is '£1,234.56'. Your model predicts '1,234.56' (missing the £ symbol). Calculate the approximate ANLS score and explain whether this is a serious error.

Correct. ANLS = 1 - (edit_distance / max_length) = 1 - (1/9) ≈ 0.89. The calculation confirms that ANLS handles this case gracefully — a single missing character earns most of the score rather than zero. This is the design intent: OCR systems sometimes omit currency symbols, quotation marks, or other prefix/suffix characters. ANLS ensures these minor OCR variations don't unfairly penalise otherwise correct extractions. For production systems, ANLS serves as a proxy for 'how useful is this extraction?' rather than 'is this character-perfect?'
Question 3 of 5

On InfographicVQA, a model must answer 'What percentage of respondents preferred option B in Q3?' from a bar chart infographic. The chart shows percentages as bar heights with numeric labels. What makes this fundamentally harder than standard DocVQA on text-based documents?

Correct. The fundamental challenge is visual grounding of chart elements — not OCR accuracy or vocabulary. A model answering infographic questions must trace a multi-hop path through visual structure: question keyword → chart element type → specific element → value. This requires understanding chart-specific visual conventions (x-axis = categories, y-axis = values, bar colour = series, legend = colour-to-series mapping) that are learned from chart-specific training data, not from general document pretraining. This is why InfographicVQA models are typically trained on chart-specific datasets (ChartQA, FigureQA) in addition to or instead of general document VQA data.
Question 4 of 5

A multi-hop DocVQA question asks: 'How much more did the company spend on R&D in FY2023 compared to FY2022?' The R&D spend figures are in two different tables on page 4 and page 7 of a 12-page annual report. Describe the retrieval and reasoning chain required.

Correct. The three-stage retrieve-extract-reason pipeline is the standard approach. The key insight is that this question requires two extraction steps (R&D FY2023, R&D FY2022) plus one computation step (subtraction) — no single text span contains the answer. Stage 1 (question understanding) identifies this multi-hop structure. Stage 2 (retrieval) brings the relevant context into the context window. Stage 3 (reasoning) performs the computation. Each stage can fail independently: retrieval may fetch the wrong table (revenue vs R&D), extraction may read the wrong row, reasoning may confuse the subtraction direction. Per-stage evaluation is essential for diagnosing which stage drives errors.
Question 5 of 5

You are evaluating two DocVQA models on a financial document test set. Model A achieves ANLS=0.81. Model B achieves ANLS=0.79. Model A's errors are concentrated on numeric questions (dates, amounts) while Model B's errors are more uniform. Which model should you prefer for a production invoice extraction system, and why does ANLS alone not provide sufficient information?

Correct. Per-category performance analysis is essential for domain-specific deployment decisions. ANLS=0.81 overall means nothing if the 0.02 advantage over Model B comes from better performance on text-heavy fields (addresses, company names) while numeric accuracy is lower. For invoice extraction, numeric errors are critical failures — a wrong amount or date causes downstream financial errors. Model B's uniform errors may actually include better numeric accuracy despite lower overall ANLS. This is the production evaluation principle: always decompose aggregate metrics by the categories that matter for your specific use case.
