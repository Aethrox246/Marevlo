Reranking:
Cross-Encoders, Cohere, and LLM Judges

Retrieval maximizes recall — getting the right documents into the candidate set. Reranking maximizes precision — putting the best documents first so the LLM generates from the highest-quality evidence. This module covers cross-encoder architecture, training losses, every production reranker, and LLM reranking patterns.

01 · Why Retrieval Is Not Enough

Recall vs Precision:
The Fundamental Tension

Retrieval systems are optimized for recall: find as many relevant documents as possible from a large corpus at low latency. But recall is not precision. The top-100 candidates may contain the right answer, but if that answer sits at rank 47 and you pass only top-5 to the LLM, the generation is wrong. Reranking applies a slower but far more accurate model to re-score the shortlist and fix the ordering.

Concrete failure: correct answer at rank #4, only top-3 passed to LLM

Query: "What is the refund policy for annual subscriptions if Marevlo discontinues service?"

#1 "Marevlo offers a 30-day free trial on all plans."  Mentions trials, not relevant 0.82
#2 "Annual subscriptions include priority support and API access."  Annual, not refunds 0.79
#3 "Cancellation before 30 days results in a full refund."  Refunds but wrong scenario 0.77
#4 "If Marevlo discontinues service, annual subscribers receive prorated refunds for unused months." — THE answer 0.74
⚠️

The problem in practice

The correct answer is ranked #4. If only top-3 are passed to the LLM, it never sees the answer and either hallucinates or says "I don't know." A cross-encoder reranker sees the query and all candidates together, applies deep attention over (query, document) pairs, and correctly promotes document #4 to rank #1 because it is the only one that addresses both "annual subscriptions" AND "service discontinuation."

02 · The 3-Stage Retrieval Pipeline

Architecture: Recall Then Precision
With Latency Budget Allocation

A production RAG retrieval pipeline has three stages, each optimizing a different objective. First-stage retrieval maximizes recall at low latency across the full corpus. Second-stage reranking maximizes precision on the shortlist. Third-stage LLM context selection is optional for high-stakes applications.

Full 3-stage pipeline with latency budgets

Stage 1: Retrieval (recall) — 20-50ms
BM25 + Dense hybrid search → top-100 candidates from 1M+ documents. Uses pre-computed document vectors (offline) and ANN index. Goal: capture every relevant document in the shortlist.
↓ pass top-100 candidates
Stage 2: Reranking (precision) — 200-800ms
Cross-encoder scores each (query, candidate) pair with full transformer attention. Cannot pre-compute: query must be present at encoding time. Returns top-5 to top-10 highly relevant documents with calibrated scores.
↓ pass top-5 to LLM
Stage 3: LLM generation (optional judge) — 1-5s
LLM receives final 3-10 chunks. Optional: a 3rd-stage LLM reranker applies chain-of-thought relevance reasoning before generation. Adds latency but catches cases cross-encoder misses on subtle reasoning.
💡

Why 3 stages instead of a better single-stage retriever?

Cross-encoders see query and document together — this is what makes them accurate. But this means you cannot pre-compute document scores: every (query, doc) pair requires a fresh forward pass. Scoring 1M documents per query would take hours. The cascade architecture is the only way to achieve both recall (stage 1) and precision (stage 2) at production scale and latency.

03 · Cross-Encoder Architecture

Joint Attention: How Cross-Encoders
See Query and Document Together

A cross-encoder concatenates query and document as a single input: [CLS] query [SEP] document [SEP]. The full transformer self-attention operates over this combined sequence, allowing every query token to attend to every document token. This joint attention is what enables cross-encoders to detect temporal mismatches ("after 30 days" vs "within 30 days"), negations, and conditional relevance that bi-encoders miss completely.

Bi-encoder (retriever): independent encoding

query: "refund after 30 days" → BERT → [0.80, 0.20, ...]
doc A: "refund within 30 days" → BERT → [0.78, 0.22, ...]
score = dot(q, d_A) = 0.97 — both look like "refund + 30 days"

Query and document encoded independently. Pooling loses the directional context. "After" vs "within" becomes invisible in the averaged 768-dim vector. Both look equally relevant.

Cross-encoder (reranker): joint encoding

input: [CLS] refund after 30 days [SEP]
refund within 30 days policy [SEP]
"after" attends to "within" → temporal mismatch detected
score = linear(h_[CLS]) = 0.31 — low relevance

The transformer layer connecting "after" in the query to "within" in the document learns they are opposite temporal conditions. The cross-encoder correctly assigns low relevance. Bi-encoder cannot see this interaction.

What cross-encoders catch that bi-encoders miss

Negation: Query "plans without annual contracts" → Doc "all plans require annual contracts." Bi-encoder: both about annual contracts, high score. Cross-encoder: "without" attends to "require", opposite meaning, low score.
Conditional relevance: Query "refund for enterprise accounts" → Doc "individual accounts are non-refundable, enterprise accounts have custom terms." Bi-encoder misses the conditional. Cross-encoder attends to "enterprise" aligning in both.
Specificity: Query "refund for subscription cancelled after 45 days" → Doc A covers the 30-day period only. Bi-encoder: both are about subscription refunds. Cross-encoder: 45 days falls outside the 30-day policy, low relevance for the specific question.
04 · Training Cross-Encoders

Pointwise, Pairwise, Listwise:
Three Loss Paradigms

Cross-encoders can be trained with three different objective functions. The choice depends on what labeled data you have and whether you need absolute relevance scores or just relative ranking order.

Pointwise Loss

Labels each (query, doc) pair independently with a relevance score (0=irrelevant, 1=relevant, or multi-level 0-3). Uses cross-entropy or MSE against the label.

L = CE(sigmoid(score), label)

Pros: simple, works with binary annotation. Cons: does not optimize relative ordering. Two relevant docs can both score 0.9 with no ordering information.

Pairwise Loss (recommended)

For each query, compare pairs of docs where one is more relevant. Train to score the positive doc higher than the negative.

L = −log(sigmoid(s+ − s−))

Directly optimizes ranking order. Works perfectly with click-through data (clicked A over B). Standard approach for domain fine-tuning. Used in RLHF preference training.

Listwise Loss

Scores the entire candidate list for each query simultaneously, matching the full ground-truth relevance ordering.

L = −Σ yi log(softmax(scores)i)

Globally optimizes the ranking. Requires full relevance labels for all candidates per query — expensive to annotate. Used when distilling from GPT-4 full-list judgments.

💡

Which loss to use when fine-tuning on your domain data

Click-through logs or human preference pairs ("A is better than B") → pairwise loss. Binary relevance labels from annotation (relevant/not) → pointwise. Distillation from a GPT-4 judge ranking full candidate sets → listwise. For most domain adaptation projects, pairwise is the practical choice because preference pairs are cheapest to collect: show annotators two candidates, ask which one better answers the query.

06 · LLM-as-Reranker

GPT-4 and Claude as
Fine-Grained Relevance Judges

Cross-encoders miss subtle reasoning: "This document discusses refunds for B2B enterprise contracts, but the user is asking about consumer annual subscriptions." Large LLMs reason about such distinctions with chain-of-thought. LLM reranking adds 500ms-3s of latency but catches cases cross-encoders consistently get wrong.

Pattern 1: Pointwise scoring (1 call per candidate)

System: "Rate document relevance to query on 1-10."
User: "Query: [query] Document: [document] Score:"

Run N calls in parallel. Sort by score. Simple and interpretable. Latency: N x API latency. Cost: N x per-token cost.

Pattern 2: Listwise sliding window (1 call per window)

System: "Rerank these N documents for the query. Return doc IDs in order of relevance."
User: "Query: [query] Doc 1: [...] Doc 2: [...] ..."

Window slides with overlap (window=10, step=5). More accurate, fewer calls. Implementation: LangChain RankGPT.
⚠️

When LLM reranking is worth the latency cost

Use LLM reranking when: the task is high-stakes (medical, legal, financial decisions), a cross-encoder is already deployed but evaluation shows measurable quality gaps, or you have fewer than 20 candidates to rescore. Skip it for high-throughput consumer applications where latency matters more than marginal quality improvement and cross-encoder quality is sufficient.

08 · Production Code

Cohere, BGE-Reranker, and
LangChain ContextualCompression

reranking_production.py

# ── Option 1: Cohere Rerank API (no infra, managed) ──────────
import cohere

co = cohere.Client("YOUR_COHERE_API_KEY")
query = "What is the refund policy for annual subscriptions?"
candidates = [
    "Annual subscriptions are non-refundable after 30 days.",
    "Monthly plans can be cancelled any time with no penalty.",
    "Refund requests require your subscription ID and purchase date.",
    "Enterprise accounts have custom refund terms negotiated at signup.",
]

results = co.rerank(
    query=query, documents=candidates,
    model="rerank-v3.5", top_n=3, return_documents=True
)
for r in results.results:
    print(f"[{r.relevance_score:.4f}] {r.document.text[:70]}")

# ── Option 2: BGE-reranker (self-hosted, open source) ─────────
from sentence_transformers import CrossEncoder

reranker = CrossEncoder(
    "BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3",
    max_length=512,
    device="cuda"      # use "cpu" for ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2
)
pairs = [(query, doc) for doc in candidates]
scores = reranker.predict(pairs, batch_size=32)
ranked = sorted(zip(candidates, scores), key=lambda x: -x[1])
for doc, score in ranked:
    print(f"[{score:.4f}] {doc[:70]}")

# ── Option 3: LangChain ContextualCompressionRetriever ────────
from langchain.retrievers import ContextualCompressionRetriever
from langchain.retrievers.document_compressors import CohereRerank
from langchain.retrievers import EnsembleRetriever
from langchain_community.retrievers import BM25Retriever
from langchain_community.vectorstores import Qdrant
from langchain_community.embeddings import HuggingFaceEmbeddings
from langchain.schema import Document

docs = [Document(page_content=c) for c in candidates]
embeddings = HuggingFaceEmbeddings(model_name="BAAI/bge-large-en-v1.5",
    encode_kwargs={"normalize_embeddings": True},
    query_instruction="Represent this sentence for searching relevant passages: ")
vectorstore = Qdrant.from_documents(docs, embeddings,
    location=":memory:", collection_name="docs")
bm25 = BM25Retriever.from_documents(docs, k=10)
hybrid = EnsembleRetriever(
    retrievers=[bm25, vectorstore.as_retriever(search_kwargs={"k": 10})],
    weights=[0.4, 0.6])

# Wrap hybrid retriever with Cohere reranker
# Retrieves top-10, reranks and returns top-3
pipeline = ContextualCompressionRetriever(
    base_compressor=CohereRerank(
        cohere_api_key="YOUR_KEY",
        model="rerank-v3.5",
        top_n=3
    ),
    base_retriever=hybrid
)
final_docs = pipeline.invoke("annual subscription refund policy")

# ── LLM pointwise reranker (works with any LangChain LLM) ─────
def llm_rerank(query: str, docs: list, llm) -> list:
    scored = []
    prompt_template = (
        "Rate relevance 1-10.
Query: {q}
Document: {d}
Score:"
    )
    for doc in docs:
        prompt = prompt_template.format(
            q=query, d=doc.page_content[:500])
        score = int(llm.invoke(prompt).content.strip())
        scored.append((doc, score))
    return [d for d, _ in sorted(scored, key=lambda x: -x[1])]
09 · Assessment

Module 8 Quiz

1. Your hybrid retriever returns the correct answer at rank #8 out of 50 candidates, but you only pass top-5 to the LLM. The retriever has recall@50=100%. What is the problem and how does reranking fix it?

Correct. Recall vs precision is the key distinction. Recall@50=100% means the retriever found the right document (it is in the top-50). But it is at rank #8. Reranking operates on the already-retrieved set to improve precision (the ranking order). If the answer were at rank #51, no reranker could help — you would need to improve recall first by increasing retriever top-k or using a better retrieval model.

2. A cross-encoder detects that "Refunds available within 30 days" is NOT relevant to "Can I get a refund after 30 days?" but a bi-encoder misses this. What architectural property enables this?

Correct. The joint self-attention is the key property. When "after" in the query and "within" in the document appear in the same input sequence, every attention head in the transformer can attend from one to the other. Bi-encoders produce independent vectors where both "after 30 days" and "within 30 days" get similar representations for "temporal refund policy" without preserving the directional difference.

3. You are fine-tuning BGE-reranker on your domain data. You have click-through logs: for each query, users clicked some documents over others. Which training loss is most appropriate?

Correct. Click data is inherently pairwise preference data: the user clicked A over B, expressing a preference. Pairwise loss trains the reranker to score A above B for that query type. Pointwise loss would treat all non-clicked docs as irrelevant (label=0), which is wrong — a user may not have seen or reached other relevant documents. Pairwise avoids this assumption by only using the explicit preference signals.

4. BGE-reranker-v2-m3 has a 512-token context limit. Your legal documents average 3000 tokens. What is the best approach?

Correct. Legal documents often have the key clause (force majeure, liability cap, termination terms) buried in the latter half. Truncating at 512 tokens discards most of the document. Jina's 8192 context handles 3000-token documents natively. The chunk-level approach is a solid alternative: the document's most relevant 500-token segment gets promoted, which is often better than feeding the entire 3000-token document to any reranker. Note: Cohere Rerank has a 4096-token limit per document, not unlimited.

5. You add a cross-encoder reranker to an existing RAG system. You pass the same 5 documents to the LLM before and after reranking. Why does generation quality improve even though the count is the same?

Correct. "Lost in the middle" (Liu et al. 2023) showed that LLMs perform worse when relevant information is surrounded by irrelevant content in the context window. Before reranking: the top-5 might include 2-3 genuinely relevant docs and 2-3 tangentially related noise. After reranking: all 5 are highly relevant. The LLM now generates from dense, high-quality evidence rather than diluted context, which reduces hallucination rates and improves specificity.
