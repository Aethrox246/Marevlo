Why We Need Multiple Variables

Real life is multivariate

One Variable Isn't Enough

Throughout Modules 3.1 to 3.3, every distribution described a single random variable. The Bernoulli described one coin flip. The Normal described one height. The Poisson described one arrival count.

But the real world isn't one variable at a time. It's tangled. Real questions usually involve relationships between variables:

  • "How does height relate to weight?"
  • "How does study hours affect exam grade?"
  • "How does temperature change ice cream sales?"
  • "How does age connect with blood pressure?"
  • "How does income match with savings?"

To answer these, we need a way to describe two or more random variables together. That's exactly what this module gives us.

Big idea: When two random variables live in the same world, we can't just describe each separately and call it a day. We have to capture how they move together — and that requires a new kind of distribution: a joint distribution.
A simple analogy

Think of It Like a 2D Map

Imagine you're describing where people live in a city.

  • If you only know "how far north" each person lives — that's one variable.
  • If you only know "how far east" each person lives — that's another one variable.
  • But to put a dot on a 2D map, you need both coordinates together.

A joint distribution is like the 2D map. It tells you the probability of every (X, Y) pair — every combination of "how far north" and "how far east" together.

And a marginal distribution is what you get when you forget one dimension. If you only care about north-south positions and forget east-west, you've taken the marginal distribution of Y — just one piece of the 2D map's information.

The whole picture vs the slices

The Big Picture for This Module

By the end of Module 3.4, you'll have a powerful toolkit for working with multiple random variables. Here's the map of where we're going:

Joint Distributions

The full picture

What is a Joint Distribution?

A joint distribution describes the probability of two (or more) random variables taking specific values at the same time. It's the complete probability map for the pair (X, Y).

For two random variables X and Y, the joint distribution answers questions like:

  • "What's the probability that X = 3 AND Y = 5?"
  • "What's the probability that X is between 0 and 1 AND Y is between 2 and 3?"
  • "What pairs (X, Y) are most likely? Least likely?"
Simple way to think about it: A single distribution gives you a 1D picture (a line of probabilities). A joint distribution gives you a 2D picture (a grid or surface of probabilities). Three variables? A 3D cloud. And so on. Each new variable adds a new dimension.
Discrete case

Joint PMF — For Pairs of Discrete Variables

When both X and Y are discrete (countable values like 0, 1, 2, 3 or "Red, Blue, Green"), the joint distribution is described by a joint probability mass function (joint PMF).

⟁ Joint PMF — Discrete Case
p(x, y) = P(X = x AND Y = y)

In words: p(x, y) gives the probability that X equals x at the same time as Y equals y. Each cell in a joint table holds one of these values.

Two rules every joint PMF must satisfy:

p(x, y) ≥ 0 — every cell is non-negative
Σₓ Σᵧ p(x, y) = 1 — all cells sum to 1

Just like a regular PMF must be non-negative and sum to 1, a joint PMF has the same rules — it just sums across both dimensions instead of one.

A concrete example

Example: Study Hours & Exam Grades

Suppose we survey 100 students and record two variables:

  • X = study hours per week (categorized: Low, Medium, High)
  • Y = grade letter (A, B, C)

The joint PMF tells us the probability of every combination. Here's the joint probability table:

X \\ Y Grade Y
A B C
High study 0.25 0.10 0.02
Medium study 0.10 0.20 0.08
Low study 0.02 0.08 0.15
Joint PMF — p(x, y) for study hours and grades. Total = 1.00

How to read this:

  • p(High, A) = 0.25 → 25% of students study a lot AND get an A
  • p(Low, C) = 0.15 → 15% of students study little AND get a C
  • p(High, C) = 0.02 → only 2% study a lot AND still get a C (rare!)
  • All 9 cells add up to 1.00 — every student falls into exactly one combination
What this captures: The joint distribution shows the relationship between study hours and grades. Notice how high-study + A is the biggest cell, and low-study + C is also big. The diagonal pattern hints that more study leads to better grades — which is exactly the kind of insight a joint distribution reveals.
Continuous case

Joint PDF — For Pairs of Continuous Variables

When X and Y are continuous (any real value — like height, weight, time), we can't use a table because there are infinitely many possible values. Instead we use a joint probability density function (joint PDF), written f(x, y).

Just like with single continuous variables, f(x, y) is a density — probability per unit area. To get an actual probability, you integrate over a region:

⟁ Joint PDF — Continuous Case
P(a ≤ X ≤ b, c ≤ Y ≤ d) = ∫ₐᵇ ∫꜀ᵈ f(x, y) dy dx

In words: the probability of (X, Y) falling in a rectangle is the double integral of the joint density over that rectangle. We integrate twice — once over each variable.

Two rules every joint PDF must satisfy:

f(x, y) ≥ 0 — density is non-negative
∫∫ f(x, y) dx dy = 1 — total volume under the surface is 1

For continuous variables, the joint distribution can be visualized as a 3D surface floating above the (X, Y) plane. The total volume under the surface always equals 1.

Why joint distributions matter

What the Joint Reveals That Individual Distributions Hide

Here's why we can't just describe X and Y separately. Imagine someone tells you:

  • "30% of people have high study hours."
  • "40% of people get an A."

You might guess that 30% × 40% = 12% of people have BOTH. But that's only true if X and Y are independent (we'll cover this in Part 2). In reality, the joint probability could be much higher (25%) if study hours and grades are related.

The joint distribution captures this relationship — single distributions cannot. The whole is more than the sum of the parts.

Marginal Distributions

Extracting one variable from a joint

What is a Marginal Distribution?

A marginal distribution is the probability distribution of just one variable, extracted from a joint distribution by "forgetting" the other variable.

Suppose you have the joint distribution of X and Y. The marginal of X tells you the probability distribution of X all by itself — as if Y didn't exist. Similarly, the marginal of Y tells you Y's distribution alone.

The name "marginal": When statisticians wrote out joint tables on paper, they would sum up the probabilities row by row and column by column, and write the sums in the margins of the table — literally in the side margins. So those sums became called "marginal" distributions. The name stuck.
⟁ Marginal — Discrete Case
pX(x) = Σᵧ p(x, y)    (sum over all y)
pY(y) = Σₓ p(x, y)    (sum over all x)

In words: To get the marginal probability of X = x, add up the joint probabilities for that value of x across all possible values of y. You're summing across one whole row (or column) of the joint table.

⟁ Marginal — Continuous Case
fX(x) = ∫ f(x, y) dy
fY(y) = ∫ f(x, y) dx

In words: For continuous variables, replace summation with integration. To find the marginal density of X, integrate the joint density over all values of y. The "summing" idea is the same — just continuous.

Going back to study hours and grades

Computing Marginals from a Joint Table

Let's go back to the study-hours-vs-grades table and find the marginals. We add up rows for the marginal of X (study hours), and columns for the marginal of Y (grades).

X \\ Y Grade Y pX(x)
Row Sum
A B C
High study 0.25 0.10 0.02 0.37
Medium study 0.10 0.20 0.08 0.38
Low study 0.02 0.08 0.15 0.25
pY(y)
Col Sum		 0.37 0.38 0.25 1.00
Joint table with marginals written in the margins (green cells)

How we got these marginals:

  • pX(High) = 0.25 + 0.10 + 0.02 = 0.37 — sum across the High-study row, ignoring grade
  • pX(Medium) = 0.10 + 0.20 + 0.08 = 0.38
  • pX(Low) = 0.02 + 0.08 + 0.15 = 0.25
  • pY(A) = 0.25 + 0.10 + 0.02 = 0.37 — sum down the A column, ignoring study hours
  • pY(B) = 0.10 + 0.20 + 0.08 = 0.38
  • pY(C) = 0.02 + 0.08 + 0.15 = 0.25
Sanity check: Both marginals must each sum to 1 (since they're valid probability distributions). Row sums: 0.37 + 0.38 + 0.25 = 1.00 ✓. Column sums: 0.37 + 0.38 + 0.25 = 1.00 ✓. The marginals are valid PMFs on their own.

Marginals are valid distributions

Properties of Marginal Distributions

  • Always sum to 1 — they are themselves valid PMFs (discrete) or PDFs (continuous).
  • Always non-negative — probabilities can never go below zero.
  • Lose relationship info — once you go from joint to marginal, you can no longer tell how X and Y relate. The relationship has been "summed out."
  • One-way street — you can compute marginals from a joint, but you cannot generally rebuild the joint from just the marginals (unless X and Y are independent — coming in Part 2!).
Critical insight: Joint distributions contain more information than marginals. Two completely different joint distributions can have the exact same marginals! This is why we need joint distributions in the first place — marginals alone don't tell the full story.

Worked Practice — Try It Yourself

Example 1 — Coin and die

Joint Distribution of a Coin Flip and Die Roll

Flip a fair coin (X = 0 for tails, 1 for heads) and roll a fair 4-sided die (Y = 1, 2, 3, or 4). Since the flip and the roll are independent, the joint PMF is simple:

p(x, y) = P(X = x) × P(Y = y) = (1/2) × (1/4) = 1/8 for every cell

X \\ Y Die Y pX(x)
1234
X = 0 (Tail) 1/81/81/81/8 1/2
X = 1 (Head) 1/81/81/81/8 1/2
pY(y) 1/4 1/4 1/4 1/4 1.00
Joint PMF — coin × die, with marginals

What this tells us:

  • All 8 cells are equally likely — each has probability 1/8.
  • Marginal of X is a fair coin: P(X = 0) = P(X = 1) = 1/2.
  • Marginal of Y is a fair 4-sided die: each face has probability 1/4.
  • This is the simplest type of joint — we'll see in Part 2 why it's called independent.
Example 2 — Weather and umbrellas

A More Realistic Joint

Consider weather (X) and whether people carry umbrellas (Y):

  • X = R (Rainy) or S (Sunny)
  • Y = U (carries umbrella) or N (no umbrella)
X \\ Y Umbrella Y pX(x)
U (yes)N (no)
R (rainy) 0.250.05 0.30
S (sunny) 0.050.65 0.70
pY(y) 0.30 0.70 1.00
Joint PMF — weather × umbrella

Observations:

  • 30% of days are rainy (marginal: pX(R) = 0.30)
  • 30% of days people carry umbrellas (marginal: pY(U) = 0.30)
  • Most rainy days have umbrellas (0.25 out of 0.30 → 83%)
  • Most sunny days have no umbrella (0.65 out of 0.70 → 93%)
  • The joint clearly shows a strong relationship: rain and umbrellas go together!
Notice: If you only saw the marginals (30% rainy, 30% umbrellas), you'd never know how strongly these are related. The joint distribution reveals the connection. This is exactly the kind of structure that conditional distributions (Part 2) will let us explore in detail.
