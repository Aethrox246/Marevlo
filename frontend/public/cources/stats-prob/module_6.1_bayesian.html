Bayesian Statistics & Inference

Probability is not just about counting outcomes — it is also a way to express belief. Update those beliefs cleanly when new data arrives, and you get Bayesian inference.

This module covers the four core pieces of Bayesian thinking: the prior distribution (what you believed before), the posterior distribution (what you believe after data), the Bayesian updating rule that turns one into the other, and the intuition for decision making that lets you act on uncertain beliefs in the real world. Everything is built on one equation — Bayes' rule — applied step by step.

Topic 1

Prior Distributions

What it is

Belief Before Evidence

A prior distribution is a probability distribution over an unknown quantity, written down before seeing any data. It captures what you believe about the unknown, and how sure you are about that belief.

The unknown is usually called a parameter — for example, the true bias of a coin, the average rating of a new film, the fraction of defective items in a factory. You don't know its exact value, but you can describe your uncertainty as a distribution.

One-line definition: A prior is your belief about an unknown parameter, written as a probability distribution, before any data has been observed.
Why we need it

Why Priors Exist

Three reasons priors are useful — and in Bayesian thinking, unavoidable:

1. We Are Rarely Starting From Zero

  • Most real problems come with background knowledge
  • Past studies, physical limits, expert judgement
  • Ignoring this knowledge wastes information
  • A prior is the formal way to include it

2. Small Data Needs Help

  • With few observations, raw data is noisy
  • A reasonable prior keeps estimates sensible
  • It prevents extreme conclusions from tiny samples
  • Example: 2 heads in 2 flips ≠ "this coin is 100% heads"

3. Uncertainty Must Be Expressed

  • A single best guess hides uncertainty
  • A distribution shows the full range of belief
  • Wide prior = unsure; narrow prior = confident
  • This shape gets passed into the posterior
How to use it

Picking a Prior in Practice

There is no single "right" prior, but a few practical guidelines apply:

  1. Identify what is unknown. Is it a probability between 0 and 1? A rate? A mean? Each unknown has natural prior families.
  2. Choose between informative and uninformative. If you genuinely have prior knowledge, encode it. If not, use a flat or weakly informative prior.
  3. Match the prior's shape to your knowledge. Skewed? Bounded? Centred? Pick a distribution family that respects these.
  4. Calibrate the strength. A narrow prior says "I'm very sure." A wide prior says "I'm not sure." Be honest.
  5. Sanity check. Sample from your prior and see if the samples look reasonable. If they don't, adjust.

Common Prior Families

  • Beta(α, β) — for probabilities between 0 and 1. Beta(1,1) is uniform (no prior info); Beta(10,10) leans toward 0.5; Beta(2,8) leans toward small values.
  • Normal(μ, σ²) — for unknown means or unbounded quantities. Wide σ² = weak prior; narrow σ² = strong prior.
  • Gamma(α, β) — for unknown rates and positive quantities (always > 0).
  • Uniform — equal belief over a fixed range. Use when you genuinely know nothing inside that range.
Formal Notation
P(θ) — prior distribution over parameter θ
Read as "the probability of θ" — but here θ is itself unknown, so P(θ) is a full distribution, not a single number. Different shapes of P(θ) encode different prior beliefs.
θ — the unknown parameter (probability, rate, mean, etc.)
P(θ) — the prior density at value θ
Higher P(θ) — that value of θ is more believable before data
Width of P(θ) — how uncertain we are
⟁ Real-Life Example

A New Coffee Machine

A café owner buys a new coffee machine. The supplier claims it produces a good cup about 80% of the time. The owner is not certain — supplier claims are sometimes optimistic — but they also don't think the machine is broken.

A reasonable prior: Beta(8, 2) — this distribution is centred near 0.8 but allows for values anywhere between 0.5 and 1. It captures "I roughly believe the supplier, but I'm not married to that number." Before brewing a single cup, this is what the owner believes about the machine's success rate.

Important: the prior is honest about both what we believe and how much we believe it. Both pieces matter — and both will affect the posterior in Topic 2.
Where it is used

Priors in the Real World

Clinical Trials

Before testing a new drug, statisticians use priors based on similar past drugs. This lets new trials need fewer patients to reach reliable conclusions.

Spam Filters

Each word has a prior probability of appearing in spam vs. ham emails. These priors get updated every time you mark an email as spam.

Search Ranking

A new product on a marketplace doesn't have many reviews yet. A prior based on similar products keeps its ranking sensible until enough reviews arrive.

Sports Analytics

A new player's first few games shouldn't define their full skill. A prior built from past players prevents overreacting to a hot or cold start.

Topic 2

Posterior Distributions

What it is

Belief After Evidence

A posterior distribution is what you believe about the unknown after seeing data. It combines two things — your prior belief and how well the data fits each possible value of the unknown — and produces a new, updated distribution.

If the prior was wide ("I'm not sure") and the data is strong, the posterior becomes narrower ("now I have a clearer answer"). If the data is weak or limited, the posterior stays close to the prior.

One-line definition: The posterior is the conditional probability distribution of the unknown parameter given the data — written P(θ | data).
Why it matters

What the Posterior Gives You

A posterior is more powerful than a single estimate, because it carries full information about uncertainty. From a posterior you can read off:

Best Estimate

  • The peak (mode) gives the most likely value
  • The mean gives the average belief
  • The median is the 50/50 split point

Uncertainty Range

  • Width of the posterior = how unsure we are
  • Credible interval: 95% of the belief lies inside
  • Narrow = confident, wide = unsure

Direct Probabilities

  • "What is P(θ > 0.5)?" — read it off the posterior
  • "Is the new ad better than the old one?" — same idea
  • No need to translate into p-values
Posterior Definition
P(θ | data) — posterior density of θ given the observed data
The | symbol means "given." This distribution sits in the same space as the prior — it's still a distribution over θ — but its shape has been reshaped by the evidence.
P(θ | data) — posterior density
Peak of posterior — best single estimate of θ
Width — remaining uncertainty
Shift from prior — how much the data moved your belief

⟁ Real-Life Example

A/B Testing a Website Button

A team tests two button designs. Before the experiment, their prior is "both probably convert about the same — somewhere around 5%." After running the test on 500 visitors per variant, they observe that variant B has 32 conversions and variant A has 22.

The posterior over each variant's true conversion rate is no longer a wide prior — it has tightened around the observed rates, with some residual uncertainty. The team can now read off directly: P(B is better than A | data) ≈ 0.91. That probability lets them make a real decision.

Topic 3

Bayesian Updating

What it is

Turning Prior Into Posterior

Bayesian updating is the formal rule that takes a prior, combines it with observed data, and produces the posterior. The rule is called Bayes' rule, and it is the single most important equation in this entire module.

The intuition: at every possible value of θ, the prior says how believable that value was beforehand, and the likelihood says how well the data fits if θ were true. Multiplying them gives the updated belief. Bigger prior × bigger likelihood = much higher posterior at that value.

Bayes' Rule — The Update Equation
P(θ | data) = P(data | θ) × P(θ) / P(data)
The posterior equals the likelihood times the prior, divided by the marginal probability of the data. In plain language: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior.
P(θ | data) — posterior: updated belief about θ
P(data | θ) — likelihood: how well θ explains the data
P(θ) — prior: belief about θ before data
P(data) — normalising constant (makes the posterior sum to 1)
How to use it

Four-Step Update Procedure

  1. State the prior P(θ). Write down your belief before data — e.g. Beta(2, 2) for a coin's fairness.
  2. Write the likelihood P(data | θ). For each possible θ, say how probable the observed data would be. For coin flips, this is θheads × (1−θ)tails.
  3. Multiply: prior × likelihood. Point by point, multiply the two. This is the unnormalised posterior — the right shape, but not yet a valid probability distribution.
  4. Normalise. Scale the result so the total area is 1. This gives the final posterior.
Why this is beautiful: the same four steps work for any problem. The only thing that changes is the prior, the likelihood, and the integration. Bayes' rule is a single tool that scales from coin flips to galaxy-scale astrophysics.
Beta-Binomial Conjugate Update
Posterior = Beta( α + heads, β + tails )
When the prior is Beta(α, β) and the data is heads-and-tails from coin flips, the posterior is also a Beta — with parameters simply updated by adding observed counts. This is called a conjugate update and is the cleanest example of Bayesian updating.
α — "imagined heads" carried by the prior
β — "imagined tails" carried by the prior
heads — observed heads in the data
tails — observed tails in the data
⟁ Worked Example

Updating Belief About a Coin

Start with prior Beta(2, 2) — mild belief that the coin is fair. Flip 8 times and observe 6 heads, 2 tails.

  • ▸ Posterior: Beta(2 + 6, 2 + 2) = Beta(8, 4)
  • ▸ Posterior mean: 8 / (8 + 4) = 0.667
  • ▸ Compare: raw data says 6/8 = 0.75; the prior pulled the estimate down toward 0.5

Now flip 100 more times and observe 65 heads. New posterior: Beta(8 + 65, 4 + 35) = Beta(73, 39), mean ≈ 0.652. With more data, the prior matters less and the posterior moves closer to what the data suggests.

The key pattern: with little data, the prior has a strong voice. With lots of data, the data dominates. This balance is automatic — Bayes' rule handles it for you.

Topic 4

Bayesian Intuition & Decision Making

What it is

From Belief to Action

A posterior tells you what to believe. But knowing what to believe is not the same as knowing what to do. Bayesian decision making is the bridge — it takes the posterior and combines it with the costs and benefits of different actions to choose the best one.

In short: the posterior gives the distribution of possible truths, and a loss function tells you how bad each wrong decision would be. The optimal action minimises the average loss across all possible truths, weighted by their posterior probability.

One-line definition: Bayesian decision making picks the action that minimises the expected loss under the posterior distribution.
Expected Loss of an Action
E[ L(action) ] = ∫ L(action, θ) · P(θ | data) dθ
For each possible action, compute the average loss across all values of θ, weighted by how much you believe each value. Pick the action with the smallest expected loss.
L(action, θ) — loss if you take this action and θ is the truth
P(θ | data) — posterior weight for that value of θ
E[ L ] — expected loss for that action
Pick — the action with the lowest expected loss
How to use it

The Four-Step Decision Procedure

  1. List the actions. What are the choices available? Launch the product, wait, run more tests, refund, accept, reject.
  2. Define a loss for each (action, θ) pair. If θ turns out to be such-and-such and you took this action, how bad is the outcome?
  3. Compute expected loss for each action. Use the posterior to weight each θ. Sum or integrate.
  4. Pick the action with the smallest expected loss. This is the Bayesian-optimal decision under your current belief.
Common trap: people forget that the loss function matters. Two teams with the same posterior can make different decisions if one cares more about false positives than the other. The posterior is shared; the loss is yours.
⟁ Real-Life Example

Should You Launch the New Feature?

A team has a posterior over the new feature's true conversion-rate uplift. The posterior says: 70% chance of positive uplift, 30% chance of slightly negative uplift.

Two losses are in play:

  • Launch when feature is bad → loss of 100 (engineering hours wasted + user trust dented)
  • Don't launch when feature is good → loss of 20 (small opportunity cost)

Expected loss of launching = 0.70 × 0 + 0.30 × 100 = 30.
Expected loss of not launching = 0.70 × 20 + 0.30 × 0 = 14.

Even though the feature is more likely good than bad, don't launch is the right call — the cost of being wrong on the launch side is much bigger. This is the kind of asymmetric thinking Bayesian decisions force you into.

Where it is used

Decisions Made With Posteriors

Medical Diagnosis

The posterior probability of disease after a test, combined with the cost of treatment vs. missing the disease, determines whether to treat.

Self-Driving Cars

A car's posterior over what an object ahead is — pedestrian, cyclist, plastic bag — combined with safety losses, determines the action: brake, swerve, continue.

Finance

Posterior over future returns + investor's loss aversion determines portfolio weights. Different loss functions produce different strategies.

AI Agents

An AI agent uses posteriors over user intent and possible outcomes to decide when to ask a clarifying question vs. when to just act.
